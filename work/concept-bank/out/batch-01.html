★ Expected Credit Loss provisioning
In plain words: Banks estimate likely credit losses early and set aside provisions in advance, rather than waiting until a borrower defaults.

Why it exists

  • Under the earlier incurred loss model, banks provided for loan losses only after default occurred (too little, too late).
  • Forward-looking provisioning builds capital buffers ahead of downturns and reflects deteriorating credit quality earlier.

How it works

  • Loan originated → placed in Stage 1 (standard asset) → bank provisions twelve-month expected loss.
  • Credit risk increases significantly (overdue thirty to ninety days) → moves to Stage 2 → bank provisions lifetime expected loss.
  • Default occurs (overdue beyond ninety days) → moves to Stage 3 (NPA) → bank provisions full lifetime loss.
  • Loss formula: ECL = PD × LGD × EAD.

Fixed facts

  • Scope: applies to commercial banks, excluding regional rural banks, small finance banks and payments banks.
  • Provisioning floors: Stage 1 requires minimum 0.4% provision; Stage 2 requires minimum 5% provision; Stage 3 NPAs require full lifetime loss provisioning.

How it changed

  • April 2026: Final directions effective 1 April 2027; incremental transition provisions can be spread over four years to 31 March 2031 with CET1 add-back.
  • April 2026: All loans at 31 March 2027 move under the EIR regime by 31 March 2030.

Traps

  • Incurred loss provided only after default; ECL provisions before default.
  • Stage 1 requires twelve-month loss; Stage 2 and Stage 3 require lifetime loss.

In the news

  • October 2026: Statement on Developmental and Regulatory Policies (7 Oct 2026)
  • April 2026: RBI: ECL norms (final)
★ Ways and Means Advances
In plain words: Temporary credit facility provided by the Reserve Bank of India to the Central and State Governments to bridge short-term cash flow mismatches between receipts and payments.

Why it exists

  • Government revenues and expenditures do not match on daily cash flows; WMA provides short-term cash flow support without permanent deficit financing.
  • Prevents abrupt fiscal disruptions and maintains smooth payment disbursements for public administration.

How it works

  • Government encounters temporary cash shortfall → requests fund drawal under WMA within agreed limit.
  • Drawings within normal limit → charged at prevailing repo rate.
  • Borrowing must be repaid within three months from the advance date.

Fixed facts

  • Statutory authority: Section 17(5) of the Reserve Bank of India Act 1934 empowers RBI to make advances to Central and State Governments repayable not later than three months.
  • Purpose: WMA provides temporary assistance to the Centre for receipt–payment mismatches.

Traps

  • WMA is strictly a short-term cash-flow bridge loan, not long-term market borrowing or bond financing.
  • WMA is distinct from Treasury Bills, which are tradeable debt instruments sold in the money market.

★ Floating Rate Bonds
In plain words: Government debt securities whose coupon rate is not fixed at issuance, but is reset periodically based on an underlying market benchmark plus a fixed spread.

Why it exists

  • Protects investors against interest rate volatility in changing monetary cycles.
  • Allows the government to issue long-term debt without locking in high coupon yields during high-inflation periods.

How it works

  • Benchmark rate calculated from the average cut-off yield of preceding 182-day T-Bill auctions.
  • Fixed spread determined at initial primary auction is added to the benchmark yield.
  • Coupon rate is reset every six months; interest is paid half-yearly.
  • Principal is redeemed at par on the maturity date.

Fixed facts

  • Coupon rate is linked to the 182-day T-Bill yield calculated as the average weighted average yield of the last three auctions plus a fixed spread.
  • Coupon reset and payment frequency: reset every six months with half-yearly interest payments.

Traps

  • The coupon interest rate fluctuates across reset periods, but the principal face value redeemed at maturity remains fixed.
  • Institutional floating rate bonds are tradeable in secondary markets, unlike retail savings bonds which cannot be transferred.

★ Forex reserve management and swap facilities
In plain words: Framework through which the Reserve Bank of India manages foreign exchange reserves, regulates external currency transactions, and intervenes via swap auctions to maintain market stability.

Why it exists

  • Ensures adequate import cover, external debt debt-servicing capability, and sovereign creditworthiness.
  • Smooths exchange rate volatility and calms speculative disorder in domestic foreign exchange markets.

How it works

  • Surge in dollar inflows → RBI buys dollars and adds to reserves → injects domestic rupee liquidity.
  • Severe depreciation pressure on rupee → RBI sells dollars in spot/forward markets or runs sell/buy swap auctions.
  • Swap auctions: RBI exchanges dollars with authorised dealers for rupees, agreeing to reverse at a specified forward date and rate.

Fixed facts

  • Foreign exchange reserves comprise four distinct assets: Foreign Currency Assets, Gold holdings, SDRs at the IMF, and the Reserve Tranche Position in the IMF.
  • Reserves definition: total reserves equal foreign currency assets plus gold plus SDRs plus reserve position in IMF.

How it changed

  • October 2026: Derivative cancellation restriction: no rebooking of a rupee FX derivative once cancelled, while rollovers at maturity remain permitted.
  • October 2026: Hedging without proving underlying exposure cut from $100 million to $5 million across all authorised dealer banks.
  • October 2026: Foreign Exchange Risk Reserve introduced requiring authorised dealers to maintain 20% cash reserve with RBI in their CRR account for contracts above $2 million.
  • October 2026: Special dollar window opened for state-run oil marketing companies (IOC, HPCL, BPCL) via designated banks.
  • May 2026: RBI conducted a $5 billion three-year USD/INR buy/sell swap auction to inject long-term rupee liquidity into the banking system.

Traps

  • India maintains a managed float exchange rate regime, not a fixed peg; interventions contain volatility rather than targeting a fixed rupee level.
  • Current account transactions are fully convertible under Article VIII of the IMF; capital account transactions remain partially regulated.

★ Foreign Exchange Management Act framework
In plain words: Primary statutory legislation governing cross-border trade, investments, external borrowings, and foreign exchange transactions in India.

Why it exists

  • Replaced the criminal provisions of FERA 1973 with a civil, facilitative regime to encourage external payments and cross-border commerce.
  • Establishes legal channels for authorized foreign exchange transactions while preventing unauthorized capital flight.

How it works

  • Transactions categorized into current account and capital account.
  • Current account transactions are generally permitted unless specifically prohibited by rules.
  • Capital account transactions are prohibited unless specifically permitted by RBI regulations.
  • Transactions must be executed through RBI-licensed authorised persons (dealers and money changers).

Fixed facts

  • Statutory foundation: Foreign Exchange Management Act 1999 (Act 42 of 1999) regulates dealing in foreign exchange through authorised persons.
  • Current account transactions are permitted unless prohibited, while capital account transactions are regulated under Section 6.

How it changed

  • October 2026: Export of services brought under the EDF via authorised dealers under Export and Import of Goods and Services Regulations 2026.
  • May 2026: FEMA Authorised Persons Regulations 2026 notified; fresh authorizations restricted to three categories with no fresh full-fledged money changer licences.
  • April 2026: FEMA Guarantees Regulations 2026 established that no resident may enter a guarantee with a non-resident unless permitted.
  • January–March 2026: Borrowing and Lending First Amendment Regulations 2026 eased ECB framework parameters.

Traps

  • FEMA 1999 is a civil statute with monetary penalties; FERA 1973 was a criminal statute where contraventions led to arrest.
  • Current account transactions are permitted unless prohibited; capital account transactions are prohibited unless permitted.

★ Basel III capital and risk framework
In plain words: An internationally agreed regulatory framework that mandates capital quality, leverage limits, and liquidity reserves for commercial banks to absorb financial stress.

Why it exists

  • Formulated by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision to remedy excessive leverage, insufficient capital, and illiquidity exposed during the 2008 financial crisis.
  • Prevents systemic banking distress and protects depositor funds by requiring banks to maintain high-quality loss-absorbing capital.

How it works

  • Pillar 1: Minimum capital requirements calculated across credit, market, and operational risk.
  • Pillar 2: Supervisory review process where RBI assesses internal risk models and capital adequacy.
  • Pillar 3: Market discipline requiring public disclosures of capital structure and risk profile.

Fixed facts

  • Minimum leverage ratio standard: 3.5% for commercial banks and 4% for D-SIBs.
  • Countercyclical capital buffer framework: introduced in 2015, reviewed quarterly by RBI, and maintained at 0% when inactive.

How it changed

  • October 2026: CVA Framework Directions 2026 notified, taking effect from 1 April 2027 for OTC derivatives.
  • October 2026: Replaced operational risk methods with a single standardized approach and finalized leverage ratio directions effective 1 April 2027.
  • May 2026: Deregulated quarterly profit inclusion: banks may include interim quarterly profits directly in CET1 capital without prior audit, provided accrued dividends are deducted.
  • April 2026: Basel III credit-risk standardized approach finalized for implementation from 1 April 2027, setting 150% risk weight for unrated exposures above ₹500 crore.

Traps

  • India mandates a 9% minimum CRAR, which is stricter than the 8% international baseline.
  • Capital Conservation Buffer is permanently maintained at 2.5%, whereas CCyB is dynamic (0% to 2.5%) and activated only during excessive credit expansion.

In the news

  • October 2026: RBI: new operational-risk and leverage-ratio norms from 1 April 2027
  • May 2026: Quarterly profits in CET1 capital: condition removed
  • May 2026: Countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB) not activated
  • May 2026: RBI draft: Pillar 3 disclosures by banks
  • April 2026: RBI: Basel III credit-risk (standardised approach) final directions
  • April 2026: RBI capital, card and classification directions
★ Unified Payments Interface infrastructure
In plain words: A real-time payment architecture operated by NPCI that enables instant inter-bank peer-to-peer and peer-to-merchant fund transfers through mobile devices.

Why it exists

  • Eliminates friction in retail electronic payments by replacing complex IFSC and account details with simple virtual identifiers and QR codes.
  • Provides a safe, accessible, round-the-clock digital payments infrastructure across the banking system.

How it works

  • User binds mobile device to bank account → sets up VPA and secret UPI PIN.
  • Payer initiates transfer by scanning QR code or entering recipient VPA → inputs transaction amount → authorizes with UPI PIN.
  • NPCI switch routes payment message in real time across IMPS infrastructure → debits payer account and credits payee account instantly.

Fixed facts

  • Statutory prohibition on charges: Section 10A of the Payment and Settlement Systems Act 2007 bars banks and system providers from levying fees on UPI and RuPay debit transactions.
  • Operating history: launched in April 2016 under Payments Vision for interoperability across bank accounts.

How it changed

  • September 2026: Government notified zero charges on UPI and RuPay debit transactions up to ₹2,000 under Section 10A of the Payment and Settlement Systems Act 2007.
  • October 2026: Finance Ministry formally deferred the proposal to introduce tiered merchant discount rates on UPI payments.
  • September 2026: NPCI announced UPI Tap and Pay using NFC technology and MyUPI delegated payments at Global Fintech Fest 2026.

Traps

  • UPI is a real-time routing switch and settlement network, not a stored-value wallet; funds always reside in and move between commercial bank accounts.
  • Zero MDR applies to bank-to-bank UPI transfers; interchange fees apply exclusively to prepaid wallet merchant payments above ₹2,000.

Why it exists

★ Trade Receivables Discounting System
In plain words: An electronic auction platform regulated by the RBI that facilitates the financing and discounting of trade receivables of MSMEs from corporate and government buyers through multiple financiers.

Why it exists

  • Resolves working capital blockages and delayed payments faced by small suppliers.
  • Allows MSMEs to convert unpaid trade invoices into immediate cash at competitive bidding rates without pledging collateral.

How it works

  • MSME seller uploads unpaid invoice onto the TReDS platform.
  • Corporate or government buyer verifies and accepts the invoice online, creating a Factoring Unit.
  • Financiers bid competitive discount rates on the accepted factoring unit.
  • MSME seller accepts the best bid and receives immediate funds; financier collects payment from the buyer on the due date.

Fixed facts

  • TReDS is an electronic platform regulated under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act 2007 to finance trade receivables of Micro and Small Enterprises.
  • Factoring on TReDS is executed without recourse to the MSME supplier.

How it changed

  • September 2026: CGTMSE operationalized credit guarantee coverage for TReDS receivables through the NCGTC and CGTMSE portal.

Traps

  • Factoring on TReDS is conducted without recourse to the MSME seller; if the buyer defaults on maturity, the financier cannot recover the loss from the MSME.
  • Individual consumers cannot participate; TReDS is strictly for MSME suppliers, eligible corporate/government buyers, and licensed financiers.

★ Self-Regulatory Organisations in financial markets
In plain words: Non-profit industry associations recognized by financial regulators to establish voluntary conduct standards, enforce ethical norms, and resolve member disputes within specific financial sectors.

Why it exists

  • Fast-moving sectors like fintech and foreign exchange outpace formal statutory rulemaking.
  • Leverages practitioner expertise to develop market standards and serves as a consultative bridge between market participants and statutory regulators.

How it works

  • Industry participants establish a non-profit company with representative membership.
  • Entity applies to RBI or SEBI under published omnibus frameworks demonstrating independence and governance.
  • Recognized SRO drafts codes of conduct, monitors compliance, provides training, and submits surveillance reports to the regulator.

Fixed facts

  • SRO definition: a non-government body authorized by a regulator to set and enforce conduct rules for its members.

Traps

  • An SRO is not a statutory regulator; it operates under delegated authority and cannot issue rules that contradict RBI or SEBI circulars.

★ Account Aggregator financial data ecosystem
In plain words: An RBI-regulated entity that facilitates secure, encrypted, and consent-based sharing of financial information between financial institutions.

Why it exists

  • Eliminates financial data silos where individuals cannot easily share bank records or investment holdings to access credit.
  • Replaces unsecure practices like physical statement submissions and screen scraping with tamper-proof electronic consent.

How it works

  • Customer applies for financial service at a Financial Information User (FIU: e.g. lender).
  • FIU initiates structured data request via customer Account Aggregator application.
  • Customer reviews and authorizes electronic consent artifact specifying data fields and purpose.
  • AA retrieves encrypted data from Financial Information Provider (FIP: e.g. bank) and transmits directly to FIU without storing or viewing it.

Fixed facts

  • Definition and mandate: AA is an RBI-licensed entity moving financial data from FIP to FIU with customer consent; data-blind architecture (cannot view or store data).
  • Interoperability principle: customers can access and share data from any FIP through any NBFC-AA of their choice.

How it changed

  • October 2026: RBI mandated full technical interoperability across all licensed Account Aggregator networks by 31 December 2026 to reduce customer drop-offs and operational load.
  • October 2026: Statement on Developmental and Regulatory Policies announced bank deposits will appear in CAS via NBFC-AAs.

Traps

  • An Account Aggregator does not manage funds, execute financial transactions, or hold customer balances; its sole function is consent-based data transmission.
  • The Account Aggregator is data-blind; it cannot decrypt, view, or monetize the financial payload passing through its network.

In plain words: Payment instruments such as digital wallets and smart cards that facilitate purchases, financial services, and remittances against the stored monetary value pre-funded on them.

Why it exists

  • Provides accessible electronic payment channels for individuals who lack active bank accounts, debit cards, or credit cards.

★ Capital Market Exposure and securities lending limits
In plain words: Prudential regulatory ceilings set by the Reserve Bank of India on bank lending and investments in equity shares, stockbrokers, mutual funds, and initial public offerings.

Why it exists

  • Prevents volatile stock market crashes from spilling into and destabilizing the commercial banking sector.
  • Limits excessive speculative financing and maintains prudent distribution of bank credit.

How it works

  • Bank calculates its eligible capital base (Tier 1 + Tier 2 capital) → applies prudential CME limits on solo and consolidated bases.
  • Direct equity investments, advances to stockbrokers, loans against shares, and financial guarantees are monitored against individual and aggregate limits.
  • Lending against shares requires mandatory regulatory margins (haircuts) to guard against market volatility.

Fixed facts

  • Bank ceilings: aggregate CME cannot exceed 40% of eligible capital base (Tier 1 + Tier 2); direct investment CME capped at 20%; acquisition finance capped at 20%.

How it changed

  • January–March 2026: Concentration Risk Management Amendment Directions 2026 notified 40% aggregate CME ceiling and IPC capital treatment at 125% risk weight for commercial banks and SFBs.
  • January–March 2026: Credit Facilities Amendment Directions 2026 set individual share lending LTV at 60% and capped IPO/FPO/ESOP financing at ₹25 lakh effective 1 April 2026.

Traps

  • The ₹25 lakh ceiling on IPO financing applies across the entire banking system per borrower, not per bank branch.
  • CME rules cover both fund-based lending (loans against shares) and non-fund-based guarantees (custodian IPCs to clearing corporations).

★ Wilful defaulters classification and loan resolution
In plain words: Regulatory framework governing the identification, penal restrictions, and legal resolution of borrowers who intentionally default on loans despite having the capacity to pay, or who siphon funds.

Why it exists

  • Separates genuine business distress from intentional non-payment, diversion of funds, or asset disposal.
  • Enforces severe penal and market access restrictions on wilful defaulters to maintain credit discipline across financial institutions.

How it works

  • Lender discovers intentional default, fund diversion, or unauthorized asset disposal → Identification Committee issues show-cause notice.
  • Borrower submits representation and is provided reasonable opportunity for personal hearing.
  • Review Committee reviews findings and confirms classification → borrower declared wilful defaulter.
  • Lender reports name to CICs → borrower debarred from fresh bank loans, capital markets, and floating new corporate entities for five years post-resolution.

Fixed facts

  • Classification timeline: bank must complete wilful defaulter classification within six months of the account turning into an NPA.
  • Calamity relief framework: banks may implement resolution on SLBC recommendation for standard loans not over thirty days overdue; invoke within 45 days, implement within 135 days.

How it changed

  • April 2026: RBI mandated that from 1 April 2027 banks must classify a wilful defaulter within six months of the account turning NPA.
  • April 2026: Calamity relief framework notified effective 1 July 2026 allowing banks to implement resolution on SLBC recommendation.

Traps

  • Wilful default requires deliberate non-payment, diversion of loan proceeds, or fraudulent disposal of secured assets; ordinary business failure does not constitute wilful default.
  • A single loan officer cannot unilaterally declare a borrower a wilful defaulter; it strictly requires approval from both the Identification and Review Committees.

★ Urban Co-operative Banks regulatory tiers
In plain words: Community-oriented cooperative banking institutions regulated under a four-tiered prudential structure based on deposit size.

Why it exists

  • Provides localized retail credit and deposit services to urban and semi-urban small businesses, traders, and households.
  • Tiered structure prevents smaller unit banks from facing crushing regulatory burdens while ensuring larger multi-state UCBs maintain commercial banking stability standards.

How it works

  • UCB categorized into Tier 1, Tier 2, Tier 3, or Tier 4 based on audited deposits at the close of the preceding financial year.
  • Regulatory standards including minimum net worth, CRAR, lending limits, and board governance scale up with tier level.
  • Dual control: Banking activities (licensing, capital, management, lending) supervised by RBI; incorporation, elections, and administration supervised by Registrar of Co-operative Societies.

Fixed facts

  • Unsecured lending limit: aggregate ceiling on unsecured loans and advances is 20% of total loans and advances based on audited balance sheet of preceding year.

How it changed

  • May 2026: RBI Governance Amendment Directions 2026 mandated a three-year cooling-off period after continuous tenure of 10 years for co-operative bank directors.
  • April 2026: Amendment Directions set 20% aggregate ceiling on unsecured advances and 20-year housing loan tenure for Tier 1 and Tier 2 UCBs, in force from 1 October 2026.

Traps

  • Dual control persists: RBI regulates banking operations, capital, and prudential norms, while the Registrar of Co-operative Societies oversees administrative matters and board elections.
  • The minimum CRAR for Tier 1 UCBs is 9%, but for Tiers 2, 3, and 4 it is 12%, which is higher than the 9% required for commercial banks.
