★ Expected Credit Loss provisioning
In plain words: Banks assess forward-looking default probabilities and set aside provisions before a borrower defaults, rather than waiting for distress.

Why it exists

  • The earlier incurred loss model recognised impairment only after a default occurred, leading to sudden provisioning shocks during downturns.
  • Forward-looking provisioning forces banks to build capital buffers gradually as credit risk increases, protecting the stability of the financial system.

How it works

  • A loan starts in stage one as a performing asset, requiring twelve-month expected loss provisioning.
  • When credit risk rises significantly without default, the exposure moves to stage two, requiring full lifetime expected credit loss provisioning.
  • Upon default beyond ninety days overdue, the asset enters stage three as non-performing, requiring comprehensive lifetime loss provisioning.
  • Expected credit loss equals probability of default multiplied by loss given default multiplied by exposure at default.

Fixed facts

  • Stage 1 Normal loans 0.4% minimum (12-month loss)
  • Stage 2 Risky loans (e.g. 60-90 days overdue) 5% minimum
  • Stage 3 NPAs Full lifetime loss
  • 1 April 2027 RBI ECL and Basel III credit-risk norms start

How it changed

  • 27 Apr 2026: Transition → extra provision from the switch can be spread over 4 years, to 31 Mar 2031 , with CET1 add-back during that period
  • 27 Apr 2026: All loans at 31 March 2027 under the effective interest rate (EIR) regime by 31 March 2030

Traps

  • Incurred loss accounting waits for actual delinquency events; expected credit loss accounting assesses probabilities in advance.
  • Stage one covers twelve months of expected defaults, whereas stage two and stage three mandate provisions across the entire remaining lifetime of the facility.

In the news

  • October 2026: Statement on Developmental and Regulatory Policies (7 Oct 2026)
  • April 2026: RBI: ECL norms (final)
★ Ways and Means Advances
In plain words: The central bank extends short-term credit facilities to the government to bridge temporary gaps between cash receipts and expenditure.

Why it exists

  • Government revenue collections arrive unevenly during the year, while ongoing welfare and administrative commitments must be disbursed smoothly.
  • It prevents the government from borrowing impulsively from open markets at high yields during brief cash-flow deficits.

How it works

  • The central bank and the government fix advance limits for each half of the fiscal year based on projected expenditure profiles.
  • When the government draws funds within the limit, interest is charged at the benchmark repo rate.
  • If the drawdown persists beyond agreed ceilings or durations, punitive overdraft interest applies above the standard repo rate.
  • Reaching high utilization triggers formal debt issuances in sovereign bond markets.

Fixed facts

  • WMA: introduced 1 Apr 1997 under Section 17(5) RBI Act (replaced ad-hoc treasury bills); 90 days; states' Special Drawing Facility 1% below repo

Traps

  • Ways and Means Advances are not permanent budget deficit financing; they must be cleared within ninety days.
  • The facility carries interest at the repo rate rather than zero cost, with higher punitive rates for extended overdrafts.

In the news

  • September 2026: RBI fixes H2 FY27 Ways and Means Advances limit at ₹50,000 crore
★ Floating Rate Bonds
In plain words: Sovereign debt securities whose interest coupons periodically reset based on prevailing benchmark Treasury bill yields plus a predetermined spread.

Why it exists

  • Fixed-rate bonds expose investors and the sovereign issuer to interest rate risk when market rates fluctuate over extended horizons.
  • Variable coupon structures offer protection against inflation and changing yield environments, expanding debt absorption capacity.

How it works

  • The sovereign issues debt securities with coupon payments linked directly to short-term money market auctions.
  • At each scheduled reset date, the coupon is recalculated from recent auction yields plus the fixed spread.
  • Interest is paid half-yearly to bondholders until final principal redemption at maturity.

Fixed facts

  • Linked to 182-day T-Bill yield: average weighted average yield of last 3 auctions + fixed spread 0.64 pp . Reset every 6 months; interest paid half-yearly.

How it changed

  • October 2026: RBI fixed FRB 2028 rate at 6.45% for 4 Oct 2026 – 3 Apr 2027 .

Traps

  • Floating rate bond coupons fluctuate with short-term market yields, unlike conventional fixed-rate sovereign securities.
  • The spread above the benchmark remains constant throughout the bond tenure even as the base yield fluctuates.

In the news

  • October 2026: FRB 2028 coupon
★ Forex reserve management and swap facilities
In plain words: The central bank maintains foreign currency and gold assets while deploying currency swap facilities to manage domestic liquidity and exchange stability.

Why it exists

  • Cross-border trade and capital flows generate sudden supply-demand mismatches in domestic currency markets.
  • Strategic foreign currency reserves and bilateral currency swaps insulate the domestic economy from international external shocks.

How it works

  • The central bank accumulates foreign currencies, gold, special drawing rights, and reserve positions through market purchases and operations.
  • In a buy/sell swap, the authority absorbs foreign currency from dealer banks against domestic currency, agreeing to reverse the transaction later.
  • To stabilise volatile currency swings, designated foreign exchange windows provide direct foreign exchange access to critical importers.

Fixed facts

  • Reserves = foreign currency assets + gold + SDRs + reserve position in IMF; foreign currency assets are the largest part
  • Exchange rate regime → managed float
  • Tarapore Committees on Capital Account Convertibility → 1997, 2006

How it changed

  • October 2026: Hedging without proving underlying exposure: $100 mn → $5 mn (all AD banks together); same cut for exchange-traded currency derivatives (all exchanges together).
  • May 2026: Buy/sell swap → RBI buys dollars now, rupee liquidity rises; sell/buy swap → RBI sells dollars now, rupee liquidity falls
  • May 2026: Auction on 26 May 2026; tenor 3 years; near leg 29 May 2026, far leg 29 May 2029; minimum bid $10 million, then multiples of $1 million

Traps

  • A buy/sell swap injects domestic currency into the banking system, whereas a sell/buy swap absorbs domestic liquidity.
  • Reserves consist of four separate components rather than foreign banknotes alone.

★ Foreign Exchange Management Act framework
In plain words: A civil statutory architecture governing external cross-border trade, foreign currency transactions, and overseas financial commitments.

Why it exists

  • Strict criminal regulations under earlier statutory regimes hindered economic growth and cross-border commercial engagement.
  • A modern civil regulatory system facilitates external trade and payments while orderly developing foreign exchange markets.

How it works

  • Current account transactions are generally permitted unless explicitly restricted or prohibited by central rules.
  • Capital account transactions altering overseas assets or liabilities are prohibited unless specifically permitted under delegated directions.
  • Authorised dealers execute cross-border remittance workflows and verify regulatory compliance before settling claims.

Fixed facts

  • FEMA 1999 (civil law) replaced FERA 1973 (criminal) | effective 1 Jun 2000
  • Current account convertibility → Aug 1994 (IMF Article VIII) | capital account → partial

How it changed

  • October 2026: Foreign Exchange Management (Export and Import of Goods and Services) Regulations, 2026; effective 1 Oct 2026.
  • May 2026: Notification dated 30 April 2026; in force from Gazette publication on 6 May 2026; applications through PRAVAAH portal to the RBI regional office
  • April 2026: No resident may be principal debtor, surety or creditor in a guarantee with a non-resident unless permitted; exempt: guarantees by AD bank branches abroad or in IFSC; IPC by custodian banks for FPIs to central counterparties

Traps

  • The statute is a civil code with monetary penalties, unlike the criminal detention provisions of the predecessor regime.
  • Current account transactions are fully convertible under international treaty obligations, whereas capital account flows remain partially regulated.

★ Basel III capital and risk framework
In plain words: Internationally coordinated prudential banking standards prescribing capital quality, conservation buffers, leverage ratios, and liquidity thresholds.

Why it exists

  • Under-capitalised commercial banking institutions can fail during severe macroeconomic stress, spreading contagion across the global economy.
  • Standardised capital adequacy ratios and liquid asset requirements ensure banks absorb losses and meet depositor demands independently.

How it works

  • Banking assets are risk-weighted according to credit, market, and operational risk metrics under supervisory rules.
  • Banks must hold core equity tier one and tier two capital matching or exceeding statutory risk-weighted asset ratios.
  • Leverage metrics establish unweighted exposure backstops, while buffer requirements enforce capital conservation during economic stress.

Fixed facts

  • CET1 (Common Equity Tier 1) 5.5%
  • Leverage ratio 4% for D-SIBs, 3.5% for others
  • Framework set by RBI guidelines in February 2015
  • Two aims: build extra capital in expansion to draw down in stress; curb indiscriminate lending in credit booms

How it changed

  • May 2026: RBI Amendment Directions, 8 May 2026, immediate effect: commercial banks (Fifth Amendment), SFBs (Fourth Amendment), payments banks (Second Amendment)
  • April 2026: From 1 April 2027; 150% risk weight on unrated corporate/NBFC exposures only above ₹500 cr (₹200 cr was proposed)
  • April 2026: Unrated bank exposures: long-term 100%, short-term 50%; foreign bank branches may use parent ratings

Traps

  • Domestic capital requirements are stricter than international baseline standards.
  • Leverage ratio calculations evaluate total exposure without applying risk weight reductions.

In the news

  • October 2026: RBI: CVA Framework Directions, 2026
  • October 2026: RBI: new operational-risk and leverage-ratio norms from 1 April 2027
  • May 2026: Quarterly profits in CET1 capital: condition removed
  • May 2026: Countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB) not activated
  • May 2026: RBI draft: Pillar 3 disclosures by banks
  • April 2026: RBI: Basel III credit-risk (standardised approach) final directions
  • April 2026: RBI capital, card and classification directions

Why it exists

How it works

  • October 2026: MDR on select UPI merchant payments deferred until after Diwali 2026.

Traps

In plain words: Sovereign digital legal tender issued directly by the monetary authority representing a direct sovereign claim on the central bank balance sheet.

Why it exists

How it works

★ Self-Regulatory Organisations in financial markets
In plain words: Non-governmental industry bodies recognised by statutory authorities to establish codes of conduct, monitor market integrity, and settle disputes.

Why it exists

How it works

Traps

  • Aggregate exposure is calculated against total eligible regulatory capital rather than bank asset size alone.
  • Loans against equity shares are subject to conservative regulatory margins rather than discretionary bank limits.
