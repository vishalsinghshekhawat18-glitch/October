★ Expected Credit Loss provisioning
In plain words: Under Expected Credit Loss norms, banks assess credit risk and set aside provisions in advance based on forward-looking estimates rather than waiting for an actual default to occur.

Why it exists

  • The earlier incurred loss model delayed provisioning until after a borrower defaulted (too little, too late), which amplified bank distress during economic downturns.
  • Forward-looking provisioning forces banks to recognize credit deterioration early and build capital buffers before defaults materialize.

How it works

  • Loan originated → classified into Stage 1 (standard asset with low credit risk) → bank provisions twelve-month expected credit loss.
  • Significant increase in credit risk observed (e.g. overdue beyond thirty or sixty days) → loan migrates to Stage 2 → bank provisions full lifetime expected credit loss.
  • Objective default occurs (overdue beyond ninety days) → loan migrates to Stage 3 (credit-impaired / non-performing) → bank provisions full lifetime expected loss with highest provisioning coverage.
  • Loss estimation formula: Expected Credit Loss = Probability of Default × Loss Given Default × Exposure at Default.

Fixed facts

  • Regulatory supervisor: Reserve Bank of India.
  • Accounting standard foundation: Aligned with international standard IFRS 9 and Indian Accounting Standard Ind AS 109.
  • Scope of application: Scheduled commercial banks (excluding regional rural banks; All India Financial Institutions notified separately).
  • Three-stage classification: Stage 1 (twelve-month expected loss), Stage 2 (lifetime expected loss, not credit-impaired), Stage 3 (lifetime expected loss, credit-impaired).
  • Supervisory floors: Stage 1 general standard floor 0.4% (individual housing loans 0.25%); Stage 2 standard floor 5% (direct lending to State governments 2.5%).

How it changed

  • October 2026: RBI Statement on Developmental and Regulatory Policies reiterated phased implementation roadmap and capital transition buffers.
  • 27 April 2026: RBI issued final Master Directions on Expected Credit Loss-Based Approach for Loan Loss Provisioning, applicable from 1 April 2027.
  • Repeal Directions 2026: Ends the legacy income recognition, asset classification and provisioning (IRAC) framework for commercial banks.
  • Transition arrangements: Capital impact of initial provisioning spread over four years (up to 31 March 2031) with Common Equity Tier 1 capital add-back.
  • Effective Interest Rate method: Pre-existing loan portfolios given three-year window until 31 March 2030 to transition to EIR.
  • Wilful defaulter classification: Banks mandated to complete wilful defaulter classification within six months of an account entering Stage 3.

Traps

  • Expected Credit Loss is forward-looking and builds provisions before default, whereas IRAC required provisioning only after an account turned into an NPA.
  • Stage 1 requires twelve-month expected loss provisioning, while Stage 2 and Stage 3 both require lifetime expected loss provisioning.

In the news

  • October 2026: Statement on Developmental and Regulatory Policies (7 Oct 2026)
  • April 2026: RBI: Expected Credit Loss (ECL) norms (final)
★ Ways and Means Advances
In plain words: Ways and Means Advances are short-term loan facilities provided by the Reserve Bank of India to the Central and State Governments to bridge temporary mismatches in cash receipts and payments.

Why it exists

  • Government revenues and expenditures do not match on daily cash flows; WMA provides short-term cash flow support without resorting to permanent monetization of deficits.
  • Prevents abrupt fiscal disruptions and maintains smooth payment disbursements for public administration and welfare schemes.

How it works

  • Government encounters temporary cash shortfall → requests fund drawal under WMA facility within pre-agreed bi-annual limit.
  • Drawings within normal WMA limit → charged at prevailing repo rate → must be repaid within ninety days.
  • Drawings exceeding normal WMA limit → trigger Overdraft facility → charged at repo rate plus two percentage points.
  • Overdraft duration caps: Overdraft cannot exceed ten consecutive working days for the Central Government or fourteen consecutive working days for State Governments.

Fixed facts

  • Parent statute: Section 17(5) of the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934.
  • Tenure and repayment: Advances must be fully repaid within three months (ninety days) from the date of advance.
  • Two broad categories: Normal WMA (clean advance) and Special WMA / Special Drawing Facility (secured against government securities investments).
  • Interest structure: Normal WMA at repo rate; Overdraft at repo rate plus two percentage points; Special Drawing Facility at repo rate minus one percentage point.
  • Bi-annual limit revision: Limits for the Central Government are determined half-yearly (H1 April–September and H2 October–March) jointly by RBI and Ministry of Finance.

How it changed

  • September 2026: RBI and Government fixed the WMA limit for H2 FY27 (October 2026 – March 2027) at ₹50,000 crore for the Central Government.

Traps

  • WMA is strictly a short-term cash-flow bridge loan repayable within ninety days, not a long-term borrowing instrument or permanent deficit financing.
  • WMA is distinct from Treasury Bills, which are tradeable money market debt securities issued to the market.

★ Floating Rate Bonds
In plain words: Floating Rate Bonds are government securities whose coupon interest rate is not fixed at issuance, but is periodically reset according to an underlying money market benchmark plus a fixed spread.

Why it exists

  • Provides investors with a hedge against interest rate volatility in an uncertain or rising rate cycle.
  • Allows the government to borrow long-term funds without locking in high coupon rates when borrowing during elevated inflation cycles.

How it works

  • Base benchmark yield identified prior to coupon reset date (e.g. cut-off yield of 182-day Treasury Bills auctions).
  • Fixed spread determined at the primary auction is added to the benchmark rate.
  • Benchmark rate + spread = new coupon rate payable for the ensuing semi-annual interest period.
  • At maturity, bond is redeemed at par value regardless of coupon rate fluctuations.

Fixed facts

  • Issuing authority: Reserve Bank of India on behalf of the Government of India under Government Securities Act 2006.
  • Sovereign benchmark: Benchmark rate linked to average cut-off yield of the preceding three auctions of 182-day Treasury Bills.
  • Reset frequency: Semi-annually adjusted coupon rates.
  • Retail variant: Floating Rate Savings Bonds (FRSB), benchmarked to National Savings Certificate rate plus 0.35 percentage points; taxable and non-transferable.

How it changed

  • October 2026: RBI notified the semi-annual coupon reset for Floating Rate Bond 2028 based on cut-off yields in recent 182-day Treasury Bill auctions.

Traps

  • The coupon interest rate changes periodically, but the principal face value redeemed at maturity remains fixed at par.
  • Institutional Floating Rate Bonds trade on secondary financial markets, whereas retail Floating Rate Savings Bonds cannot be traded or transferred.

In the news

  • October 2026: Floating Rate Bond (FRB) 2028 coupon
★ Forex reserve management and swap facilities
In plain words: The regulatory and operational framework by which the Reserve Bank of India manages foreign exchange reserves, regulates currency flows, and conducts spot and swap operations to curb exchange rate volatility.

Why it exists

  • Ensures national external financial stability, maintains import cover, and buffers the domestic economy against sudden global capital reversals.
  • Calibrates domestic rupee liquidity while preventing disorderly speculative movements in the foreign exchange market.

How it works

  • Severe dollar outflow or depreciation pressure → RBI sells dollars in spot and forward markets, or conducts USD/INR sell/buy swaps to supply dollars and absorb domestic rupees.

  • Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS): Resident individuals permitted to remit up to $250,000 per financial year for permissible current and capital account transactions.
  • Exchange rate regime: Managed float; RBI intervenes to contain volatility without defending a fixed exchange rate level.

  • April 2026: Calibrated exchange-traded foreign exchange derivative norms.

Traps

  • India operates a managed float, not a fixed exchange rate regime; central bank interventions target excessive volatility rather than a specific numeric rupee level.
  • Current account transactions are fully convertible under Article VIII, but capital account transactions remain partially regulated.

★ Basel III capital and risk framework
In plain words: An internationally agreed set of regulatory measures designed to strengthen bank capital quality, leverage controls, and liquidity profiles to withstand systemic economic shocks.

Why it exists

  • Formulated by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision following the 2008 global financial crisis to address high leverage, poor capital quality, and systemic liquidity dry-ups in banking institutions.
  • Ensures commercial banks maintain sufficient loss-absorbing capital and liquid assets to prevent bank failures from spilling into the broader economy.

How it works

  • Pillar 1 (Minimum Capital Requirements): Risk-weighted assets measured across credit, market, and operational risk → bank must maintain minimum regulatory capital ratios.
  • Pillar 2 (Supervisory Review Process): Bank conducts Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP) → RBI reviews risk management and may mandate additional capital add-ons.
  • Pillar 3 (Market Discipline): Bank publishes comprehensive regulatory disclosures to enable market scrutiny of its risk profile and capital structure.

Fixed facts

  • Global standard-setter: Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) at the Bank for International Settlements (BIS), Basel.
  • India minimum capital rules: Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) minimum 5.5%; Tier 1 capital minimum 7%; Total Capital (CRAR) minimum 9% (higher than 8% international baseline).
  • Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB): Fixed at 2.5% composed of CET1 capital, bringing minimum total capital plus CCB requirement to 11.5%.
  • Countercyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB): Dynamic buffer varying between 0% and 2.5% of risk-weighted assets, activated during excessive credit growth cycles.
  • Liquidity metrics: Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) at 100% (high-quality liquid assets covering thirty-day stress outflows) and Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR) at 100%.
  • Leverage ratio: Minimum 3.5% for scheduled commercial banks and 4% for Domestic Systemically Important Banks (D-SIBs: SBI, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank).

How it changed

  • October 2026: RBI finalized Credit Valuation Adjustment (CVA) Risk Framework Directions 2026 and revised standardized approaches for operational risk and leverage ratios effective 1 April 2027.
  • May 2026: RBI removed the condition requiring prior audited accounts for including quarterly interim profits into CET1 capital; decided not to activate CCyB.
  • April 2026: RBI finalized Master Directions on Basel III Capital Regulations - Credit Risk (Standardised Approach) effective 1 April 2027.

Traps

  • India mandates a 9% minimum CRAR, which is stricter than the 8% minimum capital floor set by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision.
  • Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB) is a permanent 2.5% buffer, whereas Countercyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB) is dynamic and activated only when credit indicators signal overheating.

In the news

  • October 2026: RBI: Credit Valuation Adjustment (CVA) Framework Directions, 2026
  • October 2026: RBI: new operational-risk and leverage-ratio norms from 1 April 2027
  • May 2026: Quarterly profits in CET1 capital: condition removed
  • May 2026: Countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB) not activated
  • May 2026: RBI draft: Pillar 3 disclosures by banks
  • April 2026: RBI: Basel III credit-risk (standardised approach) final directions
  • April 2026: RBI capital, card and classification directions

Why it exists

  • Separates genuine commercial distress from deliberate financial fraud and intentional default.
  • Enforces severe penal and market access restrictions on wilful defaulters to maintain credit discipline across financial institutions.

How it works

  • Lender discovers intentional default, fund diversion, or unauthorized asset disposal → Identification Committee issues show-cause notice.
  • Borrower submits representation and is provided reasonable opportunity for personal hearing.
  • Review Committee reviews findings and confirms classification → borrower declared wilful defaulter.
  • Lender reports name to Credit Information Companies (CICs) → borrower debarred from fresh bank loans, capital markets, and floating new corporate entities for five years post-resolution.

Fixed facts

  • Regulatory foundation: RBI Master Directions on Treatment of Wilful Defaulters and Large Defaulters.
  • Monetary thresholds: Outstanding balance of ₹25 lakh and above for wilful default classification; ₹1 crore and above for reporting large defaulters.
  • Two-tier committee procedure: Mandates an Identification Committee (chaired by Executive Director) and a Review Committee (chaired by Managing Director / CEO).
  • Statutory disqualification: Section 29A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) explicitly bars wilful defaulters from submitting resolution plans for stressed assets.
  • Compromise settlements: Permitted under transparent board-approved policies; mandates a minimum cooling-off period of twelve months before fresh credit can be considered.

How it changed

  • April 2026: RBI issued comprehensive Master Directions on Wilful Defaulters, instituting six-month classification timelines post-NPA and clarifying relief provisions for borrowers affected by severe natural calamities.

Traps

  • Wilful default requires deliberate non-payment, diversion of loan proceeds, or fraudulent disposal of secured assets; ordinary business failure does not constitute wilful default.
  • A single loan officer cannot unilaterally declare a borrower a wilful defaulter; it strictly requires approval from both the Identification and Review Committees.

★ Urban Co-operative Banks regulatory tiers
In plain words: Urban Co-operative Banks are community-oriented cooperative banking institutions regulated under a four-tiered prudential structure based on deposit size.

Why it exists

  • Provides localized retail credit and deposit services to urban and semi-urban small businesses, traders, and households.
  • Tiered structure prevents smaller unit banks from facing crushing regulatory burdens while ensuring larger multi-state UCBs maintain commercial banking stability standards.

How it works

  • UCB categorized into Tier 1, Tier 2, Tier 3, or Tier 4 based on audited deposits at the close of the preceding financial year.
  • Regulatory standards including minimum net worth, capital adequacy (CRAR), lending limits, and board governance scale up with tier level.
  • Dual control: Banking activities (licensing, capital, management, lending) supervised by RBI; incorporation, elections, and administration supervised by Registrar of Co-operative Societies.

Fixed facts

  • Statutory extension: Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act 2020 amended Section 56 of the Banking Regulation Act 1949, giving RBI extensive supervisory control over co-operative banks.
  • Four-tier structure (based on N. S. Vishwanathan Committee):
  • Tier 1: Unit and salary-earner UCBs, and other UCBs with deposits up to ₹100 crore.
  • Tier 2: Deposits above ₹100 crore up to ₹1,000 crore.
  • Tier 3: Deposits above ₹1,000 crore up to ₹10,000 crore.
  • Tier 4: Deposits above ₹10,000 crore.
  • Minimum net worth: ₹2 crore for Tier 1 UCBs in a single district; ₹5 crore for all other UCBs.
  • Capital Adequacy (CRAR): Minimum 9% for Tier 1 UCBs; minimum 12% for Tier 2, Tier 3, and Tier 4 UCBs.
  • Priority Sector Lending target: Target of 75% of Adjusted Net Bank Credit.

How it changed

  • May 2026: RBI finalized governance directions mandating a mandatory three-year cooling-off period for directors of co-operative banks seeking re-election after completing consecutive terms.
  • April 2026: RBI finalized revised lending and exposure norms for Urban Co-operative Banks, calibrating exposure limits for single and group borrowers.

Traps

  • Dual control persists: RBI regulates banking operations, capital, and prudential norms, while the Registrar of Co-operative Societies (RCS or Central Registrar) oversees administrative matters and board elections.
  • The minimum CRAR for Tier 1 UCBs is 9%, but for Tiers 2, 3, and 4 it is 12%, which is higher than the 9% required for commercial banks.
