★ Index of Eight Core Industries
In plain words: A monthly production index tracking the physical output volume of foundational infrastructure and energy industries.

Why it exists

  • Core infrastructure sectors drive downstream manufacturing activity and signal industrial output trends early.
  • Frequent volume tracking of heavy industries provides essential leading data for business cycle surveillance.

How it works

  • Designated industrial authorities collect physical output volumes from major producers across core utility and mineral sectors.
  • Sectoral production metrics are indexed against base year volumes and weighted by capital intensity and industrial share.
  • Statistical agencies release the composite index monthly ahead of broad industrial production aggregates.

Fixed facts

  • New base 2022-23 (old 2011-12); core industries 8 → 9 with Iron Ore added
  • Base year 2022-23; covers 9 industries: coal, crude oil, natural gas, refinery products, fertilizers, steel, cement, electricity, iron ore. Biggest weights: electricity (30.932), refinery products (22.572), steel (17.584).

How it changed

Traps

★ Balance of Payments and Current Account Deficit
In plain words: The accounting record of all economic and financial transactions conducted between domestic residents and the rest of the world.

Why it exists

  • Persistent trade imbalances and excessive external deficits can deplete foreign exchange reserves and spark currency crises.
  • Monitoring cross-border trade and capital flows ensures external debt sustainability and macroeconomic financial resilience.

How it works

  • Customs and banking channels log all cross-border merchandise shipments, service transfers, and financial remittances.
  • Transactions are categorized into current accounts covering trade in goods and services, and capital accounts covering investment flows.
  • Net surpluses or deficits in external balances determine movements in sovereign foreign exchange reserve holdings.

Fixed facts

How it changed

  • September 2026: CAD: USD 4.2 bn = 0.5% of GDP (Q1 FY26: USD 3.4 bn = 0.4%)
  • January 2026: BoP Oct–Dec 2025: CAD Apr–Dec 2025 $30.1 bn (1% of GDP) vs $36.6 bn; net FDI $3 bn vs $0.6 bn; FPI net outflow $4.3 bn (vs inflow $9.4 bn); reserves depleted $30.8 bn

Traps

  • The current account tracks goods, services, and unilateral transfers, whereas asset investments sit on the capital account.

★ Bilateral and regional currency swap frameworks
In plain words: Inter-central bank agreements enabling exchange of domestic currencies to provide short-term foreign exchange liquidity support.

Why it exists

  • Emerging market partner countries experience sudden external balance-of-payments pressures and foreign currency shortages during global crises.
  • Bilateral and regional swap lines reinforce regional financial stability without demanding immediate international market borrowing.

How it works

  • Two central banks establish a standing agreement fixing swap borrowing limits and eligible drawdown currencies.
  • The requesting monetary authority draws foreign currency against an equivalent deposit of domestic currency at predetermined interest benchmarks.
  • At maturity, the swap is reversed or rolled over by mutual consent, restoring original currency reserves.

  • April 2026: India approved ₹30 billion first withdrawal (SAARC Currency Swap Framework 2024-27, between RBI and Maldives)

Traps

  • Currency swaps provide temporary liquidity assistance rather than non-repayable fiscal aid or permanent grants.
  • Drawdowns remain subject to repayment tenors and pre-agreed benchmark interest conditions.

How it works
