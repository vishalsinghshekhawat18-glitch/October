Mains: GS-I Part A: Renaissance and Reformation

The big picture

Between about 1300 and 1600, educated Europeans, starting in the cities of northern Italy, changed how they looked at human life. They returned to Greek and Roman texts, valued human ability and life in this world, and made art and writing that studied nature closely. This shift is called the Renaissance, French for "rebirth". It did not overthrow the medieval world at once. It grew out of a change in the economy: trade had made Italian towns rich, and rich townsmen paid for scholars and artists who served their own tastes and needs. Printing then carried the new learning across Europe, and that wider reading public prepared the ground for the Reformation (W02), modern science and the voyages of exploration.

Core notes

W01.1 The medieval background and why change came

Medieval Western Europe rested on two institutions. Feudalism tied the peasant to a lord's land and tied the lord to a king through military service. The Roman Catholic Church owned vast lands, controlled education and gave the main explanation of life: this world was a preparation for the next, and truth lay in scripture as explained by the clergy. Scholasticism, the learning of the medieval universities, used logic mainly to support that teaching.

From about the 11th century this order began to loosen. Farm output rose, towns grew, and the Crusades widened trade with the eastern Mediterranean. Then the Black Death (1347–51) killed perhaps a third of the population. Labour became scarce, so surviving peasants could demand better terms and serfdom weakened in the west. The Church's prestige also fell, partly because of the plague and partly because of the Great Schism (1378–1417), when rival popes claimed office. A society with more money, more towns and less certainty was ready for new ideas.

W01.2 Why Italy came first

The question "why Italy?" is the core of the RAS 2016 question. Five factors worked together.

  • Trade and wealth. Venice and Genoa dominated Mediterranean trade with the East. Florence grew rich on woollen cloth and banking.
  • Independent city-states. Italy had no single monarch. Florence, Venice and Milan governed themselves or were ruled by local families, so civic life and public service were valued. A merchant or lawyer needed rhetoric, history and ethics more than theology.
  • Patrons. The Medici bankers of Florence, the Sforza dukes of Milan and the popes at Rome competed for prestige by paying artists and collecting manuscripts.
  • The Roman inheritance. Ruins, statues and inscriptions stood all around Italians, who saw ancient Rome as their own past.
  • Contact with Greek and Arab learning. Greek scholars came from Byzantium: Chrysoloras taught at Florence from 1397, and more arrived around the fall of Constantinople in 1453. Works of Aristotle had already reached Europe through Arabic translations and the commentaries of Ibn Rushd (Averroes), and medical knowledge through Ibn Sina (Avicenna).
Concept

Humanism. Humanism was first an education, the studia humanitatis: grammar, rhetoric, history, poetry and moral philosophy, studied from classical texts. It arose because the urban elite wanted a training for public and business life that the Church-centred schools did not give. It worked through careful reading of original texts. Lorenzo Valla used language study to prove in 1440 that the Donation of Constantine, a document that supported papal claims to territory, was a later forgery. Humanists did not usually reject God; Erasmus was a devout Christian. What changed was the emphasis, from the next world to human dignity and ability in this one. Pico della Mirandola's Oration on the Dignity of Man (1486) is the classic statement.

W01.3 Art: seeing the world as it is

Medieval art served religious teaching and did not try to show space or bodies exactly. Renaissance artists wanted their paintings to look true, and to do that they studied mathematics and anatomy. Linear perspective, worked out by Brunelleschi in the early 15th century, showed depth on a flat surface. Chiaroscuro used light and shade to model forms. Patrons wanted their fame and their city displayed, so portraits and secular subjects grew alongside religious ones.

Leonardo shows the Renaissance ideal of the many-sided person: his notebooks hold anatomy, machines and studies of water alongside painting. The artist's status rose too, from a craftsman in a guild to a celebrated individual. Giorgio Vasari's Lives of the Artists (1550) recorded this, and used the word rinascita (rebirth).

W01.4 Literature and political thought

Writing in the vernacular widened the reading public beyond those who knew Latin. Dante's Divine Comedy, Petrarch's sonnets and Boccaccio's Decameron made Tuscan the base of literary Italian. Later, national literatures grew elsewhere: Chaucer in English, Rabelais and Montaigne in French, Cervantes (Don Quixote) in Spanish, and Shakespeare.

The most lasting political book was Machiavelli's The Prince. Italy was being invaded by France and Spain from 1494, and Machiavelli, a Florentine official, asked how a ruler actually keeps power. He judged actions by results and not by Christian morality, which separated politics from religious ethics. This is why he is seen as a founder of modern political thought. Castiglione's The Courtier (1528) set the model of the cultivated gentleman.

W01.5 Printing, science and exploration

Printing made the Renaissance a European event. Paper had come from China through the Arab world. Johannes Gutenberg combined movable metal type, oil-based ink and a press at Mainz, and printed his Bible about 1455. Books became cheap and identical copies spread quickly, so scholars could compare and correct texts and ideas could not easily be suppressed. Luther's writings of 1517 and after spread by this route (W02).

Science began to rest on observation. Copernicus argued in 1543 that the earth moves around the sun, against the Ptolemaic view the Church accepted. Vesalius corrected ancient anatomy from dissection in the same year. Galileo, later, used the telescope and was forced by the Inquisition to recant in 1633. This line of work led to the Scientific Revolution and to Newton (W02).

Exploration had practical causes. Europeans wanted direct access to Asian spices and gold, and Ottoman control of the eastern routes raised the incentive, though Portugal's Atlantic voyages had begun well before 1453. The compass, better maps and the caravel made long voyages possible. Dias rounded the Cape in 1488, Columbus reached the Americas in 1492, Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498, and Magellan's expedition (1519–22) sailed round the world. This opened the colonial age that reached India (I26).

W01.6 How far did it spread, and whom did it reach?

North of the Alps the Renaissance took a more religious form. Christian humanists such as Erasmus and More used classical scholarship to criticise Church abuses and return to the Bible itself; Erasmus published a Greek New Testament in 1516. Dürer took Italian technique to Germany. Because these scholars read scripture directly and printed widely, the Northern Renaissance led straight to the Reformation.

The limits matter for a balanced answer. The movement touched a small literate urban elite, and most peasants saw little change. Renaissance states were not more peaceful, and belief in witchcraft and persecution continued. Women's educational gains were confined to a few, such as the writer Christine de Pizan and the patron Isabella d'Este. Its results were long-term: a confidence in reason and in the individual, and a habit of testing old authority against evidence.

Debate

Was it really a "rebirth"? Jacob Burckhardt (1860) described a sharp break, the birth of modern individualism in Italy. C.H. Haskins (1927) replied that a "Renaissance of the twelfth century" had already revived Latin classics and universities. Joan Kelly (1977) asked whether women had a Renaissance at all. Others stress the debt to Arab and Byzantine scholars, which shows the classical revival was not a purely European achievement. A safe answer: real change, but gradual, elite and built on medieval foundations.

What historians say

Jacob Burckhardt, The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy (1860), set the classic view of individualism born in Italy. Jules Michelet had already popularised the term in 1855. C.H. Haskins pushed the roots back to the 12th century. Peter Burke treats it as a social process in which patrons, markets and audiences matter as much as genius. NCERT's Themes in World History also stresses the Arab contribution and the role of Italian towns.

The Renaissance was the revival of classical learning and a new human-centred outlook, from about the 14th century. Its base was economic: Mediterranean trade made Italian towns rich and created a lay urban class. Independent city-states such as Florence and Venice valued civic skills, and patrons like the Medici funded scholars and artists. Roman ruins, Greek scholars from Byzantium and Arabic translations of Aristotle supplied the learning. The Black Death and the Great Schism weakened feudal and Church authority. Gutenberg's printing press then spread these ideas across Europe. Thus the Renaissance grew from social and economic change rather than a sudden break with the medieval past.

The big picture

In 1517 a German monk, Martin Luther, attacked the Church's sale of indulgences. Within a generation Western Christendom had split into Catholic and Protestant churches, and rulers had taken new power over religion. Luther himself wanted to reform the Church and not to divide it. His challenge spread because it joined three forces: long anger at Church wealth and abuses, German princes who wanted power and land, and a printing press that made pamphlets cheap. The Catholic Church reformed itself in reply, and over a century of religious wars ended in a Europe of sovereign states. Then, from the late 17th century, thinkers of the Enlightenment applied the methods of the new science to society itself, and asked whether kings, churches and privileges could justify themselves before reason. Those questions led to the revolutions of America and France.

Core notes

W02.1 Why the Reformation came

By 1500 the Church was the richest landowner in Europe and it collected tithes and fees from all. Many of its offices were bought and sold (simony), bishops often held several posts at once, and clergy were exempt from ordinary courts. Earlier critics had raised these issues: John Wycliffe in England and Jan Hus in Bohemia, who was burnt in 1415. Humanists such as Erasmus mocked abuses and sent readers back to the Greek New Testament.

Politics made the difference. Rising monarchs and German princes resented money flowing to Rome and courts beyond their control. Germany was especially open: it had no strong central ruler, and hundreds of small states could protect a rebel. The trigger came when Pope Leo X needed money to rebuild St Peter's in Rome, and indulgences were sold in Germany by the Dominican Johann Tetzel.

Concept

Indulgences. The Church taught that sins carried punishment after death, which could be reduced by good works. An indulgence granted a reduction drawn from the merits of Christ and the saints. It arose as a pious practice, for example as a reward for crusading, but by 1500 it had become a source of revenue, and preachers made it sound as if a payment bought salvation. Luther's protest was theological: no payment could affect God's forgiveness. The Council of Trent abolished the office of indulgence seller in 1562.

W02.2 Luther and his ideas

Luther, a professor at Wittenberg, reached his central belief through personal anxiety: humans cannot earn salvation by their own works; it comes only through faith, as God's free gift. In 1517 he set out 95 Theses against indulgences. Printers spread them through Germany within weeks. In 1520 he published three major pamphlets, and in 1521 he refused to recant before Emperor Charles V at the Diet of Worms. Frederick the Wise of Saxony sheltered him at Wartburg, where he translated the New Testament into German.

Concept

Universal priesthood (priesthood of all believers). Luther taught that every baptised Christian has direct access to God and needs no priest as a go-between. He stated it in his 1520 address to the German nobility, in order to deny the clergy's claim to be a separate and higher estate. In practice clergy remained, but as ministers appointed for a duty and not as a sacred caste. They could marry, and the laity were encouraged to read the Bible in their own language. It also let secular rulers act in church matters.

Luther was socially conservative. When peasants rose in the Peasants' War (1524–25), citing his language of Christian freedom, he urged princes to crush them. Lutheranism therefore grew as a religion protected by princes. The name "Protestant" comes from the princes' protest at the Diet of Speyer (1529). The Peace of Augsburg (1555) let each German ruler choose Catholicism or Lutheranism for his subjects: cuius regio, eius religio.

W02.3 Other reformers and the Catholic reply

Zwingli led reform in Zurich. John Calvin, a French lawyer, made Geneva a disciplined godly city from 1541. His predestination taught that God has already chosen who will be saved. Calvinism had a clear organisation without bishops, and it spread to Scotland (John Knox), the Netherlands, France (the Huguenots) and England (the Puritans). In England the cause was political: Henry VIII wanted his marriage annulled, the pope refused, and the Act of Supremacy (1534) made the king head of the church.

The Catholic response is called the Counter-Reformation or Catholic Reformation. The Council of Trent (1545–63) reaffirmed doctrine, including the seven sacraments and the value of works, but it also corrected abuses and ordered seminaries to train priests. The Jesuits, founded by Ignatius Loyola and approved in 1540, ran excellent schools and sent missionaries abroad; Francis Xavier reached Goa in 1542. The Roman Inquisition and the Index of Prohibited Books policed belief.

W02.4 What the Reformation changed

Religious unity ended, and war followed: the French Wars of Religion, the Dutch Revolt and the Thirty Years War (1618–48). The Peace of Westphalia (1648) recognised rulers' authority within their territories, and it is often taken as the start of the modern system of sovereign states. Monarchs gained church land and control over religion. Bible translation strengthened national languages. Literacy grew because believers were expected to read.

Debate

Did Protestantism create capitalism? Max Weber, in The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1905), argued that Calvinist believers sought signs of being chosen through disciplined work and saving, which encouraged capitalist habits. R.H. Tawney saw a more complicated two-way relation, and critics point to capitalism in Catholic Italy and Flanders long before Calvin. A careful answer treats it as a helpful influence and not as a single cause.

W02.5 From the Scientific Revolution to the Enlightenment

The new science gave Europe a method and a model. Copernicus, Kepler and Galileo overturned the old picture of the heavens. Francis Bacon urged experiment and induction, and Descartes urged systematic doubt. Newton's Principia (1687) showed that one set of mathematical laws governed falling apples and planets. If nature had laws that reason could find, thinkers asked, might society have them too? Religious wars and the absolute monarchy of Louis XIV made that question urgent, and the growth of print, salons and coffee houses gave it a public.

Concept

The Enlightenment. The Enlightenment was the 18th-century European intellectual movement that held that reason, not tradition or revelation, should judge institutions and guide human progress. It grew from the success of science and from disgust at religious persecution and arbitrary rule. It worked through books, the Encyclopédie, salons and correspondence, with the French philosophes at its centre. Kant summed it up in 1784 with the motto "Dare to know". Its critics, from Romantics onward, said that it undervalued feeling, custom and faith.

What historians say

Max Weber linked Calvinism and capitalism, and R.H. Tawney qualified the claim in Religion and the Rise of Capitalism (1926). Peter Gay treats the Enlightenment as a confident movement of secular reason, while Robert Darnton studies its books, publishers and readers, and Roger Chartier asks whether it caused the French Revolution or was invented afterwards as its cause. Horkheimer and Adorno criticise it for treating reason as a tool of domination.

How to frame it: Abuses and earlier critics → Rulers and German politics → Printing and the trigger of indulgences → Consequences: division, Trent, wars → State power, literacy → Conclusion

Model answer (96 words)

The Reformation arose from Church wealth, simony and clerical privilege, already criticised by Wycliffe, Hus and Erasmus. Princes resented payments to Rome, and fragmented Germany could shelter a rebel. The sale of indulgences provoked Luther's 95 Theses (1517), which printing spread quickly. Western Christianity then split into Lutheran, Calvinist, Anglican and Catholic churches. Rome replied with the Council of Trent and the Jesuits. Religious wars ended at Westphalia (1648) in a system of sovereign states. Rulers gained church lands and control, while vernacular Bibles strengthened literacy and national languages. Tier A. The most-asked world history unit: RAS Mains 2021 (10 marks, socio-economic revolution), 2023 (10 marks, New Imperialism), 2024 (5 marks, textile inventions) and 2024 (10 marks, opium and China). Learn the textile sequence exactly and the logic of the link to imperialism.

The big picture

From about 1760 Britain began to make goods in a new way: with machines in place of hand tools, with coal and steam in place of muscle, wind and water, and in factories in place of homes. Output grew steadily and, for the first time in history, kept growing. But this was a change in how people lived as well as how they made things. Villagers became town wage-earners, the working day was set by the machine, new classes faced each other, and new ideas such as socialism and trade unions appeared. Industrial nations then needed raw materials, markets and places to invest. From about 1870, armed with steamships, telegraphs and rifles, they seized most of Africa and much of Asia. This second part is the New Imperialism, and it was also a major cause of World War I (W07).

Core notes

W03.1 Why Britain first

The term "Industrial Revolution" was popularised by Arnold Toynbee in lectures given in 1880–81; the French and others had used it earlier. Historians still ask why it happened first in Britain, not in China or India, which were long richer and whose cotton was the world's finest. No single cause is enough. Britain combined several advantages that reinforced each other.

  • Agriculture. Enclosures and new crop rotations raised farm output, which fed a growing population and pushed labour off the land.
  • Capital and finance. Overseas trade, including slavery and colonial profit, built wealth. The Bank of England (1694) and country banks made credit available.
  • Coal and iron lay close together, often close to water. Canals and a long coast made transport cheap.
  • Markets. The empire and a large internal market with no internal customs barriers. Tariffs protected British cloth from Indian competition.
  • Politics and society. After 1688 Parliament protected property, and patents rewarded invention. Practical tinkerers worked in a culture that valued useful knowledge.
  • Wages and fuel. R.C. Allen's explanation: British wages were high and coal was cheap, so it paid to invent machines that replaced labour with coal. In India and China, with cheap labour, it did not.

The first factories were in cotton, in Lancashire, because cotton was new, not bound by guild rules, and enjoyed a huge demand created by Indian calicoes that Britain now wanted to imitate.

W03.2 The textile inventions: one bottleneck after another

This is the 2024 question. The point is not a list but a sequence: each invention solved one shortage and thereby created the next. Cloth-making has two stages, spinning thread and weaving it into cloth.

Watt's steam engine, applied to textile mills from the 1780s, then freed factories from riverbanks, and cotton towns such as Manchester grew around coal. British cotton output soared, and cheap machine cloth was soon flooding India (I31).

Concept

The factory system. Before machines, merchants gave out materials to families working at home, the "putting-out" or domestic system. Costly and water-driven machines could not be placed in cottages, so owners gathered workers in one building. Arkwright's mill at Cromford (1771) is the classic early case. The factory changed work itself: owners set hours by the clock, workers no longer owned tools or their product, and discipline replaced the family's own rhythm. This is why historians see the factory, and not any single machine, as the central invention.

W03.3 Steam, iron, coal and railways

Iron and coal advanced together. Darby's coke smelting (1709) freed iron from scarce charcoal; Henry Cort's puddling process (1784) made wrought iron cheap. Steam power came from the mines: Newcomen's engine pumped water; James Watt's separate condenser (patented 1769) saved fuel, and his partnership with Matthew Boulton made it a general source of power by the 1780s.

Transport linked it all. Canals, such as the Bridgewater Canal (1761), halved the price of coal in Manchester. George Stephenson's locomotives ran on the Stockton and Darlington line (1825) and the Liverpool and Manchester line (1830). Railways created a huge demand for iron, coal and engineers, and they tied together national markets. Steamships and the Suez Canal (1869) then did the same across the world.

After about 1870 a Second Industrial Revolution was led by Germany and the USA: Bessemer steel, chemicals, electricity and oil. Germany caught up through state support, banks and technical schools, and the USA through a large market and mass production. Japan industrialised from above after the Meiji Restoration of 1868. By 1900 Britain had lost its lead, which sharpened rivalry for colonies.

W03.4 The social revolution

The 2021 question quoted the claim that this was a socio-economic revolution, and here is the evidence.

Cities. Britain became the first mainly urban society: over half its people lived in towns by the 1851 census. Housing was crowded and sewers absent. Cholera struck in 1831–32. Life expectancy in Manchester was far below the national level. Edwin Chadwick's report of 1842 led to the Public Health Act of 1848.

Classes. Land had once defined status. Now an industrial middle class and a wage-earning working class grew, each conscious of its interest. The middle class won the vote in the Reform Act of 1832, and workers demanded it in Chartism (1838–48).

Work, women and children. Hours of 12 to 16 were common. Pauper children were sent to mills and small boys and girls worked in mines. Women earned wages but at lower rates, and work was separated from the home. Reports led to the Factory Act of 1833 (no children under nine in textile mills, with inspectors), the Mines Act of 1842 (no women or young children underground) and the Ten Hours Act of 1847.

Resistance and organisation. The Luddites (1811–16) smashed machines that threatened skilled hand workers. Trade unions were legalised in 1824–25. The Rochdale Pioneers started the cooperative movement in 1844.

Ideas. Adam Smith and later Ricardo argued for free markets; Malthus warned about population. Robert Owen tried model mills at New Lanark, while Marx and Engels (The Communist Manifesto, 1848) argued that capitalism creates its own gravediggers, an idea that came to power in Russia (W06). Engels's Condition of the Working Class in England (1845) drew on Manchester.

Debate

Did living standards rise? The "standard of living debate" divides pessimists such as E.P. Thompson and Eric Hobsbawm, who stress urban misery and loss of independence, from optimists such as R.M. Hartwell, who point to rising real wages. Most now accept that gains were small until about 1840, and came with a heavy cost in health and hours. Was it a "revolution" at all? N.F.R. Crafts shows that overall growth was slower than once believed, but the change in the structure of society was still real.

W03.5 From industry to New Imperialism

Colonies were old. The New Imperialism of about 1870–1914 differed in speed, in scale and in motive. In 1870 Europeans held about a tenth of Africa, and by 1914 almost all of it, apart from Ethiopia and Liberia. Old mercantile empires wanted trade goods; the new ones wanted direct control. Industrialisation drove the change in four ways.

  • Raw materials. Factories needed cotton, rubber, tin, copper, oil and palm oil.
  • Markets. Output outran home demand, and a long price depression from 1873 made rivals raise tariffs, so a protected colony looked safe.
  • Surplus capital. Savings found higher returns in railways and mines abroad.
  • Technology made conquest cheap. Steamships, telegraphs, quinine against malaria, and the Maxim gun gave a few soldiers power over many. Germany's and the USA's rise after 1870 added competition: nobody wished to be left out.

Non-economic factors added force: nationalism, strategic bases, and Social Darwinism with its racist "civilising mission", which Kipling's poem called "the white man's burden" (1899). Many colonies, moreover, never earned much. The political pressure for prestige often went further than the economic logic.

Concept

The economic theory of imperialism. J.A. Hobson, a British liberal, argued in 1902 that low wages at home limited demand, so surplus capital sought profit overseas and a small group of investors pushed the state into empire. Lenin (1916) turned this into a Marxist theory: monopoly and finance capital, needing outlets, had to carve up the world, and the rivalry made World War I inevitable. Critics reply that most British capital went to the USA and white settler colonies and not to Africa. Gallagher and Robinson (1953) stressed "informal empire" and local crises, which pulled Europeans in.

Africa. King Leopold II of Belgium's personal claim to the Congo, French and British moves in Egypt and West Africa, and German entry led Bismarck to call the Berlin Conference (1884–85). No African ruler attended. It set the rules for claiming territory ("effective occupation"), and lines were drawn with little regard to peoples. Ethiopia defeated Italy at Adwa in 1896, the great exception. In Leopold's Congo, forced rubber collection brought horrifying mortality.

Rivalry and results. Clashes at Fashoda (1898) and Morocco (1905, 1911), and the Boer War (1899–1902) showed the danger. Colonies were turned into suppliers of primary goods, which is the same drain and deindustrialisation that Indian nationalists exposed (I31, I36).

W03.6 Opium and the opening of China

The 2024 question uses China as a test case. Under the Canton system (from 1757) Qing China let Europeans trade only at Canton through licensed merchants, the Cohong. Britain bought vast quantities of tea, but China wanted few British goods, and silver flowed out of Britain. The British East India Company's answer was opium, grown under its monopoly in Bengal and also taken from Malwa, and sold through private traders to smugglers in China. By the 1830s the silver flow had reversed. Chests of opium imported rose to about 40,000 a year in the late 1830s, addiction spread, and the Qing treasury lost silver.

In 1839 the commissioner Lin Zexu seized and destroyed about 20,000 chests at Canton. Britain went to war in the name of free trade. The First Opium War (1839–42) ended in the Treaty of Nanjing: Hong Kong was ceded, five ports were opened, Cohong ended, a large indemnity was paid and tariffs were fixed by treaty. Extraterritoriality followed in 1843. The Second Opium War (1856–60), with France, ended in the burning of the Summer Palace and the Convention of Peking; the opium trade was now effectively legal, and more ports and rights were given up. Other powers then took the same rights through "most-favoured-nation" clauses.

The consequences were lasting. China lost tariff and legal autonomy, and the indemnities and the Taiping Rebellion (1850–64) weakened the Qing. China later lost more to Japan (1895) and to a scramble for "spheres of influence", and the Chinese remember it as the start of a "century of humiliation". For India too it mattered: opium was a major source of British Indian revenue in the 19th century, so Indian peasants, Chinese consumers and British tea drinkers were tied into one imperial triangle.

10 marks · 100 words

"Industrial Revolution was not only a technological revolution, but a socio-economic revolution also, that changed the way people lived afterwards." Discuss.

How to frame it: Technology: machines, steam, factory → Economic: wage labour, capital → Social: cities and classes → Women, children, reform → New ideas → Conclusion

Model answer (104 words)

Machines, coal and steam changed production, but the deeper change was in society. Work moved from homes to clock-driven factories, and artisans became wage-earners without tools. Britain became urban by 1851, with crowded slums and cholera. An industrial middle class and a working class emerged, and they struggled through the 1832 Reform Act, Chartism and trade unions. Women and children worked long hours until the Factory Act of 1833 and later laws. New ideas followed: free-market liberalism and Marxian socialism. Economically, world trade was reorganised around industry and colonies. So the machine was only the start: it remade how people lived, worked and thought.

5 marks · 50 words

Throw light on the gradual development of the inventions leading to revolutionary changes in the textile industry during the Industrial Revolution.

How to frame it: Sequence with bottleneck logic → Steam and factory

Model answer (50 words)

Each invention answered a bottleneck. Kay's flying shuttle (1733) speeded weaving, causing a thread shortage. Hargreaves's jenny (c. 1764), Arkwright's water frame (1769) and Crompton's mule (1779) then speeded spinning. Cartwright's power loom (1785) mechanised weaving, Whitney's gin (1793) supplied cotton, and Watt's steam engine made factories independent of rivers.

10 marks · 100 words

European industrial revolution finally gave rise to New Imperialism. Explain.

How to frame it: Define New Imperialism → Raw materials and markets → Surplus capital, Hobson and Lenin → Technology → Rivalry, Berlin 1884–85 → Balance: non-economic motives

Model answer (96 words)

New Imperialism (c. 1870–1914) meant the rapid, direct conquest of Africa and Asia. Industrial growth drove it. Factories needed cotton, rubber and tin; output outran home demand, and tariffs after 1873 closed rival markets; surplus capital sought profit abroad, as Hobson and Lenin argued. Steamships, telegraphs, quinine and machine guns made conquest cheap. The rise of Germany and the USA added competition, which the Berlin Conference (1884–85) tried to regulate. Nationalism, strategy and racist "civilising" ideas added force, and many colonies paid little. Industry was thus the main engine of imperialism, though not the only one.

10 marks · 100 words

Opium played a major role in the imperialistic exploitation of China. Elucidate.

How to frame it: Canton system and tea deficit → Opium from India reverses silver → Lin Zexu and First War → Treaties and their terms → Consequences → Conclusion

Model answer (99 words)

Britain bought Chinese tea but had little to sell, and silver drained away under the restrictive Canton system. The East India Company's Bengal opium, sold through smugglers, reversed this flow, and spread addiction. When Lin Zexu destroyed opium in 1839, Britain fought the First Opium War. The Treaty of Nanjing (1842) ceded Hong Kong, opened five ports and imposed fixed tariffs and an indemnity. The Second Opium War (1856–60) gave wider rights and effectively legalised the trade. China lost tariff and legal autonomy, and the Qing were weakened. Opium thus funded the empire and opened China to foreign domination.

5 marks · 50 words

Why did the Industrial Revolution begin in Britain?

How to frame it: Agriculture, capital → Coal, iron, transport → Markets and politics → High wages, cheap coal

Model answer (51 words)

Britain combined advantages: enclosures raised farm output, colonial trade built capital, and coal and iron lay near water and canals. Empire and tariffs gave markets. Parliament protected property and patents. High wages and cheap coal made labour-saving machines profitable, as R.C. Allen argues, which was not so in India or China.

The big picture

In 1763 Britain stood at the height of its power in North America, having just driven France out. Twenty years later it had lost the thirteen colonies. The break began as a dispute over taxes and grew into a dispute over who had the right to govern. The colonists had long run their own assemblies and their own affairs. When Britain tried to make them pay for the empire, they replied in the language of English liberties and Enlightenment natural rights. With French help they won, and then did something new: they wrote a constitution that set up a federal republic, with power divided and limited. It was a model watched closely in France, and much later by colonised peoples, though its promise of equality did not reach slaves, women or Native Americans.

Core notes

W04.1 The colonies before the crisis

By 1763 thirteen British colonies ran along the Atlantic coast, with about two million people, a large share of them enslaved Africans in the South. They differed: small farms and shipping in New England, grain in the middle colonies, and tobacco and rice plantations in the South. What they shared was experience of self-rule. Each had an elected assembly that controlled local taxes. Britain's government followed "salutary neglect", and enforced its trade laws only loosely.

Concept

Mercantilism and the Navigation Acts. Mercantilism held that national wealth lay in a favourable trade balance, and colonies existed to supply raw materials and buy the mother country's goods. Under the Navigation Acts (from 1651), colonial trade had to use British ships, and goods such as tobacco could go only to Britain. In practice colonial merchants smuggled, and colonies prospered, so there was little strain. It became a grievance only when Britain began to enforce it strictly after 1763. Adam Smith attacked this system in 1776 (W02).

W04.2 From taxes to rebellion, 1763–1775

The Seven Years War removed the French threat, so colonists needed British protection less. It also doubled Britain's debt, so London wanted the colonies to pay part of the cost of defending them. The change was sudden, and it came with a ban on settling beyond the Appalachians (the Proclamation of 1763), which angered land-hungry colonists.

W04.3 The ideological background

This section is the RAS 2021 question. The colonists did not begin as revolutionaries. They first argued as Englishmen claiming ancient rights, then moved to universal natural rights as their disagreement deepened. Five streams fed this.

  • English constitutional tradition. Magna Carta, the Petition of Right and the Bill of Rights of 1689 made taxation by consent and trial by jury an inheritance they could claim.
  • John Locke. Humans have natural rights to life, liberty and property; government rests on consent; a people may overthrow a government that breaks its trust. This was the Declaration's backbone.
  • Montesquieu. Separation of powers, which later shaped the Constitution.
  • Republicanism. Classical and English "Country" writers taught that power corrupts and that virtuous citizens must be alert to tyranny, so that a standing army and a tax were read as signs of a plot.
  • Religious dissent. Many colonists were Puritans or dissenters who rejected hierarchy, and the Great Awakening of the 1730s–40s taught individuals to question authority.

Thomas Paine's Common Sense (January 1776) turned these ideas into popular politics. It attacked monarchy itself and called the idea of an island ruling a continent absurd, and it sold perhaps 100,000 copies within months. Six months later came the Declaration of Independence, drafted mainly by Thomas Jefferson.

Concept

The Declaration of Independence. Its second paragraph states that "all men are created equal" and have unalienable rights, among them "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness". Its purpose was practical: to justify secession to the world and to win foreign help. It reasoned from natural rights to a right of revolution, and then listed the King's offences. Its significance lay in stating a universal claim, which later reformers, from abolitionists to anti-colonial movements, could turn against those who had written it. Jefferson himself held slaves, and a passage attacking the slave trade was struck from his draft.

W04.4 Why the colonists won

Britain had the stronger navy and regular army and hired German troops. George Washington's Continental Army often lost battles but survived. The colonists fought at home, on a huge area with no one capital whose fall would decide things. British supply lines crossed the Atlantic. Britain expected many Loyalists, perhaps a fifth of the people, but never controlled the countryside.

The turning point was Saratoga (1777), whose American victory convinced France to sign an alliance in 1778 in revenge for 1763. Spain and the Netherlands followed against Britain. French money, troops under Rochambeau and the fleet of de Grasse trapped Cornwallis at Yorktown (1781). In the Treaty of Paris (1783) Britain recognised independence, with the boundary west to the Mississippi. It is a useful side note that Cornwallis then went to India as Governor-General (I30).

W04.5 The new political system

The first government under the Articles of Confederation was weak by design: there was no executive, and Congress could not tax. Debts and unrest such as Shays' Rebellion (1786–87) worried leaders. So fifty-five delegates met at Philadelphia in 1787, with Washington presiding and James Madison as chief planner. They solved two problems: how to give strength without tyranny, and how to balance large and small states.

W04.6 Significance and limits

America offered the first modern example of a colony breaking from an empire and founding a large republic with a written constitution. It gave the world federalism in working form. France was affected at once: French officers such as Lafayette came home with new ideas, and the cost of the war deepened the French debt that brought on 1789 (W05). Latin American leaders such as Bolívar drew on it. For Britain, the loss shifted attention towards India and the east.

Its limits are as important. Around half a million slaves stayed enslaved, and the Constitution counted a slave as three-fifths of a person when apportioning seats. Slavery ended only with the Civil War and the 13th Amendment of 1865. Women could not vote, as Abigail Adams's letter of 1776 ("remember the ladies") anticipated. Native Americans lost the British limit on settlement, and they were pushed west. Property rules limited the vote too.

Debate

How revolutionary was it? Charles Beard (1913) argued that the Constitution served the economic interests of the wealthy founders. Bernard Bailyn (1967) stressed the power of ideas, and the colonists' sincere fear of a conspiracy against liberty. Gordon Wood argued that the Revolution radically changed society by replacing hierarchy with equality among white men. Historians such as Gary Nash and Woody Holton add the role of artisans, slaves and Native Americans.

The big picture

In 1789 France was the most populous state of Western Europe, rich in culture and yet nearly bankrupt. Its legal order divided people into three estates, and the one that paid most had least voice. When the King called the old assembly to solve the debt, the commoners' deputies declared themselves the nation, and Paris rose in support. Within four years France had ended feudalism, declared human rights, executed its King, founded a republic and gone through the Terror. The revolution then swung back to conservatism in the Thermidorian Reaction, and an army general, Napoleon, took power. He spread its legal gains across Europe while destroying its political freedoms. After his defeat in 1815 the old monarchs returned, but the ideas of citizenship, equality before the law and nationalism could no longer be removed.

Core notes

W05.1 The Old Regime and why it broke

Under the Ancien Régime the population of some 26 million was legally divided. The First Estate (clergy) and Second Estate (nobles) were under 3 percent of the people, held much of the land and were largely free from main direct taxes. The Third Estate included everyone else: wealthy lawyers and merchants, artisans and the peasants who formed the great majority. It paid the taille (direct tax) and the gabelle (salt tax), tithes to the Church and dues to lords.

The causes of the revolution should be sorted into four levels.

  • Fiscal crisis. The immediate cause. Wars, especially the American war, doubled debt, and by 1788 about half of state spending went on interest. Privileged groups blocked tax reform by Turgot, Necker and Calonne.
  • Social tension. The bourgeoisie had wealth and education but were closed out of top offices by birth. Peasants carried feudal dues.
  • Ideas. Montesquieu, Voltaire and Rousseau (W02) gave critics a language, and the American war gave them an example.
  • Economic trigger. A bad harvest in 1788 raised bread prices to a level that consumed most of a labourer's wage by early 1789.

Louis XVI was not a tyrant but an indecisive ruler, which proved dangerous when firm compromise was needed. Abbé Sieyès stated the case in January 1789: "What is the Third Estate? Everything."

W05.2 The moderate phase, 1789–1792

The Estates-General met on 5 May 1789, for the first time since 1614. The fight at once was over voting: by order, which gave privileged estates two votes to one, or by head, which favoured the doubled Third Estate. On 17 June the commoners declared themselves the National Assembly, and when locked out they swore the Tennis Court Oath not to part until France had a constitution.

Troop movements frightened Paris. On 14 July crowds took the Bastille for gunpowder. It was a prison of little practical importance but a strong symbol. In the countryside the "Great Fear" drove peasants to burn feudal records, and so on 4 August the Assembly abolished feudal privileges. Then came the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (26 August), and in October Parisian women marched to Versailles and brought the royal family back to Paris.

Concept

The Rights of Man. It stated that men are born free and equal in rights; that sovereignty lies in the nation; that law is the expression of the general will; and that liberty, property, security and resistance to oppression are natural rights. It arose to replace a society of privilege with one of citizens, drawing on Rousseau and America. It was universal in form but limited in practice: the Constitution of 1791 gave the vote only to "active" male taxpayers. Olympe de Gouges replied in 1791 with a Declaration of the Rights of Woman, and slaves in Saint-Domingue rose the same year, and they won abolition by 1794.

The Assembly reorganised France. Church lands were sold, backed by paper money (assignats). The Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790) made priests state employees, which split the country. France became a limited monarchy under the Constitution of 1791. The King's flight to Varennes that June destroyed trust in him.

W05.3 The radical phase and the Terror, 1792–1794

War transformed the Revolution. France went to war with Austria and Prussia in April 1792, partly because both factions hoped to gain by it. Defeats and fear of treason drove the Paris crowd, the sans-culottes, to storm the Tuileries in August. A new Convention, elected by adult male suffrage, abolished the monarchy and declared the Republic in September 1792. Louis XVI was guillotined in January 1793.

Two factions struggled: the Girondins, provincial and more moderate, and the Jacobins (the Mountain), led by Robespierre, Danton and Marat, with Paris support. With foreign invasion, civil war in the Vendée and high prices, the Jacobins expelled the Girondins in June 1793. The Committee of Public Safety then ran what was in effect an emergency dictatorship. It raised a mass army through the levée en masse, fixed prices through the Maximum and used the Revolutionary Tribunal against suspects. In about a year perhaps 17,000 were officially executed, and many more died in prison or in the Vendée.

Debate

Why the Terror? The circumstances thesis says war and civil war forced emergency rule, and indeed France's armies did recover. Critics such as François Furet note that the Terror worsened after the military crisis had eased in 1794, and they blame an ideology that treated dissent as treason.

W05.4 Thermidor and the Directory, 1794–1799

Concept

The Thermidorian Reaction. On 9 Thermidor Year II (27 July 1794) the Convention turned against Robespierre, who was guillotined the next day. It arose because victory at Fleurus made emergency rule seem needless, the Terror threatened the deputies themselves, and the sans-culottes no longer supported him. The term "reaction" refers to what followed: Jacobin clubs closed, price controls ended, and the wealthy and moderates regained influence. The new Constitution of 1795 returned the vote to property and set up a five-member Directory. "Thermidor" has become a general word for the conservative phase that follows a radical revolution.

The Directory (1795–99) was weak and corrupt, and it was attacked from the right by royalists and from the left by radicals such as Babeuf, whose Conspiracy of Equals (1796) is a forerunner of communism. Dependent on the army to survive, it used generals against its opponents. One of them, the young Napoleon Bonaparte, had won fame in Italy.

W05.5 Napoleon: child and destroyer of the Revolution

With the coup of 18 Brumaire (November 1799) Napoleon became First Consul, and in 1804 Emperor, crowning himself in Notre-Dame. People accepted him because he offered order, secured the land gains of the Revolution and brought military glory.

He kept parts of the Revolution. The Napoleonic Code (1804) gave equality before the law, protected property, ended feudalism and opened careers to talent, and it influenced law widely. The Bank of France (1800), the centralised prefects, and the lycées built a lasting state. The Concordat of 1801 made peace with the Church. He destroyed other parts. Political freedom and press freedom vanished. Slavery was restored in the colonies in 1802, and the Code reduced women's and children's rights under the father's authority.

His armies carried the Code to Germany, Italy and Spain. Victories at Austerlitz (1805) and Jena (1806) gave him control of the continent. Britain, safe after Trafalgar (1805), could not be invaded, so he tried economic war through the Continental System (1806). Enforcing it drew him into Spain (1808) and Russia (1812), where the army was destroyed. Defeated at Leipzig (1813) he was exiled to Elba, and after a brief return he lost at Waterloo in June 1815 and was sent to St Helena.

He also touched India: his Egyptian campaign (1798) and alliance hopes with Tipu Sultan alarmed Britain, and Wellesley's actions in India (I28) must be read with this in mind.

W05.6 Impact and legacy

The Congress of Vienna (1814–15), led by Metternich, tried to restore the old order through legitimacy and a balance of power. But the changes could not be undone. Feudalism and legal privileges were abolished in France and weakened in the lands that Napoleon had ruled. Modern nationalism was born: a nation of citizens, a mass army and a national anthem. Napoleon's conquests provoked national feeling in Germany and Spain, and unification came to Italy (1861) and Germany (1871). Liberalism and socialism grew out of its unresolved questions about political and social equality; Babeuf and the sans-culottes were remembered, and Marx studied them. The words Left and Right themselves come from the seating of the Assembly. Revolutions followed in 1830 and 1848.

In India, Tipu Sultan was reported to have planted a tree of liberty at Seringapatam, and Rammohun Roy admired the ideals of 1789 and 1830. Later nationalists drew on French language of liberty.

Debate

Whose revolution? The Marxist view (Georges Lefebvre, Albert Soboul) sees a bourgeois revolution that overthrew feudalism and opened the way for capitalism. Revisionists (Alfred Cobban, François Furet) reply that the bourgeoisie and nobility overlapped, that capitalism did not advance much, and that the true change was in political culture. Lynn Hunt studies its symbols.

The big picture

Early 20th-century Russia combined a medieval political system with fast modern industry. The Tsar held absolute power, most people were poor peasants hungry for land, and new factories in a few cities had produced a small but concentrated working class. Defeat in war exposed this mismatch twice: in 1905 and, decisively, in World War I. In February 1917 strikes and a mutiny brought down the monarchy in a matter of days. A weak Provisional Government kept on fighting the war, and that was its downfall. In October the Bolsheviks, led by Lenin, seized power with the promise of "Peace, Land and Bread". They then won a civil war and created the world's first state claiming to be socialist. For the rest of the century that state offered an alternative to capitalism, which is why the Cold War (W10) cannot be understood without this unit.

Core notes

W06.1 The old Russia and its contradictions

Tsar Nicholas II (1894–1917) ruled as an autocrat, with no parliament until 1906, through a bureaucracy, a secret police (the Okhrana) and the Orthodox Church. Non-Russian peoples, such as Poles and Finns, suffered enforced Russification, and Jews faced pogroms.

The Emancipation of 1861 freed about 23 million serfs, but they had to pay redemption charges for land, which they held through the village commune (mir). So they remained poor and land-hungry. Then, from the 1890s under Finance Minister Sergei Witte, foreign loans and state support built railways, including the Trans-Siberian, and huge factories. Workers were few in number, perhaps 3 million in factory work by 1914, but they were packed in Petrograd and Moscow into big plants, with long hours and low pay. These two groups, peasants who wanted land and workers who wanted a better life, gave revolution its force. The intelligentsia gave it its ideas.

W06.2 Revolutionary ideas: from Marx to Lenin

The first Russian radicals, the Narodniks ("Populists"), placed their hopes in the peasants. When villagers ignored them, some turned to terror, and a bomb killed Alexander II in 1881. Their heirs were the Socialist Revolutionaries (SRs), the largest party among peasants. Marxists looked instead to industrial workers.

Concept

Marxism and Leninism. Marx taught that history moves through class struggle. Capitalism creates a proletariat that will overthrow it, leading to socialism and in time a classless communism. By this theory revolution should come first in advanced countries, not in backward Russia. Lenin adapted it. In What Is to Be Done? (1902) he argued that workers alone reach only trade-union demands, so they need a disciplined party of professional revolutionaries as their vanguard. In Imperialism (1916, see W03) he argued that capitalism might break at its weakest link. The result was a theory fitted to Russia, but one with an authoritarian party at its core.

The party split in 1903. Bolsheviks ("majority") under Lenin wanted a small, disciplined party, while Mensheviks ("minority") under Martov wanted a broader one, and they expected a long bourgeois stage before socialism.

The Revolution of 1905 was the rehearsal. Defeat by Japan humiliated the regime. On Bloody Sunday (January) troops fired on a peaceful crowd led by Father Gapon, bringing a petition to the Winter Palace. Strikes spread, sailors of the Potemkin mutinied, and workers formed the first soviets (councils). The October Manifesto split the opposition by promising a parliament, the Duma, but the Tsar soon cut its powers. Stolypin combined repression with reforms that let richer peasants leave the commune, in a "wager on the strong". He was assassinated in 1911.

W06.3 War and the February Revolution

World War I broke the regime. By 1917 Russia had suffered millions of casualties. Railways could not bring bread to cities, and inflation ate wages. In 1915 Nicholas took personal command at the front, so he got the blame for defeat, while the Tsarina and her adviser Rasputin discredited the court at home.

In February 1917 (March by our calendar) women textile workers in Petrograd struck on International Women's Day, demanding bread. Hundreds of thousands joined, and within days the garrison mutinied rather than fire. No party led this. Nicholas abdicated, ending three centuries of Romanov rule.

Concept

Dual power. Two authorities emerged after February. The Provisional Government, formed from Duma liberals under Prince Lvov and later Kerensky, held formal authority but no armed force. The Petrograd Soviet of workers and soldiers controlled the troops, railways and telegraph through Order No. 1. The moderate socialists who led the Soviet believed that this was the bourgeois stage, so they did not take power. This division made government difficult. The government postponed land reform and elections and continued the war, which lost it support among peasants and soldiers. The Bolsheviks, who alone rejected it, benefited when it failed.

W06.4 October 1917

Germany allowed Lenin to return through its territory in a sealed train, hoping he would take Russia out of the war. His April Theses startled his own party: no support for the Provisional Government, all power to the soviets, peace, land to the peasants and control of banks. His slogan was "Peace, Land and Bread". A failed rising in the July Days forced him to flee to Finland. Then the failed march of General Kornilov on Petrograd in August made Kerensky arm the Bolsheviks to defend the city. By September they held majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow soviets, with Trotsky at the head of the Petrograd Soviet.

On 25 October (7 November) the Military Revolutionary Committee took bridges, stations and the Winter Palace with little bloodshed, and it handed power to the Congress of Soviets. The new Council of People's Commissars issued the Decree on Peace and the Decree on Land, which recognised the peasants' seizure of estates. Banks and factories were later nationalised.

The dictatorship began quickly. In elections for the Constituent Assembly, SRs won most seats and the Bolsheviks about a quarter, so the Bolsheviks closed the Assembly by force in January 1918. The Cheka, a political police, was established in December 1917.

W06.5 Civil war, NEP and the USSR

Lenin accepted the severe Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (March 1918), which gave up Ukraine, Poland and the Baltic lands, to end the war at any price. Then the Civil War (1918–21) began: Reds against an assortment of Whites (Tsarist generals such as Kolchak and Denikin), backed by foreign forces. Trotsky's Red Army won, since the Reds held the central railways and industry, the Whites were divided, and peasants feared the return of landlords. The war killed millions, more by famine and disease than by battle.

War Communism was the emergency economy of that time: grain was seized, and industry was nationalised and militarised. Output collapsed, famine followed in 1921, and in March the sailors of Kronstadt, once the party's firmest supporters, rebelled for free soviets. Lenin crushed them but changed course.

Concept

The New Economic Policy (NEP), 1921. The NEP replaced grain seizures with a tax in kind, so peasants could sell their surplus, and it allowed small private trade and firms, while the state kept the "commanding heights": big industry, banks and foreign trade. It arose because War Communism had destroyed the economy and peasants' goodwill. Production recovered to pre-war levels by about 1926–27. Some Bolsheviks saw it as a betrayal. After Lenin's death, Stalin defeated Trotsky, and replaced NEP with the Five-Year Plans (from 1928) and forced collectivisation of farming, at the cost of millions of lives, including the famine of 1932–33.

The USSR was formed in December 1922 as a federation, and in practice the Communist Party controlled everything.

W06.6 Global impact

The Revolution divided the world's politics into capitalism and communism. The Comintern (1919) founded communist parties worldwide. Fear of communism helped fascists gain support (W08), and that rivalry became the Cold War after 1945 (W10). In the West, it encouraged welfare reforms to keep workers loyal. In the colonies, Lenin's support for national liberation and its promise of rapid development were attractive: China's revolution owed much to this model.

In India, the example inspired socialist and labour politics. M.N. Roy helped found the Communist Party of India at Tashkent in 1920, and the AITUC was founded that year as well (I40). The Rowlatt Committee and British officials feared "Bolshevism". Jawaharlal Nehru visited Moscow in 1927 and was impressed by the Soviet plan, and Tagore wrote about his 1930 visit (Letters from Russia). Bhagat Singh studied Lenin (I38). India's Planning Commission and Five-Year Plans owe something to it (I45).

Debate

Popular revolution or coup? The Soviet view saw a workers' revolution led by the party. Richard Pipes calls October a coup by a small minority that had no popular basis. Social historians such as Alexander Rabinowitch and Sheila Fitzpatrick show that the Bolsheviks really were popular in 1917 among workers and soldiers, who were moving to the left on their own. Orlando Figes stresses the tragedy of a people who were not free to shape the result. Did Lenin lead to Stalin? Historians still disagree on this.

The big picture

In June 1914 the murder of an Austrian archduke in Bosnia turned within six weeks into a war among all the major powers of Europe. Forty years of alliances, arms races and colonial rivalry had made the continent ready to burst, and mobilisation plans gave leaders little time to think. Most expected a short war. It lasted four years, killed about nine to ten million soldiers, and it destroyed four empires: German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian and Ottoman. The peace of 1919 tried to create a new order through the League of Nations and national self-determination, but its treatment of Germany left bitter resentment, and the League failed in its main duty. So this war led in many ways to the next one, and it also weakened European empires, helped the Russian Revolution, and drew India and other colonies into world politics.

Core notes

W07.1 Long-term causes

The easiest way to order the causes is in four groups, often remembered as MAIN: militarism, alliances, imperialism and nationalism.

Alliances. Bismarck's Germany isolated France after 1871 by the Triple Alliance. After he fell in 1890, Germany let its Russian link lapse, so France and Russia allied in 1894. Britain, alarmed by German naval building, settled its colonial quarrels with France (1904) and Russia (1907). So Europe was split into two armed camps, and a local conflict could spread.

Militarism. Generals gained influence. Germany's naval programme under Tirpitz, from 1898, set off a race with Britain, with Dreadnought battleships from 1906. Large conscript armies had rail-based mobilisation plans; Germany's Schlieffen Plan required striking France through Belgium before Russia could be ready, so once mobilisation began, time for diplomacy ended.

Imperialism. Germany was late for colonies and wanted its "place in the sun". Morocco in 1905 and 1911 brought Britain and France closer against it (W03).

Nationalism. France wanted Alsace-Lorraine back. In the Balkans, the decline of the Ottoman Empire left a dangerous gap: Serbian nationalism threatened Austria-Hungary, whose empire contained many Slavs, while Russia backed the Slavs. Austria annexed Bosnia in 1908, and the Balkan Wars (1912–13) doubled Serbia's size.

W07.2 The July crisis and the course of the war

On 28 June 1914 Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb linked to the Black Hand group, shot Archduke Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo. Germany gave Austria its "blank cheque". Austria's ultimatum to Serbia led to war on 28 July, Russia mobilised, and Germany declared war on Russia and France. Its invasion of Belgium brought in Britain on 4 August. Italy stayed out, then joined the Allies in 1915 with the promise of land by the Treaty of London. Turkey and Bulgaria joined Germany.

The German advance stopped at the Marne (September 1914). The Western Front then settled into trench warfare, in which machine guns and artillery gave the advantage to defenders. Verdun and the Somme (1916) cost hundreds of thousands of lives for little ground. Poison gas, tanks and aircraft were introduced. Civilians were drawn in, in "total war": rationing, conscription and propaganda, along with a British naval blockade of Germany, and German U-boats that sank the Lusitania in 1915.

Two events of 1917 changed the war. Unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram brought the USA in, while the Russian Revolution led to Russia's exit (W06). Germany's spring offensive of 1918 failed, its allies collapsed, sailors mutinied at Kiel, the Kaiser fled and an armistice came on 11 November 1918.

W07.3 The peace of 1919

The victors met at Paris from January 1919, without Germany or Russia. Their aims clashed. Woodrow Wilson had set out his Fourteen Points (January 1918), calling for open diplomacy, self-determination and a League. Clemenceau of France wanted security and repayment. Lloyd George of Britain, who had just won an election on anti-German feeling, wanted a compromise, and Orlando of Italy wanted territory.

Debate

Was Versailles too harsh? Keynes, in The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919), called it a "Carthaginian peace" that would ruin Europe. Étienne Mantoux and later historians such as Margaret MacMillan reply that Germany could pay had it wished, and that Hitler's rise owed more to the Depression. A fair answer: it was too harsh to reconcile Germany, but too weak to hold it down. Germans called it a Diktat, and that sense of injustice was what Hitler used.

W07.4 The League of Nations

Concept

Collective security. Before 1914, peace was thought to depend on a balance of power and alliances. The war seemed to show that this system failed. The League, founded on Wilson's idea, offered collective security: an attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all, under Article 16 of the Covenant, with economic sanctions and possible force. It began work in January 1920 at Geneva, with an Assembly, a Council, a Secretariat, and the Permanent Court of International Justice at The Hague. Its weakness was that it depended on the will of its members.

The League did real work in smaller matters. It settled the Aaland Islands dispute (1921), helped refugees, ran mandates, and worked on health. The International Labour Organisation survives to this day. India was a founding member, though not self-governing.

Its failures were decisive. The USA never joined, because the Senate rejected the treaty. Germany and the USSR were absent for long periods. It needed unanimity and had no army. When Japan took Manchuria (1931) it condemned Japan, and Japan simply left. When Italy invaded Abyssinia (1935) sanctions excluded oil, and Britain and France preferred to keep Mussolini friendly. Germany left in 1933, and the League did nothing to stop Hitler's breach of Versailles. It formally ended in 1946, but its work passed to the United Nations (W09).

W07.5 Impact of the war

Human and political. Roughly nine to ten million soldiers died, with millions of civilians, including from the influenza of 1918–19. Four empires fell. Democratic republics appeared but proved fragile, and veterans' anger fed fascism (W08).

Economic. Europe lost its financial lead, and the USA became the world's creditor. Debts, reparations and inflation made a fragile system that led to the crash of 1929.

Women. Men's departure took women into munitions, transport and offices, in Britain's case about a million in munitions alone. Their work helped to win the vote: Britain in 1918 (women over 30), and also Germany, Russia and the USA (1920). But many lost jobs when men returned. The World War II half and the complete answer are in W09.

Colonies. Colonial soldiers came home with changed ideas, and Wilson's self-determination raised hopes the victors did not keep. Egypt, Iraq and China saw protests in 1919.

India. Over a million Indians served, in France, Mesopotamia and East Africa. The war brought high prices, higher taxes and forced recruitment. Tilak's and Annie Besant's Home Rule Leagues and the Lucknow Pact (1916) followed, as did the Montagu Declaration (1917). But then came the Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh in 1919, and Ottoman defeat triggered the Khilafat Movement. These led to Gandhi's mass politics (I39). Indian industry also grew, since imports fell (I31).

Debate

Who caused the war? The Treaty blamed Germany. Fritz Fischer (1961) revived this with German documents, arguing it sought world power. Lenin blamed imperialist capitalism. A.J.P. Taylor pointed to railway timetables. Christopher Clark, The Sleepwalkers (2012), sees shared responsibility among leaders who did not understand what they were doing.

The big picture

After 1918, many expected democracy to spread. Within twenty years it had collapsed across much of Europe. Italy went first: disappointed by the peace and frightened by strikes, its elites let Mussolini take office in 1922. Germany held out longer, through a difficult Weimar Republic. Then the Great Depression, which started in the United States in 1929, destroyed the German economy, and in 1933 Hitler came to power legally. Both regimes attacked liberal democracy and socialism, worshipped the leader and the nation, and glorified war. Nazism added a racial doctrine that ended in the murder of about six million Jews. Their aggression, which the League could not stop, led directly to World War II (W09). To explain them, you need three things together: war's aftermath, economic collapse and the choices made by conservative elites.

Core notes

W08.1 The Great Depression

The US economy boomed in the 1920s, but its gains were unevenly shared, so consumer demand and farm incomes were weak. Share buying on credit pushed prices far above real value. Prices began to fall in October 1929, and the Wall Street crash ("Black Thursday" 24 October, "Black Tuesday" 29 October) destroyed savings and confidence. Banks failed in thousands, and American industrial output fell by nearly half by 1932. Unemployment reached about a quarter of workers.

The crisis spread because the world economy had become dependent on America. Since the 1920s, US loans had let Germany pay reparations to Britain and France, which then repaid their war debts to the USA. When loans were recalled, this circle broke. Governments then made matters worse. The US Smoot-Hawley Tariff (1930) brought retaliation and world trade shrank. Many countries held to the gold standard and cut spending. By 1932 Germany had about six million unemployed.

Responses differed. Roosevelt's New Deal (from 1933) brought relief, public works and bank regulation, and Social Security (1935). Keynes, in The General Theory (1936), argued that governments should spend in a slump to raise demand. Britain left gold in 1931 and set up imperial preference. Germany, Italy and Japan chose rearmament and expansion. The USSR, with its Five-Year Plan, seemed to many to escape the crisis (W06).

India suffered badly. Agricultural prices collapsed by about half between 1928 and 1934. Exports fell and peasants' debts grew, while the British kept taxes high and took India's gold. This rural distress fed the Civil Disobedience Movement of 1930 (I41).

W08.2 Italy: the first fascist state

Italy had joined the winning side in 1915 for territory, but got less than it wanted at Paris. Nationalists such as D'Annunzio spoke of a "mutilated victory". Debt, unemployment and inflation followed. The "Two Red Years" of 1919–20 saw factory occupations and peasant land seizures, which frightened landlords and industrialists. Parliament was unstable.

Benito Mussolini, a former socialist editor, founded the Fasci di Combattimento at Milan in 1919. His blackshirt squads attacked socialists with the tolerance of the police, and they won middle-class gratitude. After the March on Rome (October 1922) King Victor Emmanuel III, fearing civil war, appointed him Prime Minister. It was not a seizure by force; the elite let him in.

The dictatorship was built gradually. The Acerbo Law (1923) gave the largest party two-thirds of the seats. After the murder of the socialist deputy Matteotti (1924) Mussolini took full responsibility in January 1925, and he banned opposition by 1926. As Il Duce he built a cult of leadership. The Lateran Treaty (1929) reconciled the state with the Papacy and created Vatican City. Abroad he invaded Abyssinia (1935–36) and sent troops to help Franco in Spain.

Concept

The corporate state. Fascists claimed to have a third way between capitalism and socialism. Workers and employers in each industry were grouped into corporations under state supervision, so class struggle would be replaced by national cooperation. Strikes were banned, and independent unions were abolished. In practice, it left big business alone and took power from workers, and it did not save Italy from the Depression. Fascism as a whole is usually defined by extreme nationalism, rejection of liberal and Marxist ideas, a single party and leader, and the glorification of violence. "Everything in the State, nothing outside the State" was Mussolini's formula.

W08.3 Germany: from Weimar to Hitler

The Weimar Republic was born in defeat in 1919, and it was blamed for signing Versailles. Veterans and nationalists repeated the "stab in the back" myth. Its constitution was very democratic, but proportional representation produced unstable coalitions, while Article 48 allowed the president to rule by decree. The judges and army were loyal to the old order. In 1923 France occupied the Ruhr over unpaid reparations, and hyperinflation wiped out middle-class savings. The Dawes Plan and US loans brought recovery from 1924, and Stresemann's diplomacy took Germany into the League in 1926.

Adolf Hitler, an Austrian-born veteran, led the Nazi Party (NSDAP). The Munich putsch of November 1923 failed, and in prison he wrote Mein Kampf and turned to winning power legally. The party was tiny in the good years, with under 3 percent of the vote in 1928. Then the Depression transformed it: 18 percent in 1930, and about 37 percent in July 1932. Unemployed workers turned to the Communists, but farmers, small businessmen and the young turned to Hitler. Goebbels's propaganda presented him as a saviour, and the SA fought in the streets. Fear of Communism led the elite to look on him as useful. Von Papen and others persuaded President Hindenburg to appoint him Chancellor on 30 January 1933, believing that they could control him.

Power came quickly. The Reichstag fire (February) gave an excuse to suspend rights, and the Enabling Act (March 1933) let Hitler rule without parliament. Parties and unions were banned. In the Night of the Long Knives (June 1934), he had the SA leadership killed to win the army's approval. When Hindenburg died in August, he combined the offices as Führer.

W08.4 Nazi rule and racial policy

Concept

Nazi racial ideology. Hitler saw history as a struggle among races, in which "Aryans" were supreme, and Jews were portrayed as a poison behind both capitalism and communism. It combined Social Darwinism with older anti-Semitism. The state was to purify the Volk and to win Lebensraum ("living space") in the east. Policy went by stages: boycott (1933), exclusion from citizenship in the Nuremberg Laws (1935), violence on Kristallnacht (November 1938), then ghettos, mass shooting after the invasion of the USSR (1941), and the "Final Solution" in death camps such as Auschwitz, planned at the Wannsee Conference (January 1942). About six million Jews were murdered, along with Roma, disabled people, and Soviet prisoners.

The regime ruled by terror and consent together. The SS under Himmler, the Gestapo and camps from Dachau (1933) crushed opponents. The Hitler Youth and schools shaped children, and Goebbels controlled radio, film and press. Women were told to keep to "children, kitchen, church", with loans for marriage. Public works, rearmament and the Autobahn ended mass unemployment by about 1936, and for many Germans this made the regime popular. Abroad, Hitler left the League (1933), began open rearmament (1935), reoccupied the Rhineland (1936), and with Italy formed the Rome-Berlin Axis. Each step tore up part of Versailles without any response from others (W09).

The big picture

World War II was the largest and most destructive conflict in history. It grew out of three decades of unfinished business: an unsatisfied Germany, Japanese and Italian ambitions, a broken world economy and a League that could not enforce peace. Britain and France tried appeasement until Hitler invaded Poland in 1939. By 1942 the war had reached around the world, and Germany and Japan looked unbeatable, until the turning points of Midway, El Alamein and Stalingrad. By 1945 about 60 million were dead, cities lay in ruins and two atomic bombs had been dropped. What came next matters most for the exam. Europe's dominance had ended. The USA and the USSR emerged as superpowers, in a bipolar order, the United Nations and a new economic order were created, and empires began to fall. The world of the Cold War (W10) and of independent India starts here.

Core notes

W09.1 Why war came again

The roots lie in the peace of 1919 and in the crisis after 1929. Versailles left Germany resentful but still strong (W07). The Depression brought Hitler to power and pushed Japan's military towards conquest for raw materials and markets (W08). Fascist ideology itself glorified expansion. The League of Nations failed over Manchuria and Abyssinia, which taught aggressors that nothing would stop them.

Concept

Appeasement. Appeasement was the policy, followed mainly by Britain under Neville Chamberlain, of meeting Hitler's grievances by concession to avoid war. It arose from fresh memories of 1914–18, a sense that Versailles had been too harsh, weak armaments, the Depression, and a fear of communism greater than the fear of Germany. It reached its peak at the Munich Agreement (September 1938), where Britain and France let Hitler take the Sudetenland from Czechoslovakia, and Chamberlain claimed "peace for our time". Hitler took the rest of the country in March 1939, which ended the policy. Britain then guaranteed Poland. Its critics (Churchill) said it fed the aggressor, while its defenders said that it bought time to rearm.

The USSR, left out of Munich and doubtful of the West, signed the Nazi-Soviet Pact in August 1939, with a secret clause dividing Poland. So Hitler could invade without fighting on two fronts, and Germany attacked Poland on 1 September 1939.

Debate

Hitler's war? The Nuremberg view and Hugh Trevor-Roper hold that Hitler planned it. A.J.P. Taylor (The Origins of the Second World War, 1961) said that he was an opportunist exploiting the mistakes of others, a view most historians reject while accepting its warning about contingency.

W09.2 The course of the war

German success, 1939–41. Blitzkrieg ("lightning war"), joining tanks and aircraft, conquered Poland, then Denmark, Norway, the Low Countries and France by June 1940. The British army escaped at Dunkirk. Churchill's Britain held on in the Battle of Britain, where the RAF, using radar, denied Germany air control, and US Lend-Lease from March 1941 sent supplies.

The war widens, 1941. Hitler then invaded the USSR in Operation Barbarossa, seeking Lebensraum and the destruction of communism. This opened the largest front in history, on which most German casualties came. In the Pacific, Japan attacked Pearl Harbor to knock out the US fleet, and took Malaya, Singapore and Burma, reaching India's borders. Hitler declared war on the USA, which made the conflict global.

Turning points, 1942–43. Midway (June 1942) destroyed four Japanese carriers. El Alamein (late 1942) drove Rommel out of Egypt. The German surrender at Stalingrad (February 1943) was the decisive blow in Europe. Allied landings in Italy brought down Mussolini in July 1943.

Allied victory. The Normandy landings (6 June 1944) opened the second front Stalin had long demanded. Soviet armies reached Berlin in 1945. Hitler killed himself on 30 April, and Germany surrendered on 7–8 May. In Asia, the USA advanced by island-hopping, and it dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki on 6 and 9 August, with the USSR declaring war on Japan on 8 August. Japan's surrender was announced on 15 August.

The war killed about 60 million people, most of them civilians, including those murdered in the Holocaust. The USSR alone lost some 27 million.

This section is the RAS 2018 question. Use these headings.

  • End of European dominance. Britain was bankrupt, France defeated and Germany destroyed. Power moved from Western Europe to the outside.
  • A bipolar world. The USA (with its economy intact and the bomb) and the USSR (with its huge army in Eastern Europe) were the only superpowers, and they were divided by ideology. This led to the Cold War, NATO and the Warsaw Pact (W10).
  • The United Nations. Born in 1945, with a stronger structure than the League: a Security Council with five permanent members holding the veto, and an American presence. It made collective security and later peacekeeping a part of diplomacy.
  • The nuclear age. After Hiroshima, war between great powers would risk mutual destruction. The USSR tested its bomb in 1949, which gave rise to deterrence and an arms race.
  • Division of Germany and Europe. The Iron Curtain, and two German states from 1949.
  • Decolonisation. Weakened empires could not hold their colonies, and the Atlantic Charter, the Japanese defeat of European armies in Asia and US and Soviet hostility to old-style empire all worked against them.   • Communism in Asia. China in 1949, and Korea and Vietnam after it.
  • New economic order. The IMF, the World Bank and GATT (1947), with US dollar leadership and the Marshall Plan.
  • Human rights and international law. Nuremberg, the Universal Declaration and the Genocide Convention.
  • Israel and the Middle East. The Holocaust strengthened the case for a Jewish state, created in 1948.

W09.6 India and the war

The Viceroy declared India at war in September 1939 without consulting its leaders, and Congress ministries resigned in protest. The Cripps Mission (1942) failed, and Quit India followed. Subhas Bose raised the INA with Japanese help. The Bengal Famine (1943) killed about three million people. Indian industry grew as imports stopped, which RAS Mains 2021 asked about. Over two million Indians served in the armed forces, the largest volunteer army of the war. Britain's economic weakness after 1945, with the INA trials and the naval mutiny of 1946, made withdrawal unavoidable (I42). Newly independent India's foreign policy of non-alignment was a response to the bipolar world this war had created (W10).

The big picture

In 1945 the USA and the USSR were allies. Within three years they were rivals in a contest that lasted more than four decades. Each saw its own system, capitalism or communism, as the future and the other as a threat. They divided Europe by an "Iron Curtain" and built opposing alliances and nuclear arsenals. Because nuclear war would destroy both, they never fought each other directly. Instead they fought through others, in Korea, Vietnam and Afghanistan, and they competed for influence, and in space. In those same years the old empires collapsed, and about a hundred new states emerged. Many of them, led by India, Egypt and Yugoslavia, refused to take sides and created the Non-Aligned Movement. In the 1980s the Soviet economy could not keep up, Gorbachev's reforms went out of control, Eastern Europe broke free in 1989, and the USSR dissolved in December 1991.

Core notes

W10.1 How the Cold War began

The war's allies shared only an enemy. Their ideologies were opposed, and they distrusted each other from 1917 onward. In 1945 the Red Army stood in Eastern Europe, and the USSR, having been invaded twice in thirty years, wanted friendly states on its border. The USA, rich and with the bomb, wanted open markets and elections. Soviet-backed communist governments arose in Poland, Hungary, Romania and Bulgaria, despite what had been said at Yalta. To Moscow this was security; to Washington it was expansion.

George Kennan's "Long Telegram" (1946) argued that Soviet expansion must be contained. Churchill spoke of an Iron Curtain across Europe. Britain could no longer support Greece and Turkey, so the Truman Doctrine (March 1947) promised to aid free peoples resisting subversion. The Marshall Plan (June 1947) offered about 13 billion dollars for European recovery, partly to reduce the appeal of communism. Moscow forced Eastern Europe to refuse and set up the Cominform. Stalin's Berlin Blockade (1948–49) failed against an Allied airlift, and Germany was divided into two states in 1949.

Concept

Containment and deterrence. Containment was the US aim of stopping communism from spreading, without directly attacking the USSR. It arose from Kennan's analysis and was applied through aid, alliances and local wars. Its fear was the "domino effect", in which one country's fall would bring down its neighbours. Deterrence came after the Soviet test of 1949 and the hydrogen bomb: neither side would attack, if the other could retaliate and destroy it, which is called mutually assured destruction (MAD). This kept the superpowers from direct war, while making an accident the most feared danger. It also explains why the Cold War was "cold" in Europe but hot elsewhere.

W10.2 Crises and proxy wars

Korea (1950–53). North Korea, with Stalin's and Mao's agreement, invaded South Korea. The UN, with the USSR absent from the Security Council, sent forces led by the USA, and China intervened when they neared its border. It ended roughly where it began, at the 38th parallel. India chaired the Neutral Nations Repatriation Commission after the armistice.

Suez (1956). Egypt's Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal, and Britain, France and Israel attacked. The USA and the USSR both pressed them to withdraw, showing that the old powers no longer acted freely. The UN sent its first armed peacekeeping force, UNEF. That same year the USSR crushed Hungary.

Cuba (1962). After Fidel Castro's revolution (1959) and the failed US landing at the Bay of Pigs (1961), Khrushchev placed missiles in Cuba. Kennedy imposed a naval "quarantine". For thirteen days war seemed near, until the USSR withdrew the missiles in return for a US promise not to invade, and a secret removal of US missiles from Turkey. It led to the hotline, and to the Partial Test Ban Treaty of 1963.

Vietnam. After French defeat at Dien Bien Phu (1954), Vietnam was divided at Geneva. The USA supported the South, and it sent over half a million troops by 1968. The Tet Offensive (1968) turned American opinion against the war. The US left in 1973 and Saigon fell in 1975. It showed the limits of US power.

Afghanistan (1979–89). The USSR invaded to support a communist government. The USA, with Pakistan, armed the mujahideen. The war drained the USSR and became "its Vietnam", while militants trained in it mattered later.

Détente was the relaxation that followed: SALT I (1972), Nixon's visit to China (1972) and the Helsinki Accords (1975), which accepted Europe's borders while promising human rights. It collapsed after 1979, as Reagan called for his Strategic Defense Initiative in 1983, the "second Cold War". Space too was a competition: Sputnik (1957), Gagarin (1961) and the Moon landing (1969).

W10.3 Decolonisation

Why did empires fall so quickly after 1945? Four forces came together. Nationalist movements had grown strong, with India as the great example. Europe was weakened by war, and could not pay for repression. Japan had shown in 1941–42 that European armies could lose. Superpower attitudes were hostile: both the USA and USSR opposed the old colonial system for their own reasons. And the UN gave a stage; its Declaration on the Granting of Independence (Resolution 1514, December 1960) condemned colonialism.

W10.4 The Non-Aligned Movement and India

Concept

Non-alignment. Non-alignment meant refusing to join either military bloc while judging every issue on its merits. It is not neutrality, because a neutral stays out, whereas a non-aligned state takes part in world affairs. It arose from the wish of new states to protect their independence, to win aid from both sides and to reduce tension. Nehru made it the base of India's foreign policy, and Panchsheel, the Five Principles agreed with China in 1954, stated its ethics. The Bandung Conference (April 1955) of 29 Asian and African states prepared the way, and the first summit met at Belgrade in 1961 with 25 members. Its founders were Nehru, Tito of Yugoslavia, Nasser of Egypt, Sukarno of Indonesia and Nkrumah of Ghana. It also demanded a New International Economic Order (1973–74).

India's record shows both its use and its limits. India mediated in Korea, it took part in peacekeeping and it criticised the Suez attack, but it was slower to condemn Hungary. After defeat by China (1962) it sought Western aid, and in 1971 it signed the Indo-Soviet Treaty ahead of the Bangladesh war, which was criticised as a tilt, as the USA was backing Pakistan and sent a fleet into the Bay of Bengal. Pakistan joined SEATO and CENTO, and this linked South Asia to the Cold War. Critics said NAM lacked unity, since many members were aligned in practice, and after 1991 it lost its original purpose; defenders say it gave weak states a voice, and it helped keep India's options open.

W10.5 How the Cold War ended

By the 1980s the Soviet system was failing. Central planning had built heavy industry but could not produce consumer goods or the new computer technology. Growth was close to zero, oil prices fell, and defence took perhaps a sixth or more of output. The Afghanistan war and Reagan's arms build-up added strain. Old leaders died in succession, and in 1985 the young Mikhail Gorbachev took over.

Concept

Glasnost and perestroika. Perestroika ("restructuring") tried to make the economy efficient with some market elements, and it came with glasnost ("openness"), which allowed criticism in the belief that it would expose corruption and win support. These were attempts at saving socialism, not ending it. But the economy got worse, while the press could now say so. Freedom revealed national grievances and crimes of the past. Chernobyl (1986) discredited official secrecy. The party, which had held the whole Union together, thus lost authority.

Abroad, Gorbachev signed the INF Treaty (1987), left Afghanistan (1989) and told Eastern Europe that it could decide its own future, the "Sinatra Doctrine". So in 1989 Solidarity won Polish elections, Hungary opened its border and the Berlin Wall was breached on 9 November. Germany was reunited on 3 October 1990. Only Romania's change was very violent.

The USSR broke up next. The Baltic republics and then Russia under Boris Yeltsin asserted their power. A hard-line coup in August 1991 failed, and it ruined the party. In December Russia, Ukraine and Belarus formed the Commonwealth of Independent States, Gorbachev resigned on 25 December, and fifteen states replaced the Union. The USA was left as the sole superpower.

For India, the end of the USSR removed a defence supplier and a reliable veto, and came alongside the economic crisis of 1991. So reform and a new foreign policy had to be fashioned together (I46).

Debate

Who started it, and who ended it? The orthodox view (Herbert Feis) blames Soviet expansion. Revisionists (William Appleman Williams, Gabriel Kolko) blame American economic expansion. Post-revisionists (John Lewis Gaddis) spread the blame through mutual misperception, though Gaddis after the opening of Soviet archives leaned back toward Stalin's responsibility. On the end, some credit Reagan's pressure, while others credit Gorbachev's choices and internal decay. Most stress decay with Gorbachev's decision not to use force.

What is the relevance of the Non-Aligned Movement in the Cold War period?

How to frame it: Meaning; not neutrality → Origin: Bandung, Belgrade → Founders → Achievements → Limits → Conclusion

Model answer (98 words)

Non-alignment meant staying out of both military blocs and judging issues independently; it was not neutrality. Rooted in Panchsheel (1954) and Bandung (1955), it began at Belgrade (1961) under Nehru, Tito, Nasser, Sukarno and Nkrumah. It protected new states' independence, let them take aid from both sides, supported decolonisation and demanded a fairer economic order. India used it to mediate in Korea. Its limits were real: many members were aligned in practice, and India itself signed the 1971 Indo-Soviet Treaty. Still, it gave weak states a collective voice in a bipolar world and reduced pressure to choose sides.

Why did the Soviet Union disintegrate?

How to frame it: Economic stagnation → Costs of arms and Afghanistan → Gorbachev's reforms → Nationalism → Eastern Europe 1989 → 1991 coup

Model answer (101 words)

By the 1980s central planning had stagnated, with consumer shortages and technological backwardness. Defence spending, the Afghanistan war and falling oil income worsened matters. Gorbachev's perestroika unsettled the economy without reviving it, while glasnost let people criticise the party and revealed past crimes. Nationalism rose in the Baltic and other republics. His refusal to use force in 1989 let Eastern Europe go, which encouraged them further. The failed coup of August 1991 destroyed the party and strengthened Yeltsin. In December Russia, Ukraine and Belarus formed the CIS, and the USSR ended. Internal decay was the cause, and reform made it visible.

What was the Cuban Missile Crisis?

How to frame it: Date and cause → Quarantine → Solution and result

Model answer (49 words)

In October 1962 the USSR placed missiles in Cuba, and Kennedy answered with a naval quarantine. War seemed near for thirteen days. Khrushchev withdrew the missiles for a US no-invasion pledge and a secret withdrawal from Turkey. It led to the hotline and the Partial Test Ban Treaty (1963).

How to frame it: Who, what

Model answer (16 words)

Nkrumah's term for continued economic and political control of ex-colonies by former rulers and big powers.

5 marks · 50 words

What were the main causes of decolonisation after 1945?

How to frame it: Nationalism → Europe weakened → Superpowers and UN

Model answer (45 words)

Strong nationalist movements, as in India, met a Europe weakened by war and unable to pay for repression. Japan had already broken the myth of European strength. The USA and USSR both opposed old empires, and the UN, especially Resolution 1514 (1960), added international pressure.
