1/6★ Must-learnIndia's place in the world economy (Maddison data)

In plain words

Before the British, India made and traded so much that it produced roughly a quarter of everything the world made. By independence that share had collapsed to a few per cent. The economist Angus Maddison reconstructed these old numbers, and IIBF asks you to remember the shape of the story: big share → peak → steep fall → fall continues till 1947. Think of a shop that once supplied a quarter of the neighbourhood and ended up supplying almost nobody.

Year India's share of world GDP Note
1000 AD India + China ≈ 50.5% –
1600 23% (China 29%) India also ≈ 33% of world trade
1700 24.4% peak
1820 16.1% after colonial rule set in
1947 3% trade share < 3%
  • From 1600 to 1947, India's per-capita income grew only 12%, Britain's 553%. Total GDP rose 2.44× in India against 52× in Britain.
  • How the decline happened: raw materials (cotton, indigo, jute) were bought cheaply for British mills, while cheap machine-made British goods were dumped in India and destroyed the handloom and craft industries. Indian tax revenue paid for Britain's costs (Home Charges, below). A country that exported finished textiles became an exporter of raw cotton and an importer of cloth.
  • Dadabhai Naoroji (Poverty and Un-British Rule in India) made the first national income estimate, for 1867-68: national income Rs 340 cr, per capita Rs 20.
MCQ

"India's share of world GDP in 1600?" → 23%. "Who made the first national income estimate?" → Dadabhai Naoroji (1867-68). "Whose data on historical GDP shares?" → Angus Maddison.

Remember

23% (1600) → peak 24.4% (1700) → 16.1% (1820) → about 3% (1947); trade 33% → under 3%. Different sources quote slightly different shares for 1000 AD and 1947, so go by the IIBF book if options differ.

2/6★ Must-learnThe British period: three phases

In plain words

The British came as traders, became rulers, and then ran the country as a supplier of cheap raw material and a buyer of British goods. Each phase tightened the squeeze: first buying Indian goods, then taxing the farmer heavily, and finally rebuilding the whole economy to suit British industry.

Phase Period Character
Early East India Company (founded 1600) 1600-1757 buy raw materials, sell finished goods
Company rule (from Plassey) 1757-1857 plunder disguised as trade, heavy land revenue
Crown rule 1858-1947 de-industrialisation, commercialisation of agriculture, drain of wealth
  • Land revenue systems: Zamindari, Mahalwari and Ryotwari were designed to extract maximum tax from peasants. Farmers were pushed to grow crops for British industry, which created intermediaries, more famines and landless labour.
  • De-industrialisation began in the mid-19th century. Cheap British imports, Mughal decline and unfavourable trade policy destroyed Indian handicrafts and textiles.
  • Drain of wealth (Naoroji, R.C. Dutt, C.N. Vakil) is the one-way transfer of resources to Britain with no matching return. Home Charges were its official part, 1858-1947: dividends to Company shareholders, interest on loans raised in England, army costs, pensions of British officers, guaranteed interest to railway companies and the Secretary of State's establishment.
  • Railways were built to move raw materials to ports, not to industrialise India. Western education aimed to create clerks, not scientists.
MCQ

"Home Charges relate to…?" → payments India made to Britain (dividends, interest, army and pension costs). "Drain of wealth theory by?" → Dadabhai Naoroji, R.C. Dutt.

Remember

drain is the theory; Home Charges are the official channel.

3/6★ Must-learnCharacteristics of the economy today

In plain words

India is a big economy but not a rich one per person. Total output is among the world's largest because there are 1.4 billion people; income per head is still modest, so the World Bank files India in the lower-middle-income group. It is a mixed economy: the government runs strategic areas (atomic energy, defence, railways) while private firms drive most other activity.

  • World Bank class: lower-middle income. Bands are revised each 1 July on GNI per capita: FY26 $1,136–4,495; FY27 (from 1 Jul 2026) $1,176–4,635. The four classes are low, lower-middle, upper-middle and high income.
  • Size: 4th-largest economy by nominal GDP (passed Japan in 2025; roughly USD 4 trn, IMF estimate) and 3rd by PPP, after the USA and China.
  • Mixed economy: public and private sectors coexist; the state leads in strategic and welfare areas, markets and private enterprise elsewhere.
  • Low per-capita income comes from poverty, unemployment, illiteracy, fast population growth and heavy dependence on the primary sector. India is large in total but still low in per-capita terms.

4/6★ Must-learnGrowth through the decades

In plain words

Read this as five chapters. (1) Slow decades of state control; growth was so sluggish that an economist nicknamed it the Hindu rate of growth. (2) A loosening in the 1980s that raised growth but was paid for by borrowing. (3) The 1991 crisis forced reform and a boom followed. (4) The 2008 global crisis: government spent to protect the economy, but companies and banks were left with heavy debt. (5) COVID: a sharp fall, then a fast rebound.

Remember

'Hindu rate of growth' is an economics term, not a religious one. India moved away from it in the 1980s (6th Plan), not in 1991.

Period What happened
1951-80 "Hindu rate of growth" — term coined by Raj Krishna (1978); about 3.5% a year, per-capita only 1.3%
1980s from the 6th Plan (1980) growth picked up on fiscal expansion, easier imports of capital goods and export promotion, but it was financed by borrowing and built deficits that led to the 1991 crisis
1992-2008 post-reform boom via liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation (LPG); 8th Plan about 6.7%, 10th Plan about 7.6%; 2002-03 to 2006-07 about 8.6% a year, second-fastest major economy after China
2008-09 Lehman collapse (15 Sep 2008); India gave 3 stimulus packages of Rs 1.86 lakh cr (3.5% of GDP) and RBI injected about Rs 5.6 lakh cr of liquidity. This was counter-cyclical policy: when private spending collapses, the government steps in as spender of last resort. The price was borrowing; the extra spending breached the FRBM deficit targets, and inflation followed in later years and the debt-fuelled corporate boom later left a twin balance sheet problem: over-indebted companies and banks with high NPAs
2008-09 to 2019-20 average about 6.5% (2011-12 series); GST, IBC, corporate tax cuts and demonetisation came in this phase
2019-20 slowdown to about 4.0% before COVID
2020-21 COVID contraction of –6.6% (2011-12 series); Q1 FY21 fell about 23.8%. Government responded with the Atmanirbhar Bharat package (announced May 2020, about Rs 20 lakh cr or 10% of GDP as stated)
2021-22 rebound of 8.7% (2011-12 series)
  • India faced three big shocks in four decades: the 1991 BoP crisis, the 2008 global financial crisis and COVID-19 (WHO declared a pandemic on 11 Mar 2020; India's first case was in Kerala on 30 Jan 2020; vaccination began 16 Jan 2021).

5/6★ Must-learnThe new GDP series (base 2022-23)

In plain words

GDP is measured in the prices of one base year. Over time the economy changes (new products, new sectors), so the base year is moved forward to keep the measurement realistic. That is why you will see GDP growth quoted on an old base (2011-12) in older books and on the new base (2022-23) now.

  • MoSPI released a new GDP series with base year 2022-23 on 27 Feb 2026, replacing the 2011-12 base.
  • Real GDP growth: FY24 7.2%, FY25 7.1%, FY26 7.6%. Nominal GDP in FY25 is Rs 318.07 lakh cr (FY23 base-year GDP: Rs 261.18 lakh cr).
  • Methods improved: double deflation, GST/e-Vahan/PFMS data, supply-use tables, and annual household surveys (ASUSE, PLFS).
  • The IIP and Index of Core Industries moved to the 2022-23 base too; the new CPI base is 2024.
Remember

old series = 2011-12; new GDP/IIP = 2022-23; CPI = 2024.

6/6Structural change

In plain words

In every country the economy shifts from farms to factories to services as people get richer. India's unusual feature is that services grew faster than manufacturing (see Unit 2).

As incomes rise the primary sector's share falls and services rise. Services overtook agriculture in 1980-81 and now supply more than half of GVA. COVID briefly lifted agriculture's share because it was the one sector left open during the lockdown.

Module A · Unit 2Sectors of the Indian Economy6 topics · 5 ★ must-learn · 1 ☆ good to know

1/6★ Must-learnFive sectors

In plain words

Follow one product through its life. Iron ore dug from a mine is primary. The factory that makes steel and builds a car from it is secondary. The bank that gives the car loan is tertiary. The engineer who designs the car's navigation software works in the quaternary (knowledge) sector. The board that decides whether the company should switch to electric vehicles sits in the quinary sector, the top decision layer. The higher you go, the fewer the people but the bigger the influence.

Sector Activities Note
Primary agriculture, forestry, fishing, mining direct use of natural resources
Secondary manufacturing, construction, utilities processes primary output; also called industrial sector
Tertiary banking, insurance, education, health, communication services
Quaternary teaching, research, IT, R&D the "knowledge" sector, part of tertiary
Quinary top decision-makers: senior executives, officials, scientists, advisers the "brain" of the economy; few people
  • Primary sector = main provider of livelihood (employs well over a third of workers) but gives under a fifth of GVA. Secondary workers are called blue-collar, quaternary white-collar, and the quinary elite sometimes gold-collar.
  • Secondary sector covers registered and unregistered manufacturing, construction, and electricity-gas-water utilities.
  • An economy is agrarian if agriculture gives at least 50% of income and livelihood, industrial if the secondary sector gives at least half of GDP and employment, and service-based if tertiary gives at least half.
MCQ

"Which is NOT primary?" → construction. "Quaternary is part of?" → services. "Mining and quarrying belong to?" → primary.

Remember

Mining is primary (it extracts raw nature) although national accounts place it in the 'industry' group. Quaternary = knowledge workers (IT, research); quinary = top decision makers (ministers, central bank governors, board members, judges).

3/6★ Must-learnContribution of each sector to GDP

In plain words

Classic development shows output moving farm → factory → services (the Kuznets pattern). India skipped the factory stage: it jumped from an agriculture-led economy to a services-led one. The catch is that services such as IT and finance need skilled, capital-heavy work and employ relatively few people, so most workers stayed in low-productivity farming. This is why economists speak of jobless growth: output grows, jobs do not keep pace.

  • Structural leapfrog: India moved from agriculture directly to services, bypassing mass manufacturing. Agriculture gives under a fifth of GVA but still employs a large share of workers.
  • Services have been above 50% since about 2000-01, yet they do not create jobs in proportion to their share; agriculture is still the largest employer.
  • Construction is the second-largest employer (about 8% of employment). Manufacturing employs only about 12% of the labour force, though it is the sector expected to absorb low-skilled workers leaving agriculture.

4/6★ Must-learnIndustry

  • Four sub-sectors: mining and quarrying, manufacturing, electricity-gas-water, and construction. Industry's share rose from about 17% in 1950-51 to 29% in 2021-22.
  • Phases: I 1951-65 foundation · II 1965-80 fall · III 1980-91 recovery · IV 1991 onward post-reform.
  • Index of Industrial Production (IIP): measures the change in volume of production across mining, manufacturing and electricity, and by use (basic, capital, intermediate and consumer goods). It is compiled by NSO/MoSPI and now has base 2022-23.
  • Index of Core Industries (ICI): the new series (base 2022-23) covers 9 industries — coal, crude oil, natural gas, refinery products, fertilisers, steel, cement, electricity and iron ore (new). The old series had 8, without iron ore. Electricity carries the largest weight.

5/6☆ Good to knowAgriculture and services

  • Agriculture is the largest unorganised sector and the main source of rural livelihood. Policy supports include PMFBY crop insurance (2016), 10,000 FPOs and the Agriculture Infrastructure Fund.
  • Services include trade, hotels, transport, communication, finance, real estate, professional services and public administration. India is among the top-10 service exporters, with software making up about 40% of net services exports (book).

6/6★ Must-learnSunrise sectors, organised vs unorganised

In plain words

Sunrise means 'just starting, big future' (think green hydrogen, electric vehicles, semiconductors). Organised vs unorganised is about job quality: an organised-sector employee has a registered employer, a salary slip, PF and leave; a daily-wage labourer or street vendor has none of these. The great majority of Indian workers are in the unorganised sector.

  • A sunrise sector is young with high growth potential: green energy and green hydrogen, electric vehicles, semiconductors, solar, fintech, AI, IT, electronics, pharma, automobiles, healthcare, infrastructure, retail and processing.
  • Organised sector: registered under statutory laws (Factories Act, Companies Act), keeps audited accounts, and gives social security such as PF and ESI.
  • The unorganised sector (NCEUS definition) is unincorporated private enterprise run as proprietorship or partnership with fewer than 10 workers.

1/5★ Must-learnPlanning basics

In plain words

Planning means the state decides in advance how much to invest, in which sectors, and with what money, instead of leaving everything to markets. A newly independent, poor country with scarce capital chose this route so that steel, power and irrigation would be built first. Before 1947 several groups had already drawn up blueprints; the table below is a favourite MCQ source.

  • Types of planning: regional or national; federal, state or local; centralised or decentralised; long or short term; sectoral (one sector) or spatial (geographic distribution of activity).
  • The USSR ran the first national plan (1928-33). The Indian National Congress set up the National Planning Committee in 1938.
  • Objectives: economic growth, poverty alleviation, employment, social justice and lower inequality, self-reliance (not autarky) and modernisation.
  • Planning Commission: set up in March 1950, a non-statutory body with the PM as Chairman and a Deputy Chairman in charge. Plans were approved by the National Development Council (NDC), also chaired by the PM.

3/5★ Must-learnFive-Year Plans

In plain words

Each Plan had a theme. 1st: feed the country (farms and irrigation). 2nd: build industry (steel plants). 3rd: stand on our own feet, but wars and drought knocked it off course. Then came a three-year plan holiday. The 1970s focused on poverty, the 1980s on jobs and productivity, and from the 8th Plan (1992) the state stopped commanding and started guiding (indicative planning).

Targets and actuals follow MoSPI data (first three plans use national income, the Fourth uses NDP, later ones GDP).

4/5★ Must-learnNITI Aayog

In plain words

The old Planning Commission was like a head of the household who held the purse strings and told each state how much it would get. NITI Aayog is closer to a consultant: it designs strategies, ranks states, and encourages them to compete and cooperate, but it does not hand out money. Fund transfers to states are decided by the Finance Commission and the Finance Ministry.

  • National Institution for Transforming India, set up on 1 Jan 2015 by a Union Cabinet resolution (non-statutory). It is the government's policy think tank, chaired by the Prime Minister.
  • Structure: Vice-Chairperson, full-time members, CEO, ex-officio members and special invitees; the Governing Council includes Chief Ministers of states and UTs with legislatures and Lt Governors of other UTs.
  • Role in practice: shapes policy and ranks states through competitive indices (such as the SDG India Index) instead of allocating funds.
  • Two hubs: the Team India Hub links states with the Centre; the Knowledge & Innovation Hub builds the think-tank role.
  • Approach: cooperative and competitive federalism, bottom-up planning, real-time monitoring and evaluation through the Development Monitoring & Evaluation Office.
  • Initiatives: Atal Innovation Mission (Atal Tinkering Labs, Atal Incubation Centres), SDG India Index and composite state indices.
  • Strategy for New India @75 (Dec 2018): 41 chapters in 4 sections — Drivers, Infrastructure, Inclusion, Governance. Goals included a $4 trn economy by 2022-23, tax-to-GDP of 22% and exports of $800 bn.
  • Planning framework: replaced five-year plans with a 15-year vision, 7-year strategy and 3-year action agenda.
  • NITI does not allocate funds to states. Both the Planning Commission and NITI Aayog are non-constitutional and non-statutory (created by executive resolution).
  • ☆ Why the Planning Commission was replaced: planning no longer fitted a market economy after 1991, and central allocation clashed with the states' growing role.

5/5★ Must-learnFinancing the plans

  1. Domestic budgetary sources — taxes (largest), public borrowing, small savings, PSU surplus. This is the biggest source.
  2. Deficit financing — borrowing from RBI, drawing down cash balances, ad-hoc Treasury bills. This is the second source.
  3. Foreign assistance — IBRD/IDA loans, bilateral aid, foreign investment.

1/4★ Must-learnPriority Sector Lending (PSL)

In plain words

Left alone, banks lend where returns and safety look best: big companies. Farmers, tiny shops and poorer households also need credit but are riskier and costlier to serve. So the RBI makes every bank reserve a minimum share of its lending for these 'priority' areas, much like a school reserving seats. The share is worked out on the bank's credit of the previous year, using a base called ANBC/CEOBE.

  • Origin: Morarji Desai (Deputy PM and Finance Minister) said in the Lok Sabha on 14 Dec 1967 that agriculture, small industry and exports were not getting their fair share of bank credit. This was the first use of "priority sector". Social control of banks followed, and the National Credit Council stressed priority lending in July 1968.
  • Nine categories: Agriculture · MSME · Export Credit · Education · Housing · Social Infrastructure · Renewable Energy · Others · Weaker Sections.
  • Krishnaswamy Committee (1980) recommended the 40% priority sector target for commercial banks.
  • Targets are computed on Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC) or Credit Equivalent of Off-Balance Sheet Exposure (CEOBE), whichever is higher, of the previous year-end.
  • RBI's revised PSL Master Directions apply from 1 Apr 2025, replacing the 2020 guidelines.

  • Foreign banks with fewer than 20 branches: 40% overall, of which export credit can be up to 32% and at least 8% must go to other priority sectors.
  • Banks that miss targets must deposit the shortfall in funds such as RIDF (NABARD), SEDF/MSE fund (SIDBI), the NHB fund and MUDRA, which pay below-market interest.
  • PSL Certificates (PSLC): a market where banks with surplus PSL lending sell it to banks with a shortfall, traded on RBI's e-Kuber platform; no loan moves between balance sheets. Four categories: Agriculture, Small and Marginal Farmers, Micro Enterprises, General.
  • The Sept 2020 revision added bank finance to start-ups, solar power for agricultural pumps and compressed biogas plants.
Example

Bank A meets 42% overall PSL but only 6% in micro enterprises against the 7.5% target (shortfall 1.5%). Instead of parking money in a low-interest development fund, it buys PSLC-Micro from a regional rural bank with surplus micro lending. The seller earns a premium, the buyer meets the rule.

2/4★ Must-learnMSME definition (from 1 Apr 2025)

In plain words

MSMEs are the small and mid-sized businesses that employ most people outside farming. Their size is checked by two tests together: how much is invested in plant and machinery/equipment, and annual turnover. To be a micro enterprise a firm must be within both micro limits; if either is crossed it moves into the next class. Export turnover is left out when turnover is counted, so exporters are not pushed into a bigger class.

The classification is under the MSMED Act, 2006.

4/4★ Must-learnMake in India, Start-up India, Stand-up India, PLI

In plain words

These are the government's push to make India a manufacturing and start-up hub. Make in India invites firms to manufacture here; Start-up India eases rules and finance for new ventures; Stand-up India targets women and SC/ST entrepreneurs; PLI pays firms a reward on extra sales they make in India.

  • Make in India (Sept 2014) aims to make India a global design and manufacturing hub, with four pillars: New Processes, New Infrastructure, New Sectors, New Mindset.
  • Start-up India (Jan 2016) supports new ventures with easier finance, tax breaks and simpler compliance. Budget 2025-26 added Rs 10,000 cr to the Fund of Funds.
  • Stand-up India gives loans of Rs 10 lakh to Rs 1 crore to women and SC/ST entrepreneurs for greenfield ventures.
  • Production Linked Incentive (PLI): announced in Budget 2021-22 with Rs 1.97 lakh cr over five years for 13 sectors — mobiles and electronic components, drug intermediates/APIs, medical devices, autos and components, pharma drugs, specialty steel, telecom and networking, electronic/technology products, white goods, food products, textiles, high-efficiency solar PV modules and ACC batteries. Incentives are about 4-6% of incremental sales over the 2019-20 base for 4-6 years.
  • The Atmanirbhar Bharat package (2020) carried the MSME relief measures (ECLGS, revised definition).

Module A · Unit 5Infrastructure including Social Infrastructure4 topics · 4 ★ must-learn

1/4★ Must-learnConcepts

In plain words

Infrastructure is the underlying network that lets every other activity run: roads, power, ports, telecom (hard), and the institutions that run the economy, such as banks, schools and courts (soft). Because it is costly and slow to pay back, governments often build it jointly with private firms in a public-private partnership (PPP).

  • Hard infrastructure is physical networks such as roads, ports, airports and pipelines. Soft infrastructure is institutions that keep the economy running: financial, education, health and law-and-order bodies. Infrastructure is also split into economic and social.
  • Critical infrastructure is whatever the wider economy depends on: power, gas, oil, telecom, water, health, transport, financial services and security.
  • Green infrastructure is natural ecosystems providing life-support services: wildlife sanctuaries, tiger and elephant reserves, bird sanctuaries, green belts, the Western Ghats. A highway is not green infrastructure.
  • Public money is scarce, so PPP is the preferred model; the National Investment & Infrastructure Fund (NIIF) was created to improve infrastructure finance.

Module A · Unit 6Globalisation: Impact on India3 topics · 3 ★ must-learn

1/3★ Must-learnConcept and impact

In plain words

Globalisation means an economy is plugged into the world: goods, money, technology and people move across borders with fewer barriers. India opened up after 1991 by cutting import duties, removing quotas, joining WTO agreements and creating Special Economic Zones (SEZs). The gain is bigger markets and better technology; the cost is that a shock abroad now hurts India too.

  • Globalisation is rising economic integration among nations through cross-border flows of goods, services, capital, technology, labour and information. The WTO describes it as "unrestricted cross-border flows of goods and services, capital and labour".
  • The two World Wars and the Great Depression forced a retreat from globalisation; the OECD popularised the term again in the mid-1980s. India adopted it formally with the new economic policy of 1991-92.
  • Gains for India: higher per-capita income, more and better jobs, more choice and lower prices for consumers, technology transfer, foreign investment, and new agricultural export opportunities that pushed farm mechanisation and commercial farming.
  • Risks: greater exposure to external shocks and volatile capital flows.
  • Fair globalisation means benefits are shared and workers' rights are protected. It rests on three mutually reinforcing pillars: economic development, social development and environmental protection.

Module A · Unit 7Economic Reforms4 topics · 4 ★ must-learn

1/4★ Must-learnWhy the 1991 crisis happened

In plain words

Think of a household that spent far more than it earned through the 1980s, financed the gap by borrowing, and then faced a sudden shock. The government's deficit was huge, imports cost more than exports earned, oil prices spiked with the Gulf War, and Gulf workers stopped sending money home. By mid-1991 there were barely enough dollars to pay for a couple of weeks of imports, and India nearly defaulted on its foreign debt.

  • Fiscal: the Centre's fiscal deficit rose from about 5.1% of GDP in the early 1980s to above 8% of GDP in 1990-91 (Union Budget speech 1991-92), driven by non-developmental spending. Borrowing to cover it pushed domestic debt past 50% of GDP and raised the interest burden.
  • External: the current account deficit widened to 3.69% of GDP. The Gulf War cut remittances, NRIs withdrew deposits and reserves fell to about US$1.2 bn, roughly two weeks of imports. India was close to defaulting.
  • Rescue: India pledged about 67 tonnes of gold (47 t to the Bank of England, 20 t to UBS) to raise emergency foreign currency, and the rupee was devalued in two steps on 1 and 3 July 1991.
  • Prices: wholesale-price inflation hit 12.1% in 1990-91 (about 6.4% on average in the 1980s) because the 1980s were financed by deficit financing and supply was weak.
  • Root cause (Jagdish Bhagwati): (1) heavy bureaucratic control over production, investment and trade, (2) inward-looking trade and FDI policy, (3) inefficient public-sector enterprises. The 1990 oil shock turned a weak economy into a crisis.

2/4★ Must-learnThe response: LPG

In plain words

Before 1991 an entrepreneur needed dozens of permits, could not produce beyond the licensed quantity, and buyers waited years for a scooter. This was called the licence raj. The 1991 package ended it with three moves, Liberalisation (free firms from licences), Privatisation (open sectors to private players), Globalisation (open to trade and foreign capital).

  • Reforms began in July 1991 (New Industrial Policy, 24 Jul 1991). The 1980s changes were mild and influenced by the Washington Consensus; the 1991 reforms were far wider, covering industry, trade, investment and later agriculture.
  • Liberalisation: industrial licensing abolished for almost all industries (only a few strategic or hazardous ones still need a licence), freedom to import, capacity expansion without approval. The MRTP Act was later replaced by the Competition Act, 2002 (Competition Commission of India).
  • Privatisation: sectors reserved for the public sector were cut to a very few (atomic energy and railway operations), disinvestment began, and more room was given to private players, a smaller role for the public sector, more FDI and competition.
  • Globalisation: two-step devaluation (July 1991), dual exchange rate (LERMS) in 1992 and a unified market rate in 1993, lower tariffs and easier FDI; integration with the world economy; India now depends more on global conditions and vice versa.
  • Aims: faster growth, lower inflation and CAD, fix the BoP problem.

1/5★ Must-learnForeign trade policy (FTP)

In plain words

A country's trade policy is its rulebook for what may be imported and exported, and what help exporters get. Until 1991 India kept imports out with high tariffs and quotas. Since then the policy has been to push exports (incentives, easier procedures) and let trade flow more freely. The FTP is issued by the Commerce Ministry through the DGFT; the older ones lasted five years, the current one has no end date.

  • Before 1991 India protected its market with high tariffs and quantitative restrictions. After 1991 it liberalised trade through tariff cuts and rupee convertibility on the current account, driven by competitiveness needs and the BoP crisis.
  • FTP 2015-20 (from 1 Apr 2015, tied to "Make in India") targeted exports of $900 bn by 2019-20, up from $465 bn in 2013-14, and a rise in global export share from 2% to 3.5%. It introduced MEIS (Merchandise Exports from India Scheme) and SEIS (Services Exports from India Scheme), cut the EPCG export obligation to 75% for domestic capital goods, relaunched Niryat Bandhu and moved to paperless trade. It was extended several times, to March 2023.
  • MEIS was replaced by RoDTEP (Jan 2021) and SEIS was discontinued.
  • FTP 2023 (effective 1 Apr 2023) is open-ended rather than five-year. It targets $2 trillion of exports by 2030, promotes rupee settlement and merchanting trade, adds four Towns of Export Excellence, and extends benefits to e-commerce exports.
  • FTP 2023 splits the $2 trillion target into about $1 trillion of goods and $1 trillion of services. Its four pillars are incentive to remission, export promotion through collaboration (states and districts as export hubs), ease of doing business, and emerging areas (e-commerce exports, SCOMET items). It also enables trade settlement in rupees through Special Rupee Vostro Accounts (SRVA).
  • RoDTEP refunds the embedded central, state and local taxes on inputs (fuel, electricity, mandi tax) that were not otherwise refunded. It replaced MEIS because direct export incentives had been challenged under WTO subsidy rules, so India moved to a tax-remission design.
  • EPCG scheme: duty-free import of capital goods against an export obligation of 6 times the duty saved, within 6 years. Advance Authorisation: duty-free import of raw material that is physically used in the export product.
  • The apex agency is the Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT) under the Commerce Ministry.

2/5★ Must-learnFDI: routes

In plain words

A foreign investor can enter an Indian business through two doors. The automatic route is an open door: invest first, just inform the RBI afterwards. The government route is a door with a gatekeeper: get approval first. Which door applies depends on the sector. A few sectors are permanently shut (prohibited).

FDI is an investment that gives a lasting interest and control in an Indian entity. A holding of 10% or more in a listed company counts as FDI; below 10% is portfolio investment.

  • Automatic route: no prior approval from the government or RBI; the company only reports to RBI within 30 days (on the FIRMS portal).
  • Government route: prior approval through the relevant ministry (applications via the National Single Window System); proposals above Rs 5,000 cr need clearance from the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA).
  • Press Note 3 (2020): investors from countries sharing a land border with India need government approval. A March 2026 easing lets non-controlling beneficial ownership of up to 10% use the automatic route in specified manufacturing sectors (capital goods, electronic components and similar), with a 60-day decision timeline.
  • Types of FDI: Greenfield (new subsidiary from scratch), Brownfield (buy an existing firm) and Joint venture.

1/4★ Must-learnIMF

In plain words

After the Second World War, countries agreed at Bretton Woods (New Hampshire, USA, 1944) to create two institutions. The IMF is like an emergency fund and referee for exchange rates: it lends short-term when a country runs out of foreign currency (a balance-of-payments crisis). The World Bank funds long-term development projects.

  • Born at the Bretton Woods conference (July 1944) and formally in existence from 27 Dec 1945 (29 signatories). HQ Washington DC; 191 members (Liechtenstein joined Oct 2024). Managing Director: Kristalina Georgieva.
  • India is a founder member (joined 27 Dec 1945). The Finance Minister is the ex-officio Governor. India's quota is about SDR 13,114 mn (2.76%) with 2.63% of votes, among the top ten quota holders.
  • Functions: surveillance of members' policies (Article IV), financing temporary BoP needs, concessional loans for poor countries (HIPC and MDRI debt relief), mobilising other financing, technical assistance and training, research and data, and creating reserve assets (SDRs).
  • SDR (Special Drawing Right): created in 1969. It is neither a currency nor a claim on the IMF, but a potential claim on freely usable currencies, and the IMF's unit of account. It was first defined as 0.888671 g of gold (= 1 USD) and is now a basket of five currencies: US dollar, euro, Chinese renminbi (from 1 Oct 2016), Japanese yen, pound sterling. A currency qualifies if it meets the export criterion (issuer is a top-five exporter) and the freely usable criterion. The basket is reviewed every five years (last completed 2022).

3/4★ Must-learnWTO

In plain words

The WTO is the rulebook and referee for world trade. Two rules sit at its heart: Most Favoured Nation (give every member the same trade treatment you give your best partner) and National Treatment (once imported, a foreign product is treated like a domestic one).

  • The GATT was signed by 23 nations in Geneva on 30 Oct 1947 and ran 1948-94 through eight rounds; the last was the Uruguay Round (1986-94). The WTO was created on 1 Jan 1995, HQ Geneva, with 166 members (Comoros and Timor-Leste joined Aug 2024). Director-General: Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala.
  • India was a GATT founder (July 1948) and a WTO member from 1 Jan 1995.
  • Agreement on Agriculture – subsidy boxes: Green box = little or no trade distortion (research, pest control, decoupled income support), allowed without limit. Blue box = payments linked to production-limiting programmes, allowed under conditions. Amber box = trade-distorting price support such as MSP, capped by the de minimis limit: 10% of the value of production for developing countries (5% for developed). The Peace Clause agreed at Bali 2013 shields developing countries' public stockholding programmes from challenge.
  • Functions: administer trade agreements, host negotiations, settle disputes, review trade policies, give technical assistance, cooperate with the IMF and World Bank.
  • Key agreements: GATT, GATS, TRIPS, TRIMS, Agreement on Agriculture (AoA), SPS, Subsidies, Trade Facilitation (2017).
  • Ministerial Conference is the top body and meets about every two years: MC1 Singapore 1996 · MC2 Geneva 1998 · MC3 Seattle 1999 · MC4 Doha 2001 · MC5 Cancun 2003 · MC6 Hong Kong 2005 · MC7 Geneva 2009 · MC8 Geneva 2011 · MC9 Bali 2013 · MC10 Nairobi 2015 · MC11 Buenos Aires 2017 · MC12 Geneva, Jun 2022 · MC13 Abu Dhabi, 26 Feb–2 Mar 2024 · MC14 Yaoundé (Cameroon), 26-30 Mar 2026.
  • MC12 produced the first multilateral Fisheries Subsidies Agreement, in force since 15 Sep 2025; it bans subsidies for illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing, overfished stocks and unregulated high-seas fishing. MC14 ended without agreement on a reform declaration.

2/4★ Must-learnClimate commitments

In plain words

Climate action works through international treaties that set goals and national pledges that deliver them. The sequence to remember is UNFCCC (1992) → Kyoto (1997) → Paris (2015): first the principle, then binding cuts for rich countries, then every country making its own pledge (an NDC). India's pledges are called Panchamrit, the five nectar elements.

1/3★ Must-learnWhat economics studies: four definitions

In plain words

Imagine a family with a fixed monthly salary and a long list of things it wants: a bigger home, a car, school fees, holidays. It cannot have everything, so it must choose. Economics is the study of that problem for a whole society. Over time economists defined it differently: first as the study of wealth (Smith), then of human welfare (Marshall), then of choice under scarcity (Robbins).

Economics is about scarcity and choice. Wants are unlimited, resources are limited and have alternative uses, so every society must choose. The word comes from the Greek oikonomia (oikos = home, nemein = management), meaning household management.

  • A thing is "scarce" in the economic sense only if demand exceeds supply. Rotten eggs are fewer than good eggs but are not scarce, because nobody wants them.
  • Robbins' book: An Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science (1932); Marshall's welfare definition says economics studies "mankind in the ordinary business of life" and the material requisites of well-being.

1/7★ Must-learnDemand: the law, its causes and the demand curve

In plain words

Demand is not mere wanting. It is wanting and being able and willing to pay. The law of demand says that when a good gets costlier people buy less of it, and when it gets cheaper they buy more (tomatoes at Rs 20/kg versus Rs 80/kg). The relationship holds only if everything else stays the same, which economists call ceteris paribus.

  • The demand schedule (or demand curve) shows the relationship between a good's price and the quantity bought, other things held constant. Price and quantity move in opposite directions, so the curve slopes downward from left to right. This is the law of downward-sloping demand.
  • Quantity demanded falls when price rises for two reasons. The substitution effect: buyers switch to cheaper alternatives (dal gets dearer, so you eat more vegetables). The income effect: a higher price makes you feel poorer, so you buy less (petrol prices double, you cut consumption).
  • Entry of new buyers: a lower price brings in people who could not afford the good earlier, which also widens market demand.
  • Market demand is the sum of all individual demands at each price. It obeys the same downward-sloping law.
  • Law of diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit consumed gives less satisfaction than the one before (the first glass of water to a thirsty person gives far more than the fourth). This is why people pay less for additional units.
MCQ

"Market demand curve obeys…?" → law of downward-sloping demand. "Demand schedule is the relationship between…?" → price and quantity bought.

2/7★ Must-learnForces behind demand, and shifts in demand

In plain words

Ask one question: what changed? If the good's own price changed, you slide along the same curve. If anything else changed (income, a rival product's price, fashion, weather), the whole curve moves because people now want a different quantity at every price.

  • Determinants of demand besides price: average income, size of the market (population), prices and availability of related goods (substitutes), tastes and preferences, special influences (weather, seasons) and expectations about future prices.
  • A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the same curve (a change in quantity demanded). A change in any other factor shifts the whole curve (a change in demand). An income rise shifts the car demand curve to the right: more cars are bought at every price.
  • Demand for good A tends to be low when the price of its substitute B is low.
Remember

Own price changes → move along the curve. Anything else changes → curve shifts. The same rule applies to supply.

4/7★ Must-learnPrice elasticity of demand (Ep)

In plain words

The law of demand only says quantity falls when price rises. Elasticity says by how much. If a small price rise makes buyers run away, demand is elastic (stretchy). If they hardly react, it is inelastic. Salt is inelastic: you will not buy less salt because it got 10% dearer. A holiday package is elastic: a 10% rise puts many people off.

  • Formula: Ep = (% change in quantity demanded) ÷ (% change in price). Ignore the minus sign and read the size. In point form: Ep = (ΔQ ÷ ΔP) × (P1 ÷ Q1).
Example

Price falls from ₹20 to ₹18 → change = −2 ÷ 20 = −10%. Quantity rises from 100 to 125 → change = 25 ÷ 100 = +25%. Ep = 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5 → elastic.

6/7★ Must-learnSupply: the curve, its determinants and shifts

In plain words

Supply is the mirror image of demand. When the price rises, producing becomes more profitable, so firms offer more. The supply curve therefore slopes upward. Cheaper inputs or better technology shift it right; a bad harvest or costlier inputs shift it left.

  • The supply schedule shows the quantity producers will offer at each price, other things constant. The supply curve slopes upward: a higher price makes production more profitable, so more is supplied.
  • Determinants of supply: cost of production (input prices such as labour and energy), technology (lower inputs per unit), prices of related goods (production substitutes), government policy (taxes, regulation, minimum wages, trade policy), special factors (weather, market structure) and expectations about future prices.
  • Own-price change = movement along the supply curve. Change in any other factor = shift of the curve. Cost-saving technology shifts supply to the right; a bad harvest or costlier inputs shift it to the left.
MCQ

"Shift in supply means…?" → a change in factors other than the good's own price. "Forces behind supply" → cost of production, technology, government policy (all of these).

7/7★ Must-learnMarket equilibrium and the effect of shifts

In plain words

A market settles where the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer. If the price is too high, goods pile up unsold and sellers cut prices; if too low, buyers queue and the price rises. The price at which it all balances is the equilibrium (market-clearing) price.

Equilibrium is the price at which quantity demanded = quantity supplied. It is also called the market-clearing price. At any higher price there is a surplus (price falls); at any lower price a shortage (price rises).

1/6★ Must-learnFunctions of money

In plain words

Without money a farmer with wheat who needs shoes must find a cobbler who wants wheat (barter). Money removes that problem: it is the medium of exchange everyone accepts, the measure of value in which all prices are quoted, a store of value you can keep for later, and the standard of deferred payments that lets loans and contracts be settled in future.

MCQ

2/6★ Must-learnMeasures of money supply

In plain words

RBI counts money in layers, from the most liquid (cash you can spend now) to the least. M1 is cash plus money you can withdraw on demand. M3 adds fixed and recurring deposits, which are locked for a term. M0 is different: it is the base the RBI itself creates (currency plus banks' deposits with the RBI), and the banking system builds the larger M3 on top of it.

Money supply is the stock of money in the economy at a point in time. The RBI publishes it fortnightly. It influences price levels, exchange rates and business cycles.

3/6★ Must-learnComponents versus sources of M3; money multiplier; velocity

In plain words

Think of the central bank as the tap and the banking system as a set of pipes that multiply the water. The money multiplier says how many rupees of broad money come from each rupee of base money. The more cash people hold, or the more banks keep idle, the less it multiplies.

4/6★ Must-learnInflation: meaning and effects

In plain words

Inflation means the same rupee buys less over time. If groceries cost Rs 1,000 a year ago and Rs 1,060 now, inflation is 6%. Mild inflation is normal in a growing economy; high inflation hurts savers and the poor most, because their money buys less every month.

  • Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. Purchasing power of money falls, so each rupee buys fewer goods. The inflation rate is the annualised percentage change in a price index.
  • Negative effects: erodes the real value of money, creates uncertainty that discourages saving and investment, and can cause hoarding and shortages. Positive effects: can soften a recession and reduces the real burden of debt.

5/6★ Must-learnCauses: demand-pull and cost-push

In plain words

Demand-pull: buyers have more money and want more than the economy can produce, so prices are pulled up ("too much money chasing too few goods"). Cost-push: the cost of making things rises (oil, raw material, wages), so producers push the higher cost into prices.

Module B · Unit 15Theories of Interest4 topics · 4 ★ must-learn

1/4★ Must-learnWhat interest is

In plain words

Interest is the price of using someone else's money. A lender asks for it for three reasons: the risk that the loan may not come back, the trouble of lending, and the simple fact that money in hand today is worth more than money later. The sum of all three is gross interest; what remains after removing risk and trouble is pure (net) interest, roughly the rate on a risk-free government security.

  • Interest is the payment a borrower makes for using money for a period. It is one of the four types of factor income: rent (land), wages (labour), interest (capital) and profit (entrepreneur).
  • It has three elements: payment for the risk of the loan, payment for the trouble of lending, and pure interest (payment for the use of money). Inflation is also factored in. Rates differ across loans because of differences in risk and trouble: a moneylender's unsecured loan costs more than a bank loan against collateral, and the government pays more on long-term than on short-term borrowing.
MCQ

"Pick the odd man out: rent, interest, wages, money" → money (the other three are factor incomes).

2/4★ Must-learnClassical theory: savings and investment

In plain words

The classical view treats the interest rate as a plain market price. Savers supply funds and want a reward for waiting; businesses demand funds and will pay as long as the money earns more than it costs. The rate settles where savings and investment are equal.

  • Also called the real theory, demand and supply of savings or capital theory. Developed by Marshall, Fisher and Pigou. The interest rate is set where the supply of savings equals the demand for investment, under perfect competition.
  • Demand for capital comes from investors and depends on the marginal productivity of capital. Investment continues while productivity exceeds the interest rate, so the demand curve slopes downward: higher interest, less demand for capital.
  • Supply of savings depends on thrift or time preference. Interest is the reward for abstinence or waiting, so the supply curve slopes upward.
  • Keynes' criticism: the theory is circular (it needs income to know savings, but income depends on investment), it assumes full employment, and interest is a monetary phenomenon, not a reward for saving. Investment depends more on the marginal efficiency of capital and expectations than on the interest rate, and saving depends mainly on income, not on the interest rate.

3/4★ Must-learnKeynes' liquidity preference theory

In plain words

Keynes asked a different question: why would anyone hold idle cash that earns nothing? Because cash is liquid. Interest is the price you must be paid to give up that liquidity. Everyone wants some cash (to pay bills, for emergencies, or to wait for a better time to buy bonds), and the central bank decides how much money exists. The rate is set where these two meet.

  • In the General Theory, interest is purely monetary: it is the reward for parting with liquidity, determined by the demand for money (liquidity preference) and the supply of money, which the monetary authority fixes.
  • Keynes used a two-asset economy: money (earns no interest) and long-term bonds. Bond prices and interest rates move inversely.
  • Three motives for holding money:

  • Speculative demand: when interest is high, bond prices are low and expected to rise, so people hold bonds, not cash (money demand low). When interest is low, bond prices are high and expected to fall, so people hold cash (money demand high).
  • The money demand (LP) curve slopes downward. Money supply is a vertical line. Equilibrium is where they cross. If the rate is above equilibrium, excess money supply → people buy bonds → bond prices rise → rate falls. If below, excess demand → people sell bonds → bond prices fall → rate rises.
  • Increase in money supply (central bank buys securities in the open market) → rate of interest falls. Rise in income or in expected future interest rates shifts the LP curve up → rate of interest rises with money supply unchanged.
  • Liquidity trap: at a very low interest rate the speculative demand curve becomes horizontal: people expect rates to rise (bond prices to fall), so they hold on to any extra money as cash. Added money supply does not lower the rate any further and monetary policy becomes ineffective.

4/4★ Must-learnHicks–Hansen synthesis: the IS-LM model

In plain words

The classical and Keynesian theories each told half the story. IS-LM puts both on one diagram: the IS curve is the goods market (saving = investment) and the LM curve is the money market (money demand = money supply). Where they cross, the interest rate and national income are set together. Fiscal policy moves IS; monetary policy moves LM.

Sir John Hicks and Alvin Hansen combined the classical and Keynesian theories into a determinate theory: both real factors (productivity, thrift) and monetary factors (liquidity preference, money supply) matter.

Module B · Unit 16Business Cycles3 topics · 3 ★ must-learn

1/3★ Must-learnMeaning and characteristics

In plain words

Economies do not grow in a straight line. They go through repeated waves: a stretch of strong demand and hiring, then a slowdown, then a slump, then a revival. Each wave is a business cycle. No two waves have the same length or depth, which is why they are hard to predict.

  • A business cycle (also economic cycle) is an economy-wide fluctuation in production or activity over several months or years around a long-term growth trend. It is not regular or predictable like a pendulum; its timing is random.
  • Characteristics: it is synchronic (industries move up and down at about the same time); wave-like; recurring (boom is followed by depression and then boom again); no boom or depression lasts forever; effects are pervasive; the downturn is more sudden and violent than the upturn.

2/3★ Must-learnFour phases

In plain words

Follow one cycle. In a boom everyone is buying and hiring and prices rise. Then demand weakens: stocks pile up and firms cut jobs (recession). The slump deepens to its lowest point (depression / trough). Finally cheap credit and replacement demand lift activity again (recovery).

Module B · Unit 17Monetary Policy and Fiscal Policy8 topics · 7 ★ must-learn · 1 ☆ good to know

1/8★ Must-learnMonetary policy: meaning and framework

In plain words

Monetary policy is how the central bank steers the economy through money and interest rates. Making credit cheaper encourages spending and growth; making it dearer cools prices. Since 2016 the RBI has a clear job: keep CPI inflation near 4%, anywhere between 2% and 6% being acceptable, while keeping growth in mind.

  • Monetary policy is the RBI's control over the supply of money, availability of money and cost of money (interest rate) to achieve growth and stability. Expansionary policy raises money supply and lowers interest rates (used in a recession); contractionary policy cuts money supply and raises rates (used against inflation).
  • The primary objective is price stability, keeping growth in mind. The flexible inflation targeting (FIT) framework got a statutory basis when the RBI Act, 1934 was amended in May 2016. The Government sets the inflation target once every five years, in consultation with the RBI.
  • Current target: 4% CPI inflation with a tolerance band of ±2% (2% to 6%) for 1 April 2026 to 31 March 2031, retained by the Government in March 2026. The earlier terms were 5 Aug 2016 to 31 Mar 2021 and 1 Apr 2021 to 31 Mar 2026, each with the same 4 ± 2.
  • Operating framework: the MPC sets the policy repo rate; day-to-day liquidity management aims to anchor the weighted average call rate (WACR), the operating target, around the repo rate. Repo changes pass through the money market to the financial system and then to aggregate demand, which drives inflation and growth.

2/8★ Must-learnMonetary Policy Committee (MPC)

In plain words

Instead of the Governor alone deciding the repo rate, a six-member committee votes on it, so the decision is shared and recorded. Three members come from the RBI and three are outside experts appointed by the Government. If votes tie, the Governor has the deciding (casting) vote.

  • Constituted by the Central Government under Section 45ZB of the RBI Act. It has six members: three from the RBI (Governor as chairperson ex officio, the Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy, and one RBI officer nominated by the Central Board) and three external members appointed by the Government, who hold office for four years. The first MPC was constituted on 29 Sep 2016.
  • Present MPC (63rd meeting, 5–7 Oct 2026): Sanjay Malhotra (Governor), Dr Poonam Gupta (Deputy Governor), Shri Indranil Bhattacharyya (RBI Executive Director), and external members Dr Nagesh Kumar, Shri Saugata Bhattacharya and Prof. Ram Singh.
  • Process: meets at least four times a year; quorum is four; each member has one vote and the Governor has a casting vote on a tie; minutes are published on the 14th day (resolution, each member's vote and statement); the RBI publishes the Monetary Policy Report every six months (sources of inflation and a 6–18 month forecast). Members observe a blackout period from seven days before the decision day to seven days after the announcement. RBI announces policy bi-monthly.
  • Failure to meet the target: average inflation above the upper limit or below the lower limit for three consecutive quarters. Under Section 45ZN the RBI must report to the Government the reasons, the remedial actions and the time needed, within one month.

7/8★ Must-learnFiscal policy

In plain words

If monetary policy is the RBI's steering wheel, fiscal policy is the Government's: it spends and taxes to push the economy. In a slowdown the Government spends more or cuts taxes (expansionary); in an inflationary boom it spends less or raises taxes (contractionary).

  • Fiscal policy is the Government's use of taxation and expenditure to influence growth, employment, inflation and investment. Keynes was the first to link fiscal policy to economic performance. Its two basic instruments are taxation and government expenditure.
  • Taxes affect disposable income, consumption, savings and investment, and prices. Government expenditure has two kinds: purchases of goods and services (roads, salaries) and transfer payments (support to the poor, unemployed and elderly), which do not buy output.
  • Primary objectives in India: promote economic growth; maintain price stability. Additional objectives: mobilise resources, promote allocative efficiency, reduce inequality in income and wealth, promote private investment.
  • To fight inflation the Government uses contractionary fiscal policy (cut spending or raise taxes), plus supply-side steps.
Remember

Monetary policy = RBI (money and interest). Fiscal policy = Government (tax and spend).

8/8★ Must-learnFRBM Act

In plain words

Governments are tempted to spend more than they earn. The Government also has an escape clause for emergencies (a war, a pandemic).

  • Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2003: enacted in 2003, in force from 5 July 2004. Aim: long-term macro stability, prudential debt management, transparent fiscal management and a medium-term framework.

  • Escape clause: invoked in the COVID year, when the fiscal deficit target was raised from 3.3% to 3.8% for 2019-20 (RE) and 2020-21 (BE).

1/5★ Must-learnSystem of National Accounts (SNA) and India's GDP series

In plain words

GDP is only meaningful if countries count it the same way. The System of National Accounts is the rulebook they follow, and the base year is the year whose prices are used to strip out inflation. When the economy changes, the base year is moved forward, which is why India has a 2011-12 series and now a 2022-23 series.

  • The SNA is the internationally agreed framework (developed by the UN with the IMF, OECD, World Bank and Eurostat) for compiling national accounts so that data are consistent and comparable. The latest edition used is SNA 2008, an update of SNA 1993. The SNA advises revising the base year periodically.
  • India: the base year moved from 2004-05 to 2011-12 in the series released on 30 Jan 2015, aligned to SNA 2008. The new series moved the headline growth measure to GDP at constant market prices (earlier factor cost), and sector value added to GVA at basic prices. It also used company accounts from the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA21) and wider financial-sector coverage (SEBI, PFRDA, IRDAI data).
  • Now: MoSPI released a newer GDP series with base year 2022-23 on 27 Feb 2026 (see Unit 1). The base year follows the year of the NSO's employment-unemployment survey, hence it was revised about every five years.
  • The National Statistical Office (NSO) was formed on 23 May 2019 by merging the CSO and NSSO, under MoSPI.

In plain words

One year's output can be counted three ways, and the answer must be the same: add up what is spent (expenditure), add up what is earned (income), or add up the value added at each stage of production (output).   • GVA versus GDP: GVA is the supply-side measure (sectoral value added less intermediate consumption); GDP is the demand-side measure. GDP = GVA + net product taxes (taxes − subsidies). GDP is better for comparing countries; GVA is better for comparing sectors and for quarterly sector data.
Remember

Basic price excludes product taxes; market price includes them. Production taxes enter at the factor-cost-to-basic-price step; product taxes at the basic-to-market step.

The big G, N, D labels are just two switches. Domestic ↔ National: add income earned by residents abroad and subtract what foreigners earn here (NFIA). Gross ↔ Net: subtract depreciation, the wear on machines. 5/5☆ Good to knowUses and limits of GDP

  • GDP is an aggregate: it says nothing about distribution of income. A high GDP can coexist with skewed income and regional disparity.
  • Higher GDP is not higher welfare. Health, education and sanitation need to be read alongside it, for example through the Human Development Index. GDP also ignores financial exclusion and environmental damage; green GDP tries to adjust for this.
  • For an export-oriented economy that earns a lot from abroad, GNP is the more accurate income measure.
  • Useful ratios: GFCF/GDP = share of investment; saving/GDP = saving rate; saving/GFCF = how much of investment is funded by domestic saving; compensation of employees/GVA = labour share; operating surplus/GVA = capital share.

1/5★ Must-learnBudget basics

In plain words

The Budget is the Government's yearly statement of expected income and spending. It is not a word in the Constitution: Article 112 calls it the Annual Financial Statement. The Government keeps its money in three places: the Consolidated Fund (nothing can be spent from it without Parliament's approval), the Public Account (money held as banker, such as provident funds) and the Contingency Fund (a fund for urgent unforeseen needs).

  • The Union Budget is the annual statement of the Government's receipts and expenditure, presented in Parliament by the Union Finance Minister. Since 2017 it is presented on 1 February (earlier the last working day of February), so that it can be implemented from 1 April. The Railway Budget was merged with the Union Budget from 2017.
  • Constitutional basis: Article 112 (Annual Financial Statement); Article 266(1) Consolidated Fund of India; Article 266(2) Public Account of India (withdrawals need no parliamentary appropriation); Article 267 Contingency Fund of India, kept at the President's disposal, with a corpus of Rs 30,000 cr (raised from Rs 500 cr by the Finance Bill 2021).

3/5★ Must-learnExpenditure

In plain words

Spending that is used up and leaves nothing behind (salaries, pensions, interest, subsidies) is revenue expenditure, like a household paying rent and electricity bills. Spending that builds something lasting (a highway, a rail line) is capital expenditure, like taking a loan to buy a shop. Borrowing for capital spending builds the economy; borrowing just to meet running costs risks a debt problem.

  • Revenue expenditure does not create assets: interest payments, defence (revenue part), subsidies (food, fertiliser, petroleum), grants to states and UTs, pensions, police, economic, social and general services.
  • Capital expenditure creates assets or reduces liabilities: defence capital outlay, loans to public enterprises, loans to states and UTs and to foreign governments, other capital outlay.
  • Effective capital expenditure = capital expenditure + grants-in-aid for creation of capital assets.

In plain words

Module C · Unit 20Overview of the Indian Financial System5 topics · 5 ★ must-learn

1/5★ Must-learnStructure of the financial system

In plain words

Every economy has savers with spare money and borrowers who need it. The financial system is the machinery that connects them. It has four parts: institutions (banks, NBFCs, insurers, mutual funds), markets (where money and securities are traded), instruments (shares, bonds, treasury bills, derivatives) and services (merchant banking, credit rating, factoring, leasing). Its job is to move savings into investment, to turn short-term deposits into long-term loans (maturity transformation) and to spread risk across many borrowers (risk transformation). The informal system (moneylenders, chit funds) is quick and paperwork-free, but unregulated and often very costly for the borrower.

  • The financial system moves savings from surplus units (households) to deficit units (firms, government) through institutions, markets, instruments and regulators.
  • Informal sector: moneylenders, chit funds, pawnbrokers, indigenous bankers — unregulated by RBI/SEBI. Formal sector: regulated by RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA, Ministry of Finance.
  • Four market segments: money market (short-term, up to 1 year), forex market, capital market (long-term; equity and debt), and insurance market.
MCQ

1/8★ Must-learnDefinitions and legal basis

In plain words

A bank, in law, is a company that takes deposits from the public (to lend or invest) which the depositor can take back on demand and withdraw by cheque. Only such a company may use the word "bank". A scheduled bank is one listed in the Second Schedule of the RBI Act; in return for meeting conditions it gets access to RBI loans and the clearing system.

  • BR Act s.5(1)(b): "banking" = accepting, for the purpose of lending or investment, deposits of money from the public, repayable on demand or otherwise, and withdrawable by cheque, draft, order or otherwise.
  • s.5(c): banking company = a company that transacts the business of banking. s.6: forms of business banks may do. s.7: only a banking company may use the words bank, banker, banking in its name.
  • Banks are scheduled if included in the Second Schedule of the RBI Act (paid-up capital and reserves of at least Rs 5 lakh and affairs not detrimental to depositors); others are non-scheduled. Scheduled banks get RBI refinance/LAF access.

In plain words

Microfinance means very small loans and savings services for poor households that cannot offer collateral. Models: I – bank lends directly to SHG; II – NGO/facilitator-formed SHGs, bank lends to SHG; III – NGO acts as financial intermediary (bank → NGO → SHG). In plain words

Module D · Unit 29Financial Markets1 topic · 1 ★ must-learn

1/1★ Must-learnSegments and functions of financial markets

In plain words

A financial market is a meeting place for buyers and sellers of money and securities. Split it by time (money market up to one year, capital market beyond), by stage (primary: new securities sold for the first time; secondary: existing securities traded between investors) and by product. The primary market raises fresh funds for the issuer; the secondary market only gives investors liquidity and a fair price, and the issuer receives nothing from it.

Example: when a company such as Zomato (2021) or LIC (2022) launched an IPO, the application money went straight into the company's account to fund its business: that is the primary market. Once the shares were listed and you sold yours to another investor, the company received nothing; ownership just changed hands: that is the secondary market. Two market structures: an exchange-traded market (BSE, NSE) has standardised contracts, an open order book and a clearing corporation that guarantees settlement; an over-the-counter (OTC) market is a direct, customised deal between two parties (forwards, swaps, bilateral loans) and carries the risk that the other side defaults. Broadly, RBI looks after the money market and SEBI the capital market.

  • A financial market is where financial assets are bought and sold; prices are fixed by demand and supply — the process is called price discovery. Till the early 1990s Indian markets had administered prices, entry barriers, low liquidity and high costs; the 1991 reforms brought free pricing, new instruments, wider participation and better trading, clearing and settlement.
  • Segments (book list): credit, money, debt, forex, derivatives, capital, insurance and mutual-fund markets.

In plain words

3/3★ Must-learnRepo, tri-party repo, BRDS, LTRO and TLTRO

In plain words

A repo is a loan against securities. One party sells securities today and agrees to buy them back later at a slightly higher price; the difference is the interest, called the repo rate. For the borrower it is cheap secured borrowing; for the lender it is safe because it holds securities as collateral. A reverse repo is the same deal seen from the lender's side. When the RBI lends cash to banks through a repo it injects liquidity; when it takes cash in, it drains liquidity. In a tri-party repo a third party (the tri-party agent, e.g. CCIL) looks after collateral selection, settlement and custody, so the two main parties need not trust each other's operations. LTROs and TLTROs are longer-tenor repo-type loans from the RBI to banks (the targeted version for specified sectors).

  • Repo = sale of a security with agreement to repurchase at a future date at a predetermined price (collateralised borrowing); the same deal is a reverse repo for the other party. Eligible collateral: Central/State G-secs, listed corporate bonds/debentures (not own or related-party paper), CPs, CDs, units of debt ETFs. Banks use repo to manage CRR/SLR positions.
  • Tri-party repo (RBI, August 2017): a tri-party agent handles collateral selection, settlement, custody and revaluation but does not share the credit risk. Agents: CCIL and NSE (approved). Agent needs RBI authorisation, paid-up equity Rs 25 cr, 5 years' experience. Purpose: depth in the corporate bond repo market.
  • BRDS (Bills Rediscounting Scheme): banks raise funds against discounted genuine trade bills by issuing a Derivative Usance Promissory Note (DUPN); tenor 15–90 days; house bills and finance-company bills are ineligible.

1/5★ Must-learnGovernment securities market

In plain words

G-secs are loans to the Government, dated (long-term, with coupons) or T-bills (short-term). They carry no default risk, are the benchmark for all other interest rates, and banks must hold a share of them as SLR.

Dirty vs clean price: clean price is the quoted price; dirty (invoice) price = clean price + accrued interest since the last coupon. Example: clean 102.50 + accrued 1.20 = 103.70 actually paid.

  • A deep G-sec market gives the risk-free benchmark for pricing money-market and corporate paper, and helps the Government manage its borrowing and maturity profile. G-secs = Central dated securities, Treasury bills and State Development Loans (SDLs); issued to finance the fiscal deficit and cash mismatches.
  • Dated securities: issued at par (Rs 100), fixed half-yearly coupon set at auction, redeemed at par; named by coupon and maturity year, e.g. 7.99% GOI 2027. Issued for less than 1 year (T-bills) up to 40 years and above. A 10-year security issued in 2018 at 7.85% is "7.85 GOI 2028".
  • Who holds: banks are the largest holders (share falling: 47.25% in Mar 2010 → 40.41% in Mar 2020), with insurers rising (22.16% → 25.09%). Banks hold mostly up to 10-year paper; insurers dominate the very long end (about 90% of paper above 25 years).
  • Holding forms: SGL account with RBI, CSGL account with a bank/PD, or demat. Anyone resident in India may invest; NRIs, OCIs and FPIs as per FEMA/RBI rules; minimum Rs 10,000 face value in multiples of Rs 10,000.
  • Secondary market: telephone market and NDS-OM (RBI's anonymous order-matching system for G-secs, T-bills, SDLs); settlement through CCIL which guarantees it; trading 9 am–5 pm on working days. "When Issued" trading allowed before actual issue; individuals, HUFs, NRIs and OCIs can take only long positions.

2/5★ Must-learnAuction of G-secs

In plain words

The RBI sells new G-secs on behalf of the Government by auction: bidders offer a price or a yield, the RBI ranks the bids, and a cut-off decides who gets allotted. In a uniform-price auction every successful bidder pays the cut-off price; in a multiple-price auction each pays what they bid. Non-competitive bids let small investors take part without quoting a price: they get securities at the cut-off. This is how the Government borrows to finance its deficit, and the cut-off yield becomes the benchmark for other interest rates.

  • RBI conducts auctions on E-Kuber. Two methods: uniform price (every accepted bidder pays/receives the cut-off yield/price) and multiple price (each accepted bidder pays their own bid). Winner's curse – paying more than the clearing level – is associated with the multiple-price auction.
  • Price-based auctions (re-issues: coupon fixed, bidders quote price per Rs 100) vs yield-based (new issue: highest accepted yield becomes the coupon). Green-shoe option may be exercised above the notified amount.
  • Non-competitive bidding by retail investors through an aggregator; allotted at the weighted-average price of accepted competitive bids; capped at a notified percentage of the issue. The book states bids can range from Rs 10,000 to Rs 2 crore face value.
Remember

Yield-based auction: highest accepted yield = coupon for the whole issue. Uniform price → one price for all.

3/5★ Must-learnBond valuation and the four theorems

In plain words

A bond's price is the present value of its future interest and principal. When market yields rise, fixed coupons look less attractive, so the bond price falls; when yields fall, the price rises. That is why price and yield move opposite ways.

Current yield = annual coupon ÷ current market price × 100 (it ignores the gain or loss at maturity). Yield to maturity (YTM) = the total annual return if you hold the bond until maturity, counting coupons and the gain or loss on price. Why prices fall when rates rise: hold a 10-year bond of face value Rs 100 with a 7% coupon. If new bonds start paying 8%, nobody will pay Rs 100 for your 7% bond; its price must fall until it also yields 8%. Computed: price at 8% yield = Rs 93.29, so the Rs 7 coupon is a current yield of about 7.5% on that price. Accrued interest: interest earned since the last coupon date. Worked: 8% annual-coupon bond, face value Rs 1,000, clean price Rs 985, 146 days since the last coupon. Accrued = 80 × 146/365 = Rs 32.00; dirty (invoice) price = 985 + 32 = Rs 1,017.00.

  • Bond price = present value of all future cash flows (coupons + face value) at the market yield. Coupon rate never changes; price moves inversely with yield. Coupon = market rate → par; market rate above coupon → discount; below → premium.
  • Worked example: 8% annual-coupon bond, 5 years left, market yield 9%. Price = 8 × annuity factor (3.8897) + 100 × discount factor (0.6499) = 31.1176 + 64.99 = Rs 96.1076 using table factors (exact discounting gives about 96.11); below par because yield = Rs 96.1076 (below par, as yield > coupon)gt; coupon.
  • Theorem 1: price and yield are inversely related. 2: a fall in yield raises price by more than an equal rise in yield lowers it (convexity). 3: longer maturity → greater price sensitivity. 4: for equal maturity, the lower-coupon bond is more sensitive.
MCQ

1/5★ Must-learnProfile and evolution of the forex market

In plain words

The foreign exchange market is where one currency is swapped for another. Its price is the exchange rate. India moved from a fixed rate, to a basket-linked rate, to a dual rate (1992) and finally to a market-determined rate (1993).

Quotes and settlement: a direct quote gives home currency per unit of foreign currency (INR per USD, India's convention); an indirect quote is the reverse. Settlement: cash = same day, TOM = next working day, spot = two working days, forward = beyond spot. A bank's nostro account is its account abroad in foreign currency; vostro is a foreign bank's rupee account with it.

Bid and ask: the bank buys foreign currency at its bid rate and sells at its ask (offer) rate; the difference is the spread, the dealer's margin. In a direct quote the bank buys low and sells high. Loro means "their account": one bank's reference to a third bank's account held with another bank (for example, Canara Bank referring to SBI's nostro account with Citibank when settling a trade). Remember the trap: spot settlement is T+2, not T+1. Example: a Surat diamond exporter sells Rs 1 crore of diamonds to a buyer in New York. The buyer pays in dollars, the New York bank credits SBI's dollar (nostro) account there, and SBI Mumbai credits the exporter in rupees. The nostro account is the pipe.

  • Foreign exchange (FEMA s.2(n)) = foreign currency, deposits/balances payable in foreign currency, drafts, TCs, LCs and bills expressed in rupees but payable in foreign currency, and those drawn abroad but payable in rupees.
  • Mostly an OTC, 24-hour, inter-bank market that took shape in 1971 when fixed rates gave way to floating ones. Instruments: spot, forward, futures, swaps, options. The book quotes global turnover of about USD 6.6 trillion a day (BIS 2019 survey; later surveys are higher).
  • India: market began with the RBI's 1978 permission to trade intraday (square position only at close). 1975–92: rupee set by RBI against a weighted basket of currencies. 1–3 Jul 1991: two-step devaluation. LERMS (dual exchange rate; Rangarajan High Level Committee on BoP) in Mar 1992; unified market-determined rate from 1 Mar 1993; current-account convertibility Aug 1994 (IMF Article VIII); Sodhani Committee (Expert Group on Foreign Exchange, Nov 1994) widened the market.

2/5☆ Good to knowCharacteristics, participants, LIBOR and ARRs

In plain words

Forex trades round the clock, mostly between banks. LIBOR, once the benchmark for global loans, was discontinued after a rate-rigging scandal and replaced by alternative reference rates such as SOFR.

4/5★ Must-learnForeign Exchange Management Act, 1999

In plain words

It separates current account transactions (trade, travel, remittances for living costs: generally free unless restricted) from capital account transactions (investment, borrowing, buying property abroad: permitted only as the rules allow). Contraventions are civil, not criminal. It came into force on 1 June 2000, and the RBI makes most of the rules under it.

Module D · Unit 34Interconnectedness of Markets and Market Dynamics5 topics · 5 ★ must-learn

1/5★ Must-learnIntegration of financial markets

In plain words

Markets are connected: a rise in the repo rate moves call money, T-bill and bond yields, loan rates and exchange rates. A shock in one market spreads to others. The more connected the markets, the faster policy works, and the faster crises travel.

Three links. (1) Money market ↔ bond market: when interbank cash dries up, short-term yields spike and the whole yield curve moves up. (2) Bond market ↔ equity market: higher bond yields raise the return investors demand from shares, so share prices fall and money shifts to safe bonds. (3) Money market ↔ forex market: higher domestic rates attract foreign money, which pushes the rupee up. Risk: unsystematic risk is specific to one company or sector and can be diversified away; systematic (market) risk, such as a rate hike or a pandemic, hits everything and cannot. Chain reaction: a US Fed rate hike raises US Treasury yields, foreign investors sell Indian shares and buy dollars, the rupee weakens, and the RBI may have to raise the repo rate to defend it, which raises home-loan EMIs.

  • Integration = unifying markets so that risk-adjusted returns on assets of similar maturity converge across segments; it needs unimpeded access for participants. It has grown through deregulation, globalisation and IT. Shocks therefore spread — the "coupling" of economies and contagion (South-East Asian crisis 1997; Global Financial Crisis 2008-09).
  • Why markets are interconnected: mobile capital across borders; technology reducing arbitrage gaps; monetary policy moving from quantitative controls to price-based tools (policy rate); harmonised prudential regulation.
  • Importance: transmits price signals and monetary policy; promotes savings, investment and growth; supports an international financial centre; improves competition, efficiency, stability and innovation; imposes market discipline; promotes modern payment technology. Without integration there would be arbitrage and imperfect markets.
  • Heterogeneity (the reason integration matters): national vs international segments, depth/liquidity (money market more liquid than bonds), economic nature of instruments, risk profile (G-secs vs corporate bonds), and different participants.

3/5★ Must-learnBenefits and costs of integration

  • Benefits: savings mobilisation, risk sharing and consumption smoothing, stronger transmission of policy, market-based governance, higher growth and lower volatility; local markets reduce reliance on foreign capital. Global openness can raise productivity and cut intermediation cost; direct investment is more stable than other flows.
  • Costs: contagion through the real channel (domino exposures) and the information channel (panic withdrawals); volatile, pro-cyclical short-term flows; sudden reversals; exchange-rate instability; monetary expansion, inflation and real appreciation from large inflows. This is why the Tarapore Committee capital-account recommendations were put on hold during the 1997 Asian crisis.

4/5★ Must-learnHow the markets move together

  • Money market: policy-rate change → WACR (weighted average call rate) moves almost at once → other money-market rates follow at spreads; WACR–policy-rate spread widens near financial year-end. 10-year G-sec yields track the repo rate.
  • Credit market: bank lending rates (WALR) still key because India is bank-dominated; pass-through is slowed by administered rates, deposit repricing rigidity, subventions, informal finance, statutory pre-emptions (CRR/SLR) and weak asset quality. Corporate bond yields move with G-sec yields.
  • Capital market: bond yields and equities move inversely (bond yield = opportunity cost of equity: a 7% yield + 5% equity risk premium → 12% hurdle). Indian and US indices (Nifty–S&P 500) move together.
  • Forex market: stock-market fall → FII outflows → rupee depreciation (USD-INR up); equity inflows → rupee strengthens. So the stock-market level and USD-INR move inversely.

1/4★ Must-learnMerchant banking — meaning, history and comparison with commercial banking

In plain words

A merchant banker advises and manages the process of raising money (an IPO, a bond issue) and does not take public deposits or lend like an ordinary bank. It is regulated by SEBI, even if it is a bank's subsidiary.

Contrast with a commercial bank: a commercial bank lends its depositors' money and worries about getting its instalments back. A merchant banker rarely lends its own money. When a company wants Rs 3,000 crore from an IPO, the merchant banker acts like the architect: it does the due diligence, drafts the prospectus, advises on price, markets the issue to investors and coordinates the listing. Trap: merchant bankers are regulated by SEBI, not the RBI, even when the merchant banking arm is a subsidiary of a bank. The book describes four registration categories; that has since been replaced by a single category.

  • Merchant banker = a person engaged in issue management (arranging selling, buying or subscribing securities as manager, consultant, adviser) or corporate advisory service in relation to it. Serves large corporates and wealthy clients, not the general public; mostly fee-based.
  • History: origins in medieval Italy and 17th–18th century France; London merchants financed trade through bill acceptances. In India, before the Companies Act 1956 managing-agency houses were issue houses. Grindlays Bank got the first licence in 1967; Citibank followed in 1970; SBI started a merchant banking division in 1972 (after the Banking Commission 1972 suggestion); ICICI (1973), IFCI (1986), IDBI (1991) among DFIs. Prominence grew in 1983-84 and after 1991.

Module D · Unit 36Derivatives Market4 topics · 4 ★ must-learn

1/4★ Must-learnMeaning, history and participants

In plain words

A derivative is a contract whose value is derived from something else: a share, an index, a currency, a commodity or an interest rate. Why bother? Three reasons. Hedging: an exporter expecting dollars in three months can lock in today's rate and be safe from a fall in the dollar. Speculation: a trader bets on direction using a small margin. Arbitrage: profit from a price gap between two markets. The four basic types are forwards, futures, options and swaps; the notes below take them one by one.

  • Derivative (RBI definition): value changes with a specified underlying (interest rate, security price, commodity price, exchange rate, index, credit rating); needs little or no initial net investment; settled at a future date. SCRA s.2(ac) also defines it as a security derived from a debt instrument/share/loan/risk instrument/contract for differences, or a contract deriving value from prices or index of prices of underlying securities.
  • India: Bombay Cotton Trade Association started commodity futures in 1875 (the first Indian derivative was in cotton); cash settlement and options banned in 1952; Securities Laws (Amendment) Ordinance 1995 lifted the prohibition on options; L.C. Gupta Committee recommended financial derivatives; trading began June 2000 (Nifty index futures on NSE, 12 Jun 2000); Sensex options 4 Jun 2001; options on individual stocks Jul 2001; single-stock futures Nov 2001.
  • Participants: hedgers (reduce risk; majority), speculators (leverage bets), arbitrageurs (exploit price gaps). There is no "guarantor" participant.
  • Functions: price discovery, risk transfer, hedging, lower transaction cost, access to otherwise unavailable markets, leverage (futures margin roughly 20–40% in the book; option buyer pays only premium).
  • Exchange-traded contracts are standardised, margined and guaranteed by the clearing house, transparent; OTC contracts are customised, bilateral, less regulated (oversight increased after the 2008 crisis). Currency futures are exchange-traded; swaps, forwards and exotics (caps, collars) are OTC.

3/4★ Must-learnOptions

In plain words

An option gives the buyer a right, not an obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) at a fixed price. The buyer pays a premium for this right; the seller (writer) has the obligation. A put protects against falling prices; a call profits from rising prices. Index and stock options in India are European style, exercisable only on expiry.

Example (flat booking): you pay a builder a non-refundable token of Rs 2 lakh (the premium) to fix the price of a flat at Rs 1 crore (the strike price) for six months. If the flat's price jumps to Rs 1.5 crore, you buy at Rs 1 crore: profit = 1.5 cr − 1 cr − 0.02 cr = Rs 48 lakh. If the price falls to Rs 70 lakh, you walk away and lose only the Rs 2 lakh token. That is a call: right to buy. A put is the mirror image, the right to sell. Forwards vs futures: a forward is tailored, OTC, settles on the maturity date and carries default risk; a future is standardised, exchange-traded, settles daily through mark-to-market and is guaranteed by the clearing corporation.

  • An option gives the buyer the right, not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) at the strike price on or before expiry; the buyer pays a premium to the seller (writer), who has the obligation. American option: exercisable any time up to expiry; European: only on expiry. Open interest = contracts outstanding.
  • Call example: strike Rs 1,500, premium Rs 100 → breakeven Rs 1,600; at Rs 1,800 profit = (1,800 − 1,500) − 100 = Rs 200; below Rs 1,500 the buyer loses only the Rs 100 premium.
  • Put example: strike Rs 2,300, premium Rs 125; at Rs 1,800 profit = 2,300 − 1,800 − 125 = Rs 375; above 2,300 loss is the premium.
  • Buyer's loss is limited to premium; seller's gain is limited to premium.

4/4★ Must-learnSwaps and credit default swaps

In plain words

A swap is an agreement to exchange cash flows (for example fixed interest for floating interest). A credit default swap is insurance against a borrower defaulting.

  • Swap = exchange of cash flows between two parties on a notional amount. Currency swap: principal and interest in one currency exchanged for another, principal swapped at start and re-exchanged at maturity at the same rate. Interest rate swap (IRS): only interest streams exchanged (fixed ↔ floating), no principal exchange; used to cut cost or hedge. Example: bank funds a floating-rate loan with a fixed-rate FCNR(B) deposit → uses a floating-to-fixed IRS to remove the risk of falling SOFR.
  • Currency swap worked example (book): ABC (Indian parent) borrows Rs 75 lakh at 12%; XYZ (US parent) borrows USD 100,000 at 4%; swapped at Rs 75/USD. ABC's subsidiary would otherwise pay 6% on USD (USD 6,000) → pays USD 4,000, saving USD 2,000; XYZ's subsidiary would pay 15% on Rs 75 lakh (Rs 11,25,000) → pays 12% (Rs 9,00,000), saving Rs 2,25,000. A swap bank usually intermediates.
  • Credit default swap (CDS): the protection buyer pays a periodic premium to a protection seller, who pays if the reference entity defaults — it transfers credit risk (not cash flows). Minimum three parties: reference entity (borrower), protection buyer (bond holder), protection seller (often a bank/insurer). Counterparty risk remains (AIG, Lehman, Bear Stearns in 2008).
  • RBI: draft guidelines Feb 2021; final RBI (Credit Derivatives) Directions, 2022 issued 10 Feb 2022, effective 9 May 2022. Under the framework retail users may buy protection only to hedge an underlying exposure; non-retail users (insurers, pension funds, mutual funds, AIFs, FPIs, etc.) can also sell protection; market-makers (scheduled commercial banks except SFBs, payments banks, LABs and RRBs; larger NBFCs/SPDs; EXIM, NABARD, NHB, SIDBI) quote both sides. Reference obligations are rated INR corporate bonds, CPs, CDs, short NCDs; no related-party deals; OTC trades are reported to CCIL's trade repository.
  • ISDA Master Agreement: standard documentation for OTC derivatives (from the 1985 Swaps Code); the pre-printed master is customised only through the Schedule, with confirmations for each trade and credit support documents.

Module D · Unit 37Factoring, Forfaiting and TReDS3 topics · 3 ★ must-learn

1/3★ Must-learnFactoring

In plain words

A business sells goods on credit and waits for payment. In factoring it sells its unpaid invoices to a factor, who pays most of the money at once and collects from the buyer later. Factoring is regulated in India under the Factoring Regulation Act, 2011 (amended 2021).

Recourse vs non-recourse, worked: a seller invoices a buyer for Rs 50 lakh on 90 days' credit and factors it. The factor advances 85% = Rs 42.5 lakh at once, holds the 15% reserve (Rs 7.5 lakh) until the buyer pays, and charges a commission (say 1.5% = Rs 75,000). If the buyer goes bankrupt on day 75: under recourse factoring the factor takes the money back from the seller; under non-recourse (full-service) factoring the factor bears the loss and cannot demand the advance back. Factoring is normally short-term (up to about 150 days in the book), domestic and mostly with recourse; forfaiting is medium-term, for exports, and always without recourse.

  • Factoring = financing against invoices on buyers, where the factor also does credit control, sales-ledger administration and collection. Governed in India by the Factoring Regulation Act, 2011 (s.2(j) defines "factoring business" as acquiring receivables by assignment or financing against security interest over receivables; it excludes ordinary bank credit against receivables). It is short-term, on open-account sales, and works by assignment of debts.
  • Types: recourse (factor recovers from the seller if the buyer defaults) and non-recourse (factor bears bad debt and gives credit protection; the book says non-recourse domestic factoring is not permitted in India). Also domestic vs international (cross-border). Full-service factoring = finance + ledger + collection + credit protection.
  • Domestic factoring (book): advance about 80% of invoice value (the book also cites 85%; margin up to 20%), balance on collection; maximum credit period 150 days including a 60-day grace period; factor fixes sub-limits per buyer; MIS reports (debtor ageing, statements) to seller.
  • Fees: finance charge on the prepaid amount outstanding (monthly) and service fee of 0.1%–0.3% of invoice value (collection, ledger, MIS).
  • International factoring: two-factor system (export factor + import factor: prepayment, sales-ledger, credit protection, collection); single-factor (import factor pays export factor only if the buyer fails to pay within 90 days); direct export factoring (only an export factor); direct import factoring (only an import factor). There is no "three-factor" system.
  • Bills discounting vs factoring: bills discounting is transaction-based, always with recourse, needs a strong balance sheet/collateral, collection by the exporter, no assignment; factoring is ledger-based, may be without recourse, factor collects, includes MIS and credit insurance, involves assignment of debt, and can be done with a weaker balance sheet if receivables are good.
  • Advantages: instant finance against invoices, low margin, higher turnover of receivables, cash-discount purchasing, ledger and follow-up handled by the factor.

2/3★ Must-learnForfaiting

In plain words

Forfaiting is selling medium-term export receivables to a forfaiter, who pays the exporter cash immediately and takes the risk of non-payment. It is always without recourse to the exporter, usually for 1-7 years.

  • Forfaiting = discounting of export receivables (bills of exchange, promissory notes, LC drafts), usually medium-term (1–5 years), without recourse to the exporter, at a fixed discount, up to 100% of invoice value. Name from French à forfait – "surrendering of rights". Typical minimum size about USD 250,000. In India only EXIM Bank and AD Category-I banks (non-recourse basis) may handle it.
  • Mechanism: forfaiter commits → exporter ships → importer's bank avalises/co-accepts the bill → exporter sells the avalised bill to the forfaiter and receives cash less discount → forfaiter collects from the importer's bank at maturity. Via EXIM Bank, the forfaiting charges are shown in the invoice/EDF/shipping bill for Customs.
  • Fees: discount fee (interest, linked to an international benchmark such as LIBOR/SOFR-type rates) and commitment fee (0.5%–1.5% p.a. on the unutilised amount until discounting or validity ends).
  • Advantages: 100% finance without recourse and without using credit lines, removes interest-rate, currency, credit and political risk, saves administration, lets exporter offer credit terms.

3/3★ Must-learnTReDS — Trade Receivables Discounting System

In plain words

TReDS is an electronic platform where MSME sellers get their invoices to large buyers financed by banks and other financiers, so that small suppliers are paid quickly. Discounting is without recourse to the MSME.

The three platforms are RXIL (Receivables Exchange of India), M1xchange and Invoicemart. Module D · Unit 38Venture Capital3 topics · 3 ★ must-learn

1/3★ Must-learnConcept, characteristics and evolution

In plain words

Venture capital is money given to young, high-risk, high-growth companies in exchange for a share in the business. The investor bears equity risk and gains only if the company succeeds; it does not charge fixed interest like a bank.

Usual instrument: compulsorily convertible preference shares (CCPS), which convert into equity at a later stage.

Bank vs venture capital: walk into a bank with only an idea and it asks for collateral and three years of audited profit, because it lends depositors' money. A venture capitalist gives the money without collateral or interest in return for a share in the company: if it fails, the founder owes nothing; if it succeeds, the investor's stake grows with it. VCs also give guidance and often a board seat ("smart money"). Stages: seed (idea and prototype, often angel investors) → early (product launched, first institutional round) → expansion/growth (scaling up) → bridge (pre-IPO, preparing to list). Venture capital funds are Category I AIFs.

  • Venture capital (VC) = long-term, risk-bearing investment, mostly equity (also conditional and conventional loans), in young businesses with high growth potential but high uncertainty — "high risk, high return". The VC does not run the business but guides it, and exits when it matures, earning mainly capital gains rather than interest.
  • Conditional loan: no interest; repaid as a royalty on sales once revenue arises; may be partly/fully waived if the venture fails.
  • India: the Bhatt Committee 1972 recommended VC; IFCI's Risk Capital Foundation (1975) was the first VC fund; IDBI Seed Capital Scheme (1976); Technology Policy Statement 1983; ICICI venture scheme 1986; PACT (USAID grant, administered by ICICI); Government VC fund with IDBI from 1 Apr 1986 (funded by the R&D cess); TDICI (ICICI-sponsored, 1988); RCF converted into RCTFC (1988).

Module D · Unit 39Lease Finance and Hire Purchase3 topics · 2 ★ must-learn · 1 ☆ good to know

1/3★ Must-learnLeasing — meaning, evolution and types

In plain words

In a lease, the owner (lessor) gives an asset to the user (lessee) for regular rent while ownership stays with the lessor. A finance lease runs for most of the asset's life; an operating lease is short and cancellable.

Example: an airline leases many of its aircraft on operating leases; when newer, fuel-efficient models arrive it hands the old ones back, and the lessor carries the risk of obsolescence. A transport contractor buys a Rs 40 lakh truck on hire purchase instead: Rs 5 lakh down and 36 monthly instalments, and the registration moves into his name only after the 36th. Key differences: in a lease the lessor owns throughout and claims depreciation, and the lessee deducts the whole rental; in hire purchase the hirer becomes owner only on the last instalment, claims depreciation, and may deduct only the interest part. Until the last instalment the hirer is only a bailee, and if he defaults the owner can repossess the goods.

  • A lease gives the lessee the exclusive right to use an asset owned by the lessor for an agreed period against lease rentals; ownership stays with the lessor. Legally a lease is a bailment under the Indian Contract Act (no separate leasing statute; Transfer of Property Act defines lease of immovable property); GST is levied on lease rentals.
  • Evolution: leasing began in India in 1973 with First Leasing Company of India (Farouk Irani, A.C. Muthiah); 20th Century Finance Corporation (1980); ICICI entered 1983; the G.S. Dahotre Committee shaped RBI guidelines for bank funding of leasing companies; banks were allowed to lease directly in 1994 (SBI, Bank of India set up specialised branches).

2/3☆ Good to knowAdvantages, accounting effect and bank leasing rules

In plain words

Leasing lets a firm use equipment without a large upfront purchase. Banks may lease assets only within RBI's rules.

  • Advantages: assured income to lessor; lessee conserves funds for growth; tax: lessee deducts rentals, lessor claims depreciation; cheaper than many other finance sources.

3/3★ Must-learnHire purchase

In plain words

In hire purchase the user pays instalments and becomes the owner only after the last instalment. Until then the seller owns the goods. The hirer claims depreciation; in a lease the lessor does.

  • In hire purchase the hirer uses the goods, pays the price in instalments (each instalment treated as hire charge) and becomes owner only on paying the last instalment; until then the hire-vendor (owner) holds title and may repossess on default; the hirer may return the goods. Typically a down payment of 20–25% (or an FDR in its place) and EMIs over about 36–48 months.
  • Legal nature: a contract of bailment (Indian Contract Act, 1872) plus an element of sale (Sale of Goods Act, 1930); not a plain sale. The Hire Purchase Act, 1972 was enacted to regulate it (book says finance organisations are controlled by it; a 1989 amendment Bill never came into force). Terms: hire-vendor/hiree = owner; hirer/hire-purchaser = user.
  • Duties of the owner: deliver in time, goods of merchantable quality and to description, have good title, not disturb the hirer. Duties of the hirer: accept delivery, take due care, pay instalments on time, redeliver in same condition if the agreement ends, no disposal before full payment.
  • History: hire purchase in India for almost a century; early companies Commercial Credit Corporation (Madras) and Motor & General Finance (1920s–30s); consumer durables and commercial vehicles.

1/4★ Must-learnCredit rating and credit rating agencies

In plain words

A credit rating is an independent opinion on how likely a borrower is to repay a particular debt. A higher rating means lower risk and a cheaper borrowing cost. Agencies are regulated by SEBI.

CRISIL (1987) was India's first rating agency, followed by ICRA (1991) and CARE (1993); India Ratings, Brickwork, Infomerics and Acuité are other SEBI-registered agencies. Rating vs score: a rating (AAA, AA, … D) is an opinion on one debt instrument or company, regulated by SEBI; a score (300–900) summarises an individual's or firm's repayment history, produced by a credit information company and regulated by the RBI. The four licensed CICs are TransUnion CIBIL, Equifax, Experian and CRIF High Mark. Two borrowers asking for the same home loan illustrate the effect: the one with a long, clean repayment record (score around 790) gets a lower rate and faster sanction, while one with frequent overdue payments (around 620) is refused or charged more.

  • A credit rating is an agency's opinion of an issuer's capacity and willingness to meet interest and principal on a particular instrument, in time. It guides investors; it is not a recommendation to buy, sell or hold. Ratings are forward-looking, in symbols, and kept under continuous surveillance (upgrade/downgrade, "watch").
  • History: John Moody began rating US railroad bonds in 1909; big three globally: Moody's, S&P, Fitch. In India CRISIL (est. 1987, operations January 1988) started credit rating; the book's tie-ups: CRISIL–S&P, ICRA–Moody's, CARE–Fitch (older). Seven SEBI-registered CRAs: CRISIL, India Ratings & Research (formerly Fitch India), ICRA, CARE, Brickwork, Infomerics, Acuité — all RBI-accredited for risk-weighting bank claims.
  • Who pays: the issuer (issuer fees about 95% of CRA revenue) plus an annual surveillance fee; investors do not pay.
  • Benefits: to investors – risk assessment, low-cost information, continuous monitoring, choice; to issuers – easier and cheaper borrowing, credibility for lesser-known firms, financial discipline, better disclosure.
  • Factors rated: ability to service debt (cash flows, liquidity, collateral, promoters), market position, management quality, industry risks, regulatory environment, cost structure, accounting quality, leverage, forex exposure.

1/8★ Must-learnConcept, structure and NAV

In plain words

A mutual fund collects money from many investors, invests it in a basket of securities, and gives each investor units in return. You own a slice of the whole basket rather than of any one security, so one bad stock hurts less. NAV is the value of one unit: (value of the fund's investments minus liabilities and expenses) ÷ number of units. Example: assets Rs 105 cr, expenses due Rs 5 cr, 10 cr units → NAV = (105 − 5) ÷ 10 = Rs 10.00. The fund is a trust: the sponsor sets it up, trustees hold the property for unit holders, the AMC manages the money, and the custodian keeps the securities.

Why pool? One share of an expensive stock may cost over a lakh rupees. If 1,00,000 investors put in Rs 1,000 each, the AMC has Rs 10 crore to spread across dozens of companies, and each investor gets units that represent a slice of the whole portfolio. Fuller NAV example: portfolio Rs 150 cr + cash and receivables Rs 10 cr = assets Rs 160 cr; current liabilities Rs 5 cr + accrued expenses Rs 1 cr = Rs 6 cr; net assets Rs 154 cr; 1 crore units → NAV = Rs 154 per unit. Size labels (SEBI): large-cap = top 100 companies by market value, mid-cap = 101st to 250th, small-cap = 251st onwards. A liquid fund invests only in paper maturing within 91 days; an overnight fund in 1-day paper.

  • A mutual fund pools money from the public by issuing units and invests it in securities as per the objectives in the offer document. Investors are unit holders; profit or loss is shared in proportion to units held. Must be registered with SEBI before collecting funds.
  • Four-tier structure: set up as a trust by a sponsor (like a promoter) → trustees hold the property for unit holders and supervise the AMC → AMC (SEBI-approved) manages investments → custodian (SEBI-registered) holds the securities.
  • Independence rules (1996 Regulations, the textbook position): at least two-thirds of trustee directors and 50% of AMC directors independent of the sponsor. The 2026 Regulations keep the independence idea but narrow the test to whether a director is an "associate" of the sponsor; exact percentages under the 2026 text not confirmed.
  • NAV per unit = (market value of scheme securities − expenses) ÷ number of units. NAV is published daily on AMFI/AMC websites by 9 p.m. (fund-of-funds: by 10 a.m. next business day). Rounding: 4 decimals for index, debt, liquid and money-market schemes; 2 decimals for equity-oriented and balanced schemes (more allowed).

7/8★ Must-learnReturns, expenses, loads and SIP/STP/SWP

In plain words

SIP invests a fixed sum regularly; STP moves money from one scheme to another; SWP withdraws a fixed amount regularly. The expense ratio is the yearly cost charged to the scheme.

  • Absolute return = (current value − invested) ÷ invested × 100; ignores time, so use for under 1 year. Example: Rs 7,00,000 vs Rs 5,00,000 → (700000−500000)/500000 = 40%.
  • Annualised return (CAGR) = (Current value ÷ Beginning value)1/n − 1, n = years; used beyond 1 year; XIRR for irregular flows.
  • Expense ratio = annual operating expenses (administration, management, advertising) as % of daily net assets; 1% means 1% of assets goes to costs every year; disclosed in the offer document; limits set by SEBI and fungible across expense heads. Update: SEBI's new Mutual Funds Regulations, effective 1 April 2026, introduce a base expense ratio for the AMC fee, with brokerage, STT, stamp duty and exchange charges disclosed separately, and cut brokerage caps to 6 bps (cash) and 2 bps (derivatives).
  • Load: entry/exit charge as % of NAV. NAV Rs 10, 1% load → buy at Rs 10.10, sell at Rs 9.90. Entry load is banned by SEBI (since 2009); exit load may apply and is credited to the scheme. No-load fund = no charge at entry/exit.

1/7★ Must-learnPrinciples of insurance

In plain words

Insurance works on a few simple principles: you must have a real interest in what you insure (insurable interest), you must disclose everything honestly (utmost good faith), and you can be compensated only for your actual loss (indemnity). Life insurance is not an indemnity contract because a life cannot be valued in rupees.

Term vs endowment: for the same Rs 10,000 premium a 30-year-old might get only a small cover from an endowment policy (part of the premium goes to savings), but a very large cover from a term plan, which pays only if he dies in the term and returns nothing if he survives. Term is pure protection; endowment is savings mixed with a small cover. In general insurance, the other principles worth knowing are proximate cause (the dominant cause of the loss decides liability) and subrogation (after paying, the insurer steps into your rights against whoever caused the loss).

  • Insurance = contract in which the insurer promises to indemnify the insured's financial loss on a contingent event, in return for a premium; the contract is the policy. Two broad classes: life and non-life (general).

Module D · Unit 43Pension Products6 topics · 6 ★ must-learn

1/6★ Must-learnPension basics

In plain words

A pension is income after retirement. Schemes differ on who funds them (employee, employer, both), how the money is invested and how the payout is taken (lump sum, monthly annuity).

  • Pension addresses the risk of living too long (life insurance covers premature death). Needs arise from falling earning power, nuclear families, migration, rising costs and longevity.
  • Two stages: accumulation (regular contributions till retirement) and vesting/annuitisation (regular income for life after retirement).
  • Five core functions of any pension system: reliable collection of contributions; timely and correct benefit payment; secure and productive investment of assets; communication and record-keeping; financial reporting with governance and accountability.

2/6★ Must-learnEmployees' Provident Fund (EPF)

In plain words

EPF is a compulsory retirement saving scheme run by the EPFO. Employee and employer each contribute 12% of basic pay plus dearness allowance; the employer's share is split 8.33% to the pension scheme (EPS) and 3.67% to the provident fund. It applies to establishments with 20 or more employees.

Also: EPS wage ceiling Rs 15,000, so the maximum EPS contribution is 8.33% × 15,000 = Rs 1,249.5 (about Rs 1,250) a month. The UAN is a 12-digit number.

Worked example: basic pay plus DA of Rs 20,000. Employee 12% = Rs 2,400 → all to EPF. Employer 12% = Rs 2,400, split: EPS gets 8.33% but only on a ceiling of Rs 15,000 → 0.0833 × 15,000 = Rs 1,249.5 (about Rs 1,250); the remaining Rs 2,400 − 1,250 ≈ Rs 1,150 goes to EPF. Three EPFO schemes: EPF (savings lump sum), EPS 1995 (monthly pension, normally from age 58 after 10 years of contributory service) and EDLI 1976 (free life cover for members, paid for by the employer; benefit up to Rs 7 lakh, minimum Rs 2.5 lakh, as per the classroom notes). A large employer may run its own exempted PF trust, but only with EPFO's approval, and it may not pay lower contributions or interest than the EPFO.

  • Mandatory scheme for the organised private sector, run by EPFO (statutory body under the Ministry of Labour) under the EPF and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952; apex body is the tripartite Central Board of Trustees (Government, employers, employees). Applies to establishments with more than 20 employees (book).
  • Three EPFO schemes: EPF, EPS (Employees' Pension Scheme) and EDLI (Employees' Deposit Linked Insurance). EPFO is also the nodal agency for bilateral social security agreements.
  • UAN: unique 12-digit number, umbrella for multiple member IDs; launched October 2014.
  • Contribution: employee 12% of salary (basic + DA); employer 12% split 3.67% → EPF and 8.33% → EPS; employer also pays about 1% (0.5% EDLI + 0.5% admin charges) as per the book. EPS is computed on a wage ceiling of Rs 15,000 (book), i.e. Rs 1,800 a month for the minimum option.
  • Interest: declared yearly by the CBT and ratified by the Finance Ministry; 8.25% for 2025–26 (fixed 2 Mar 2026, ratified June 2026; book's 8.10% is the 2021-22 rate). Tax: EEE status, subject to the 5-year continuous service condition for tax-free withdrawal.

5/6★ Must-learnNational Pension System (NPS)

In plain words

NPS is a voluntary, market-linked pension scheme regulated by PFRDA. Your contributions are invested through professional pension fund managers and you receive a pension at retirement.

Structure: a Tier I account (compulsory, restricted withdrawal, extra tax deduction of Rs 50,000 under 80CCD(1B)) and an optional Tier II account (freely withdrawable, no special tax benefit). Each subscriber has a 12-digit PRAN. Contributions are invested through professional pension fund managers; the Central Recordkeeping Agencies keep the accounts.

  • Regulated by PFRDA (PFRDA Act, 2013). Market-linked defined-contribution product; no assured benefit. Mandatory for Central Government employees (except armed forces) joining on or after 1 Jan 2004; all States except West Bengal adopted it (book). Open to all citizens, resident or NRI, aged 18–70 (All-Citizen model); corporate model for employers.
  • Government employees: 10% own, Centre's contribution 14% since 1 Apr 2019. Tax: employer share under 80CCD(2) (14% Central Govt, 10% others); own contribution under 80CCD(1) within the Rs 1.5 lakh cap of 80C, plus an additional Rs 50,000 under 80CCD(1B).
  • Accounts: Tier I – compulsory, restricted withdrawal; Tier II – voluntary add-on, freely withdrawable, needs an active Tier I. Identity: PRAN (12 digits).
  • Architecture (unbundled): PFRDA appoints Pension Fund Managers (default: SBI Pension Funds), Custodian, Central Recordkeeping Agency (NSDL, per book), NPS Trust, Trustee Bank, Points of Presence (banks and others; the POP-SPs are branches) and Annuity Service Providers (IRDAI-licensed life insurers). Investment: Active choice (asset classes E, C, G, with E capped at 50% in the book) or Auto choice / Lifecycle (default).

6/6★ Must-learnAtal Pension Yojana (APY)

In plain words

APY gives workers in the unorganised sector a guaranteed monthly pension of Rs 1,000 to Rs 5,000 from age 60, with entry from 18 to 40 years.

  • Government scheme for unorganised-sector workers, run through PFRDA/NPS architecture. Entry age 18–40, savings bank/post-office account, Aadhaar, auto-debit contributions (monthly, quarterly or half-yearly) – amount depends on entry age and the chosen pension.
  • Guaranteed pension Rs 1,000 / 2,000 / 3,000 / 4,000 / 5,000 a month from age 60; the Government makes up any shortfall in returns, and excess returns pass to the subscriber. After the subscriber, the spouse gets the same pension; after both, the nominee gets the corpus accumulated at 60 (e.g. Rs 1.7 lakh for the Rs 1,000 slab up to Rs 8.5 lakh for Rs 5,000).

Module D · Unit 44Para-Banking and Financial Services Provided by Banks3 topics · 2 ★ must-learn · 1 ☆ good to know

1/3★ Must-learnMeaning, organisation and prudential limits

In plain words

Beyond lending and deposits, banks earn fees from other services, called para-banking: mutual fund distribution, insurance, lockers, advisory. The RBI limits how much a bank can invest in such businesses so that its core is not put at risk.

Safe deposit lockers (RBI norms effective 1 Jan 2022): bank is liable for fire, theft, burglary, robbery or dacoity, building collapse and fraud by its own employees, capped at 100 times the annual locker rent. Not liable for acts of God or the customer's sole negligence, and not required to insure the contents.

Caution on the locker relationship: the classroom notes call the bank–locker customer relationship "lessor–lessee" and not "bailor–bailee". Legal commentary notes that some banks used the lessor–lessee view to deny liability, but the courts (Amitabh Dasgupta) hold that the bank owes a duty of care either way. If an MCQ asks, follow the IIBF book's wording. Under the revised RBI instructions, liability applies where loss arises from the bank's own negligence or failure, and banks were to renew existing locker agreements with the new liability clause by 1 January 2023. Other para-banking lines: bancassurance, mutual fund distribution, ASBA, portfolio management for wealthy clients, and government business such as tax collection, pension payment and Sovereign Gold Bond issue.

  • Para-banking / financial services = activities beyond core deposit-taking and lending. Earlier governed by RBI's Master Circular on Para-Banking Activities (1 Jul 2015) and the Master Direction on Financial Services provided by Banks (26 May 2016), which added investment advisory, agency business and commodity-derivatives broking. RBI's 2025 Directions on financial services by commercial banks (effective 5 Dec 2025) repealed the 2016-era instructions and now govern the area.
  • How organised: departmentally or through a subsidiary (prior RBI approval); activities must be those permitted under s.6(1) of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, via s.19(1); equity holdings are bound by s.19(2). Departmental activity must follow KYC, Charter of Customer Rights and SEBI/IRDAI/PFRDA rules.

Module D · Unit 45REITs and InvITs3 topics · 2 ★ must-learn · 1 ☆ good to know

1/3★ Must-learnREITs – concept, structure, types

In plain words

A REIT lets many small investors own a share of income-producing real estate (offices, malls) and receive rental income, much like a mutual fund for property. You buy units on an exchange instead of buying a building, you get a share of the rent, and you can sell the units when you want. The trust owns and runs the properties through professional managers. Rules to remember: at least 90% of net distributable cash flows must be paid out to unit holders, at least 80% of assets must be in completed, income-producing property, and borrowing is capped at 49% of asset value.

Example: a large IT park may be worth thousands of crores and earn hundreds of crores in rent. No small investor can buy it, but if the trust divides it into units listed on the exchange, you can buy a few thousand rupees' worth and receive your share of the rent regularly. Who is involved: in a REIT the sponsor sets up the trust, the trustee holds the assets for unit holders, and the manager runs the investments. InvITs have the same plus a project manager who runs the physical asset day to day.

  • A Real Estate Investment Trust pools investor money (like a mutual fund) to own income-generating real estate and pays out most of the income; units are listed on stock exchanges. Underlying asset is real estate (or loans secured on it), unlike MFs (equity, debt, gold). Governed by SEBI (REIT) Regulations, 2014.
  • History: US REITs began in the 1960s; SEBI introduced the REIT framework in India in 2014 (book: 2007 idea); first Indian REIT Embassy Office Parks REIT, IPO March 2019 (book says registered 2017 and IPO opened March 2018).
  • Parties: Sponsor (sets up the trust) → Trustee (holds assets for unit holders, SEBI-registered) → Manager (investment decisions). Assets held directly or through SPVs; the REIT must hold at least 50% equity (controlling interest) in the SPV.
  • Types by funding: Equity REIT (owns and leases property), Mortgage REIT (earns net interest margin on property loans), Hybrid. By sector: retail, residential, healthcare, office (India's listed REITs mostly office), mortgage.

2/3★ Must-learnInvITs

In plain words

An InvIT does the same for infrastructure assets such as roads and power lines. At least 80% of the assets must be completed and revenue-generating; the rest can be under construction.

  • An Infrastructure Investment Trust pools money from many investors to own infrastructure assets that give predictable cash flows; set up under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882, regulated by SEBI (InvIT) Regulations, 2014 (amended 2016 onwards). Introduced in India in 2014.
  • Rationale: infrastructure companies get long-gestation capital without more debt, can repay debt and fund new projects, and get tax advantages of the trust form. Sectors: roads, ports, power, railways, airports, telecom, energy, oil & gas, coal, cement.
  • Parties: up to 3 sponsors (sponsors hold at least 15% for 3 years); Investment manager (returns and new investments); Project manager (operations, O&M, delivery of under-construction projects); SEBI-approved Trustee.
  • Types: by sector – energy; transport & logistics; communications; social and commercial infrastructure; water & sanitation. By funding – public-listed (units traded on exchanges) vs privately held (unlisted, few investors).
  • Rules: at least 80% of assets generating operational income; at least 90% of NDCF (net distributable cash flow) distributed, half-yearly. Book: minimum subscription cut in 2019 from Rs 10 lakh to Rs 1 lakh. Income depends on tariffs and usage (e.g. toll collections fell during the 2020 lockdown).
  • Examples: IRB InvIT; first public-sector InvIT sponsored by Power Grid (book).
