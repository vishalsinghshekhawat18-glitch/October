2/6★ Must-learnThe British period: three phases

Phase Period Character
Early East India Company (founded 1600) 1600-1757 buy raw materials, sell finished goods
Company rule (from Plassey) 1757-1857 plunder disguised as trade, heavy land revenue
Crown rule 1858-1947 de-industrialisation, commercialisation of agriculture, drain of wealth
  • Land revenue systems: Zamindari, Mahalwari and Ryotwari were designed to extract maximum tax from peasants. Farmers were pushed to grow crops for British industry, which created intermediaries, more famines and landless labour.
  • De-industrialisation began in the mid-19th century. Cheap British imports, Mughal decline and unfavourable trade policy destroyed Indian handicrafts and textiles.
  • Drain of wealth (Naoroji, R.C. Dutt, C.N. Vakil) is the one-way transfer of resources to Britain with no matching return. Home Charges were its official part, 1858-1947: dividends to Company shareholders, interest on loans raised in England, army costs, pensions of British officers, guaranteed interest to railway companies and the Secretary of State's establishment.
  • Railways were built to move raw materials to ports, not to industrialise India. Western education aimed to create clerks, not scientists.

3/6★ Must-learnCharacteristics of the economy today

  • World Bank class: lower-middle income. Bands are revised each 1 July on GNI per capita: FY26 $1,136–4,495; FY27 (from 1 Jul 2026) $1,176–4,635. The four classes are low, lower-middle, upper-middle and high income.
  • Size: 4th-largest economy by nominal GDP (passed Japan in 2025; roughly USD 4 trn, IMF estimate) and 3rd by PPP, after the USA and China.
  • Low per-capita income comes from poverty, unemployment, illiteracy, fast population growth and heavy dependence on the primary sector. India is large in total but still low in per-capita terms.

6/6Structural change

As incomes rise the primary sector's share falls and services rise. Services overtook agriculture in 1980-81 and now supply more than half of GVA. COVID briefly lifted agriculture's share because it was the one sector left open during the lockdown.

  • Four sub-sectors: mining and quarrying, manufacturing, electricity-gas-water, and construction. Industry's share rose from about 17% in 1950-51 to 29% in 2021-22.

Module A · Unit 3Economic Planning in India & NITI Aayog4 topics · 4 ★ must-learn

1/4★ Must-learnPlanning basics

  • Types of planning: regional or national; federal, state or local; centralised or decentralised; long or short term; sectoral (one sector) or spatial (geographic distribution of activity).
  • The USSR ran the first national plan (1928-33). The Indian National Congress set up the National Planning Committee in 1938.
  • Objectives: economic growth, poverty alleviation, employment, social justice and lower inequality, self-reliance (not autarky) and modernisation.
  • Planning Commission: set up in March 1950, a non-statutory body with the PM as Chairman and a Deputy Chairman in charge. Plans were approved by the National Development Council (NDC), also chaired by the PM.

3/4★ Must-learnNITI Aayog

  • National Institution for Transforming India, set up on 1 Jan 2015 by a Union Cabinet resolution (non-statutory). It is the government's policy think tank, chaired by the Prime Minister.
  • Structure: Vice-Chairperson, full-time members, CEO, ex-officio members and special invitees; the Governing Council includes Chief Ministers of states and UTs with legislatures and Lt Governors of other UTs.
  • Two hubs: the Team India Hub links states with the Centre; the Knowledge & Innovation Hub builds the think-tank role.
  • Approach: cooperative and competitive federalism, bottom-up planning, real-time monitoring and evaluation through the Development Monitoring & Evaluation Office.
  • Initiatives: Atal Innovation Mission (Atal Tinkering Labs, Atal Incubation Centres), SDG India Index and composite state indices.
  • Strategy for New India @75 (Dec 2018): 41 chapters in 4 sections — Drivers, Infrastructure, Inclusion, Governance. Goals included a $4 trn economy by 2022-23, tax-to-GDP of 22% and exports of $800 bn.
  • ☆ Why the Planning Commission was replaced: planning no longer fitted a market economy after 1991, and central allocation clashed with the states' growing role.

4/4★ Must-learnFinancing the plans

  1. Domestic budgetary sources — taxes (largest), public borrowing, small savings, PSU surplus. This is the biggest source.
  2. Deficit financing — borrowing from RBI, drawing down cash balances, ad-hoc Treasury bills. This is the second source.
  3. Foreign assistance — IBRD/IDA loans, bilateral aid, foreign investment.

Module A · Unit 5Infrastructure including Social Infrastructure3 topics · 3 ★ must-learn

1/3★ Must-learnConcepts

  • Hard infrastructure is physical networks such as roads, ports, airports and pipelines. Soft infrastructure is institutions that keep the economy running: financial, education, health and law-and-order bodies. Infrastructure is also split into economic and social.
  • Critical infrastructure is whatever the wider economy depends on: power, gas, oil, telecom, water, health, transport, financial services and security.
  • Green infrastructure is natural ecosystems providing life-support services: wildlife sanctuaries, tiger and elephant reserves, bird sanctuaries, green belts, the Western Ghats. A highway is not green infrastructure.
  • Public money is scarce, so PPP is the preferred model; the National Investment & Infrastructure Fund (NIIF) was created to improve infrastructure finance.

Module A · Unit 6Globalisation: Impact on India2 topics · 2 ★ must-learn

1/2★ Must-learnConcept and impact

  • Globalisation is rising economic integration among nations through cross-border flows of goods, services, capital, technology, labour and information. The WTO describes it as "unrestricted cross-border flows of goods and services, capital and labour".
  • The two World Wars and the Great Depression forced a retreat from globalisation; the OECD popularised the term again in the mid-1980s. India adopted it formally with the new economic policy of 1991-92.
  • Gains for India: higher per-capita income, more and better jobs, more choice and lower prices for consumers, technology transfer, foreign investment, and new agricultural export opportunities that pushed farm mechanisation and commercial farming.
  • Risks: greater exposure to external shocks and volatile capital flows.
  • Fair globalisation means benefits are shared and workers' rights are protected. It rests on three mutually reinforcing pillars: economic development, social development and environmental protection.
MCQ

"Not a benefit of globalisation?" → political stability. "Who popularised the term in the mid-1980s?" → OECD.

2/2★ Must-learnDeglobalisation and protectionism

  • Deglobalisation is a fall in integration and interdependence between economies. Protectionism shields home producers from foreign competition through tariffs, import quotas and non-tariff barriers. It reduces trade, raises prices and needs subsidies for protected industries; some jobs are saved but others are lost elsewhere.
  • What has driven the retreat: Brexit (UK left the EU on 31 Jan 2020), US withdrawal from the Trans-Pacific Partnership, the US–China trade war, COVID-19, the Russia–Ukraine war, a weak WTO dispute-settlement system, and rising populism. The book cites Michael O'Sullivan's The Levelling: What's Next After Globalisation.
MCQ

"Not a protectionist measure?" → increased capital-account convertibility.

Module A · Unit 7Economic Reforms4 topics · 4 ★ must-learn

1/4★ Must-learnWhy the 1991 crisis happened

  • Fiscal: the deficit rose from 5.1% of GDP in the early 1980s to 8.4% in 1990-91, driven by non-developmental spending. Borrowing to cover it pushed domestic debt past 50% of GDP and raised the interest burden.
  • External: the current account deficit widened to 3.69% of GDP. The Gulf War cut remittances, NRIs withdrew deposits and reserves fell to about US$1.2 bn, roughly two weeks of imports. India was close to defaulting.
  • Prices: inflation hit 11.3% (about 6.4% on average in the 1980s) because the 1980s were financed by deficit financing and supply was weak.
  • Root cause (Jagdish Bhagwati): (1) heavy bureaucratic control over production, investment and trade, (2) inward-looking trade and FDI policy, (3) inefficient public-sector enterprises. The 1990 oil shock turned a weak economy into a crisis.
MCQ

Remember

2/4★ Must-learnThe response: LPG

  • Reforms began in July 1991 (New Industrial Policy, 24 Jul 1991). The 1980s changes were mild and influenced by the Washington Consensus; the 1991 reforms were far wider, covering industry, trade, investment and later agriculture.
  • Liberalisation: licensing abolished, freedom to import, capacity expansion without approval.
  • Privatisation: more room for private players, a smaller role for the public sector, more FDI and competition.
  • Globalisation: integration with the world economy; India now depends more on global conditions and vice versa.
  • Aims: faster growth, lower inflation and CAD, fix the BoP problem.

Module A · Unit 8Foreign Trade Policy, Foreign Investment & Economic Development5 topics · 5 ★ must-learn

1/5★ Must-learnForeign trade policy (FTP)

  • Before 1991 India protected its market with high tariffs and quantitative restrictions. After 1991 it liberalised trade through tariff cuts and rupee convertibility on the current account, driven by competitiveness needs and the BoP crisis.
  • FTP 2015-20 (from 1 Apr 2015, tied to "Make in India") targeted exports of $900 bn by 2019-20, up from $465 bn in 2013-14, and a rise in global export share from 2% to 3.5%. It introduced MEIS (Merchandise Exports from India Scheme) and SEIS (Services Exports from India Scheme), cut the EPCG export obligation to 75% for domestic capital goods, relaunched Niryat Bandhu and moved to paperless trade. It was extended several times, to March 2023.
  • MEIS was replaced by RoDTEP (Jan 2021) and SEIS was discontinued.
  • FTP 2023 (effective 1 Apr 2023) is open-ended rather than five-year. It targets $2 trillion of exports by 2030, promotes rupee settlement and merchanting trade, adds four Towns of Export Excellence, and extends benefits to e-commerce exports.
  • The apex agency is the Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT) under the Commerce Ministry.

1/3★ Must-learnWhat economics studies: four definitions

Economics is about scarcity and choice. Wants are unlimited, resources are limited and have alternative uses, so every society must choose. The word comes from the Greek oikonomia (oikos = home, nemein = management), meaning household management.

Module B · Unit 13Supply and Demand5 topics · 5 ★ must-learn

1/5★ Must-learnDemand: the law, its two causes and the demand curve

  • The demand schedule (or demand curve) shows the relationship between a good's price and the quantity bought, other things held constant. Price and quantity move in opposite directions, so the curve slopes downward from left to right. This is the law of downward-sloping demand.
  • Quantity demanded falls when price rises for two reasons. The substitution effect: buyers switch to cheaper alternatives (dal gets dearer, so you eat more vegetables). The income effect: a higher price makes you feel poorer, so you buy less (petrol prices double, you cut consumption).
  • Market demand is the sum of all individual demands at each price. It obeys the same downward-sloping law.
  • Law of diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit consumed gives less satisfaction than the one before (the first glass of water to a thirsty person gives far more than the fourth). This is why people pay less for additional units.
MCQ

"Market demand curve obeys…?" → law of downward-sloping demand. "Demand schedule is the relationship between…?" → price and quantity bought.

2/5★ Must-learnForces behind demand, and shifts in demand

  • Determinants of demand besides price: average income, size of the market (population), prices and availability of related goods (substitutes), tastes and preferences, special influences (weather, seasons) and expectations about future prices.
  • A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the same curve (a change in quantity demanded). A change in any other factor shifts the whole curve (a change in demand). An income rise shifts the car demand curve to the right: more cars are bought at every price.
  • Demand for good A tends to be low when the price of its substitute B is low.
Remember

Own price changes → move along the curve. Anything else changes → curve shifts. The same rule applies to supply.

4/5★ Must-learnSupply: the curve, its determinants and shifts

  • The supply schedule shows the quantity producers will offer at each price, other things constant. The supply curve slopes upward: a higher price makes production more profitable, so more is supplied.
  • Determinants of supply: cost of production (input prices such as labour and energy), technology (lower inputs per unit), prices of related goods (production substitutes), government policy (taxes, regulation, minimum wages, trade policy), special factors (weather, market structure) and expectations about future prices.
  • Own-price change = movement along the supply curve. Change in any other factor = shift of the curve. Cost-saving technology shifts supply to the right; a bad harvest or costlier inputs shift it to the left.

1/4★ Must-learnWhat interest is

  • Interest is the payment a borrower makes for using money for a period. It is one of the four types of factor income: rent (land), wages (labour), interest (capital) and profit (entrepreneur).
  • It has three elements: payment for the risk of the loan, payment for the trouble of lending, and pure interest (payment for the use of money). Inflation is also factored in. Rates differ across loans because of differences in risk and trouble: a moneylender's unsecured loan costs more than a bank loan against collateral, and the government pays more on long-term than on short-term borrowing.
MCQ

"Pick the odd man out: rent, interest, wages, money" → money (the other three are factor incomes).

2/4★ Must-learnClassical theory: savings and investment

  • Also called the real theory, demand and supply of savings or capital theory. Developed by Marshall, Fisher and Pigou. The interest rate is set where the supply of savings equals the demand for investment, under perfect competition.
  • Demand for capital comes from investors and depends on the marginal productivity of capital. Investment continues while productivity exceeds the interest rate, so the demand curve slopes downward: higher interest, less demand for capital.
  • Supply of savings depends on thrift or time preference. Interest is the reward for abstinence or waiting, so the supply curve slopes upward.
  • Keynes' criticism: interest is a monetary phenomenon, not a reward for saving. Investment depends more on the marginal efficiency of capital and expectations than on the interest rate, and saving depends mainly on income, not on the interest rate.

3/4★ Must-learnKeynes' liquidity preference theory

  • In the General Theory, interest is purely monetary: it is the reward for parting with liquidity, determined by the demand for money (liquidity preference) and the supply of money, which the monetary authority fixes.
  • Keynes used a two-asset economy: money (earns no interest) and long-term bonds. Bond prices and interest rates move inversely.
  • Three motives for holding money:
MotivePurposeDepends on
Transactionsday-to-day purchases (income motive for households, business motive for firms)level of income
Precautionaryunforeseen needs: illness, accidents, unemployment, sudden opportunitieslevel of income
Speculativeto profit from expected changes in bond prices and interest ratesinterest rate (inverse)
  • Speculative demand: when interest is high, bond prices are low and expected to rise, so people hold bonds, not cash (money demand low). When interest is low, bond prices are high and expected to fall, so people hold cash (money demand high).
  • The money demand (LP) curve slopes downward. Money supply is a vertical line. Equilibrium is where they cross. If the rate is above equilibrium, excess money supply → people buy bonds → bond prices rise → rate falls. If below, excess demand → people sell bonds → bond prices fall → rate rises.
  • Increase in money supply (central bank buys securities in the open market) → rate of interest falls. Rise in income or in expected future interest rates shifts the LP curve up → rate of interest rises with money supply unchanged.

Remember

Classical = real (savings-investment). Keynes = monetary (money demand-supply). The speculative motive alone depends on the interest rate.

Module B · Unit 16Business Cycles2 topics · 2 ★ must-learn

1/2★ Must-learnMeaning and characteristics

  • A business cycle (also economic cycle) is an economy-wide fluctuation in production or activity over several months or years around a long-term growth trend. It is not regular or predictable like a pendulum; its timing is random.
  • Characteristics: it is synchronic (industries move up and down at about the same time); wave-like; recurring (boom is followed by depression and then boom again); no boom or depression lasts forever; effects are pervasive; the downturn is more sudden and violent than the upturn.

Module B · Unit 17Monetary Policy and Fiscal Policy8 topics · 7 ★ must-learn · 1 ☆ good to know

1/8★ Must-learnMonetary policy: meaning and framework

  • Monetary policy is the RBI's control over the supply of money, availability of money and cost of money (interest rate) to achieve growth and stability. Expansionary policy raises money supply and lowers interest rates (used in a recession); contractionary policy cuts money supply and raises rates (used against inflation).
  • The primary objective is price stability, keeping growth in mind. The flexible inflation targeting (FIT) framework got a statutory basis when the RBI Act, 1934 was amended in May 2016. The Government sets the inflation target once every five years, in consultation with the RBI.
  • Current target: 4% CPI inflation with a tolerance band of ±2% (2% to 6%) for 1 April 2026 to 31 March 2031, retained by the Government in March 2026. The earlier terms were 5 Aug 2016 to 31 Mar 2021 and 1 Apr 2021 to 31 Mar 2026, each with the same 4 ± 2.
  • Operating framework: the MPC sets the policy repo rate; day-to-day liquidity management aims to anchor the weighted average call rate (WACR), the operating target, around the repo rate. Repo changes pass through the money market to the financial system and then to aggregate demand, which drives inflation and growth.
MCQ

"Inflation target for RBI?" → 4% (+/− 2%). "Statutory basis for FIT came in…?" → May 2016. "Who sets the target?" → the Central Government, in consultation with RBI.

2/8★ Must-learnMonetary Policy Committee (MPC)

  • Constituted by the Central Government under Section 45ZB of the RBI Act. It has six members: three from the RBI (Governor as chairperson ex officio, the Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy, and one RBI officer nominated by the Central Board) and three external members appointed by the Government, who hold office for four years. The first MPC was constituted on 29 Sep 2016.
  • Present MPC (63rd meeting, 5–7 Oct 2026): Sanjay Malhotra (Governor), Dr Poonam Gupta (Deputy Governor), Shri Indranil Bhattacharyya (RBI Executive Director), and external members Dr Nagesh Kumar, Shri Saugata Bhattacharya and Prof. Ram Singh.
  • Process: meets at least four times a year; quorum is four; each member has one vote and the Governor has a casting vote on a tie; minutes are published on the 14th day (resolution, each member's vote and statement); the RBI publishes the Monetary Policy Report every six months (sources of inflation and a 6–18 month forecast). Members observe a blackout period from seven days before the decision day to seven days after the announcement. RBI announces policy bi-monthly.
  • Failure to meet the target: average inflation above the upper limit or below the lower limit for three consecutive quarters. Under Section 45ZN the RBI must report to the Government the reasons, the remedial actions and the time needed, within one month.

7/8★ Must-learnFiscal policy

  • Fiscal policy is the Government's use of taxation and expenditure to influence growth, employment, inflation and investment. Keynes was the first to link fiscal policy to economic performance. Its two basic instruments are taxation and government expenditure.
  • Taxes affect disposable income, consumption, savings and investment, and prices. Government expenditure has two kinds: purchases of goods and services (roads, salaries) and transfer payments (support to the poor, unemployed and elderly), which do not buy output.
  • Primary objectives in India: promote economic growth; maintain price stability. Additional objectives: mobilise resources, promote allocative efficiency, reduce inequality in income and wealth, promote private investment.
  • To fight inflation the Government uses contractionary fiscal policy (cut spending or raise taxes), plus supply-side steps.
Remember

Monetary policy = RBI (money and interest). Fiscal policy = Government (tax and spend).

8/8★ Must-learnFRBM Act

  • Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2003: enacted in 2003, in force from 5 July 2004. Aim: long-term macro stability, prudential debt management, transparent fiscal management and a medium-term framework.
  • Original requirements: table three statements with the Budget (Medium-Term Fiscal Policy, Fiscal Policy Strategy, Macroeconomic Framework); reduce the fiscal deficit (Rules set 3% of GDP) and eliminate the revenue deficit by 31 Mar 2008; ceiling on guarantees (Rules: 0.5% of GDP); no borrowing from the RBI and the RBI barred from subscribing to primary issues of central government securities (temporary Ways and Means Advances allowed); quarterly review in Parliament.
  • Deviations: the 2008 crisis breached targets. The 2012 amendment (13th Finance Commission) added the Medium-Term Expenditure Framework Statement and the concept of effective revenue deficit (revenue deficit minus grants for capital assets). The 2015 amendment pushed deadlines to March 2018. From the 2018-19 Budget the revenue deficit and effective revenue deficit stopped being FRBM targets.
  • N.K. Singh Committee (report January 2017): make debt the primary target, 60% of GDP (40% Centre, 20% states) by 2023; create an autonomous Fiscal Council; allow deviation of up to 0.5% of GDP in a year only for specified reasons (war, national security, calamity, collapse of agriculture, structural reforms, or a fall in real output growth of 3 percentage points below the four-quarter average); restrict RBI borrowing.
  • Escape clause: invoked in the COVID year, when the fiscal deficit target was raised from 3.3% to 3.8% for 2019-20 (RE) and 2020-21 (BE).
  • Current anchor: the Government now uses debt-to-GDP as the primary fiscal anchor, aiming for 50% ± 1% by 2030-31. Budget 2026-27 projects central debt at 55.6% of GDP (FY27 BE), down from 56.1% (FY26 RE).

Module B · Unit 18System of National Accounts and GDP Concepts5 topics · 4 ★ must-learn · 1 ☆ good to know

1/5★ Must-learnSystem of National Accounts (SNA) and India's GDP series

  • The SNA is the internationally agreed framework (developed by the UN with the IMF, OECD, World Bank and Eurostat) for compiling national accounts so that data are consistent and comparable. The latest edition used is SNA 2008, an update of SNA 1993. The SNA advises revising the base year periodically.
  • India: the base year moved from 2004-05 to 2011-12 in the series released on 30 Jan 2015, aligned to SNA 2008. The new series moved the headline growth measure to GDP at constant market prices (earlier factor cost), and sector value added to GVA at basic prices. It also used company accounts from the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA21) and wider financial-sector coverage (SEBI, PFRDA, IRDAI data).
  • Now: MoSPI released a newer GDP series with base year 2022-23 on 27 Feb 2026 (see Unit 1). The base year follows the year of the NSO's employment-unemployment survey, hence it was revised about every five years.
  • The National Statistical Office (NSO) was formed on 23 May 2019 by merging the CSO and NSSO, under MoSPI.
MCQ

"India's GDP base year revised to 2011-12 in…?" → 2015 (SNA 2008). "Headline growth measured by…?" → GDP at constant market prices.

5/5☆ Good to knowUses and limits of GDP

  • GDP is an aggregate: it says nothing about distribution of income. A high GDP can coexist with skewed income and regional disparity.
  • Higher GDP is not higher welfare. Health, education and sanitation need to be read alongside it, for example through the Human Development Index. GDP also ignores financial exclusion and environmental damage; green GDP tries to adjust for this.
  • For an export-oriented economy that earns a lot from abroad, GNP is the more accurate income measure.
  • Useful ratios: GFCF/GDP = share of investment; saving/GDP = saving rate; saving/GFCF = how much of investment is funded by domestic saving; compensation of employees/GVA = labour share; operating surplus/GVA = capital share.

1/5★ Must-learnStructure of the financial system

  • The financial system moves savings from surplus units (households) to deficit units (firms, government) through institutions, markets, instruments and regulators.
  • Informal sector: moneylenders, chit funds, pawnbrokers, indigenous bankers — unregulated by RBI/SEBI. Formal sector: regulated by RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA, Ministry of Finance.
  • Four market segments: money market (short-term, up to 1 year), forex market, capital market (long-term; equity and debt), and insurance market.

3/5★ Must-learn1991 crisis and the Narasimham Committees

  • 1991 BoP crisis: forex reserves covered only a few weeks of imports; rupee devalued in two steps (about 9% and 11%); 67 tonnes of gold pledged abroad to raise funds; IMF support followed. This triggered LPG reforms.
  • Narasimham Committee I (1991) → financial-sector reform: cut SLR (38.5% → 25%) and CRR, CAR of 8% introduced (1992, Basel norms), income recognition, asset classification and provisioning (IRAC), interest-rate deregulation, freer entry of private banks, Debt Recovery Tribunals (DRT Act 1993).
  • Narasimham Committee II (1998) → stronger banks: mergers of strong banks, CAR raised to 9%, concept of narrow banking for weak banks, further NPA cleanup, Asset Reconstruction concept, RBI to separate itself from ownership of banks.
MCQ

"CAR of 8% was introduced on recommendation of?" → Narasimham I. "CAR 9%" → Narasimham II.

4/5★ Must-learnCapital-market reforms

5/5★ Must-learnPost-GFC and recent reforms

  • IBC 2016; NaBFID (2021); NARCL (2021); BR (Amendment) Act 2020 – cooperative banks brought under RBI's banking-regulation powers.
  • Deposit insurance cover Rs 1 lakh → Rs 5 lakh (4 Feb 2020). UPI; NEFT and RTGS made 24×7 (Dec 2019 / Dec 2020). FDI in insurance raised repeatedly — now 100% (see Unit 26). LIC IPO 2022.

Module C · Unit 21Indian Banking System8 topics · 8 ★ must-learn

1/8★ Must-learnDefinitions and legal basis

  • BR Act s.5(1)(b): "banking" = accepting, for the purpose of lending or investment, deposits of money from the public, repayable on demand or otherwise, and withdrawable by cheque, draft, order or otherwise.
  • s.5(c): banking company = a company that transacts the business of banking. s.6: forms of business banks may do. s.7: only a banking company may use the words bank, banker, banking in its name.
  • Banks are scheduled if included in the Second Schedule of the RBI Act (paid-up capital and reserves of at least Rs 5 lakh and affairs not detrimental to depositors); others are non-scheduled. Scheduled banks get RBI refinance/LAF access.

4/8★ Must-learnCooperative banks

  • Three-tier short-term rural structure: PACS (village) → DCCB (district) → StCB (state apex). UCBs serve urban/semi-urban areas.
  • Counts (end-March 2025, RBI Trend and Progress): 1,457 UCBs (49 scheduled; 1,408 non-scheduled); 34 StCBs; 351 DCCBs.
  • Dual control: RBI (banking functions: licensing, capital, reserves, lending norms) and Registrar of Cooperative Societies (management, audit, elections, liquidation). BR Act applies via s.56; 2020 amendment extended RBI powers over management and capital of cooperative banks (not PACS).
  • Four-tier UCB framework (from 1 Dec 2022; definitions in RBI's 4 Dec 2025 guidelines): Tier 1 deposits up to Rs 100 cr; Tier 2 above Rs 100 cr to Rs 1,000 cr; Tier 3 above Rs 1,000 cr to Rs 10,000 cr; Tier 4 above Rs 10,000 cr. Second Schedule inclusion needs Tier 3 level deposits for 2 years and CRAR at least 3 percentage points above the minimum.
  • Banking Laws (Amendment) Act 2025: cooperative bank directors' tenure raised from 8 to 10 years (excluding chairperson and whole-time director).
  • Differences from commercial banks: cooperative principle (one member one vote), restricted area and membership, owned by members.
Remember

Dual control = RBI + RCS. Directors' tenure 8 → 10 years (2025).

5/8★ Must-learnBanking Laws (Amendment) Act 2025

  • Amends five statutes (RBI Act 1934, BR Act 1949, SBI Act 1955, Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Acts 1970/1980) with 19 amendments.
  • In force from 1 Aug 2025: "substantial interest" threshold raised from Rs 5 lakh to Rs 2 crore; cooperative bank director tenure 10 years; PSB statutory auditors' remuneration freedom; unclaimed shares/interest/bond redemption → IEPF.
  • From 1 Nov 2025: up to four nominees — simultaneous or successive for deposits; only successive for safe custody and lockers (Banking Companies (Nomination) Rules 2025).
MCQ

"Maximum nominees permitted in a bank deposit now?" → four.

6/8★ Must-learnFintech, neobanks and digital lending

  • Fintech = technology-led financial services firms; TechFin = tech giants moving into finance; neobanks = digital-only, partner with licensed banks (no licence themselves). BSL (bank-sourced lending) vs MPL (marketplace lending, platform connects borrowers and lenders).
  • Digital Lending Directions 2025 (8 May 2025; apply to banks, NBFCs, AIFIs): LSP register; Key Fact Statement with APR; cooling-off period: 1 day for loans up to 7 days, 3 days for longer loans; disbursal directly into borrower's bank account; consent-based data collection; data stored in India; grievance officer; digital lending apps reported to RBI's CIMS portal (from 15 Jun 2025).
  • RBI regulatory sandbox allows live testing of innovations.

7/8★ Must-learnPayment and settlement systems

  • PSS Act 2007 (effective 12 Aug 2008) – RBI designated regulator; s.4: only RBI-authorised entities can operate a payment system.
  • NEFT: started Nov 2005; deferred net settlement; 24×7 since 16 Dec 2019; no minimum or maximum. RTGS: began Mar 2004; real-time gross settlement; minimum Rs 2 lakh; 24×7 since 14 Dec 2020; ISO 20022 messaging.
  • CCIL (Apr 2001) – clearing and settlement for G-secs, forex and money-market via novation. Earlier electronic modes: ECS/NECS/RECS/EFT.
  • PPIs – prepaid instruments (closed, semi-closed, open, small, full-KYC). Mobile banking, ATM rules: free ATM limit 5 own-bank; 3 metro / 5 non-metro off-us (book values). ATM interchange from 1 May 2025: Rs 19 financial, Rs 7 non-financial; customers can be charged up to Rs 23 per transaction.

Module C · Unit 24Microfinance Institutions2 topics · 2 ★ must-learn

1/2★ Must-learnMicrofinance models

  • Microfinance = small loans, savings and services to low-income households. Grameen Bank model (Muhammad Yunus, 1983 as a formal bank) – group lending without collateral. MYRADA pioneered SHGs in India.
  • SHG-Bank Linkage Programme (NABARD, 1992): SHG of 10–20 members (minimum 5 in hilly/tribal/difficult areas). Models: I – bank lends directly to SHG; II – NGO/facilitator-formed SHGs, bank lends to SHG; III – NGO acts as financial intermediary (bank → NGO → SHG). Bank loan to SHG commonly 1:1 to 1:4 of savings.
  • JLG (Joint Liability Group): 4–10 members, usually of the same occupation.

Module C · Unit 25Non-Banking Financial Companies3 topics · 3 ★ must-learn

1/3★ Must-learnDefinition, registration and regulation

  • An NBFC is a company registered under the Companies Act 1956/2013 engaged in loans, hire purchase, leasing, securities, insurance business or chit funds — but not agriculture, industry, trading in goods, services or property. It cannot accept demand deposits, is not part of the payment/settlement system, cannot issue cheques on itself, and deposit insurance is not available to NBFC depositors.
  • Registered under s.45-IA of the RBI Act with a certificate of registration. Evolution: Shah Committee 1992, RBI Act amendments 1997.
  • Other regulators: HFCs → RBI (since 2019), earlier NHB; ARCs → RBI (SARFAESI); MFIs/CICs → RBI; Venture capital and mutual funds → SEBI; insurance → IRDAI; Nidhi companies → MCA.
  • Public deposits (NBFC-D): tenure 12–60 months; ceiling on interest 12.5% (book); deposits up to 1.5× NOF (as per prior rules) — now governed by the Acceptance of Public Deposits Directions 2025.
MCQ

"Which cannot an NBFC do?" → accept demand deposits / issue cheques drawn on itself.

Module D · Unit 29Financial Markets1 topic · 1 ★ must-learn

1/1★ Must-learnSegments and functions of financial markets

  • A financial market is where financial assets are bought and sold; prices are fixed by demand and supply — the process is called price discovery. Till the early 1990s Indian markets had administered prices, entry barriers, low liquidity and high costs; the 1991 reforms brought free pricing, new instruments, wider participation and better trading, clearing and settlement.
  • Segments (book list): credit, money, debt, forex, derivatives, capital, insurance and mutual-fund markets.

3/3★ Must-learnRepo, tri-party repo, BRDS, LTRO and TLTRO

  • Repo = sale of a security with agreement to repurchase at a future date at a predetermined price (collateralised borrowing); the same deal is a reverse repo for the other party. Eligible collateral: Central/State G-secs, listed corporate bonds/debentures (not own or related-party paper), CPs, CDs, units of debt ETFs. Banks use repo to manage CRR/SLR positions.
  • Tri-party repo (RBI, August 2017): a tri-party agent handles collateral selection, settlement, custody and revaluation but does not share the credit risk. Agents: CCIL and NSE (approved). Agent needs RBI authorisation, paid-up equity Rs 25 cr, 5 years' experience. Purpose: depth in the corporate bond repo market.
  • BRDS (Bills Rediscounting Scheme): banks raise funds against discounted genuine trade bills by issuing a Derivative Usance Promissory Note (DUPN); tenor 15–90 days; house bills and finance-company bills are ineligible.
  • LTRO (Feb 2020): 1-year and 3-year term repos at the policy repo rate, up to Rs 1,00,000 cr; first two operations 17 and 24 Feb 2020 of Rs 25,000 cr each. TLTRO (27 Mar 2020, COVID): banks lend to specified sectors/instruments (investment-grade corporate bonds); TLTRO 2.0 (17 Apr 2020, Rs 50,000 cr) for NBFCs and MFIs; securities held in HTM.

Module D · Unit 32Fixed Income Markets: Debt and Bond Markets5 topics · 5 ★ must-learn

1/5★ Must-learnGovernment securities market

  • A deep G-sec market gives the risk-free benchmark for pricing money-market and corporate paper, and helps the Government manage its borrowing and maturity profile. G-secs = Central dated securities, Treasury bills and State Development Loans (SDLs); issued to finance the fiscal deficit and cash mismatches.
  • Dated securities: issued at par (Rs 100), fixed half-yearly coupon set at auction, redeemed at par; named by coupon and maturity year, e.g. 7.99% GOI 2027. Issued for less than 1 year (T-bills) up to 40 years and above. A 10-year security issued in 2018 at 7.85% is "7.85 GOI 2028".
  • Who holds: banks are the largest holders (share falling: 47.25% in Mar 2010 → 40.41% in Mar 2020), with insurers rising (22.16% → 25.09%). Banks hold mostly up to 10-year paper; insurers dominate the very long end (about 90% of paper above 25 years).
  • Holding forms: SGL account with RBI, CSGL account with a bank/PD, or demat. Anyone resident in India may invest; NRIs, OCIs and FPIs as per FEMA/RBI rules; minimum Rs 10,000 face value in multiples of Rs 10,000.
  • Secondary market: telephone market and NDS-OM (RBI's anonymous order-matching system for G-secs, T-bills, SDLs); settlement through CCIL which guarantees it; trading 9 am–5 pm on working days. "When Issued" trading allowed before actual issue; individuals, HUFs, NRIs and OCIs can take only long positions.
MCQ

"Largest holder of G-secs?" → commercial banks. "NDS-OM trades?" → G-secs, T-bills, SDLs. "Guarantor of NDS-OM settlement?" → CCIL.

2/5★ Must-learnAuction of G-secs

  • RBI conducts auctions on E-Kuber. Two methods: uniform price (every accepted bidder pays/receives the cut-off yield/price) and multiple price (each accepted bidder pays their own bid). Winner's curse – paying more than the clearing level – is associated with the multiple-price auction.
  • Price-based auctions (re-issues: coupon fixed, bidders quote price per Rs 100) vs yield-based (new issue: highest accepted yield becomes the coupon). Green-shoe option may be exercised above the notified amount.
  • Non-competitive bidding by retail investors through an aggregator; allotted at the weighted-average price of accepted competitive bids; capped at a notified percentage of the issue. The book states bids can range from Rs 10,000 to Rs 2 crore face value.
Remember

Yield-based auction: highest accepted yield = coupon for the whole issue. Uniform price → one price for all.

3/5★ Must-learnBond valuation and the four theorems

  • Bond price = present value of all future cash flows (coupons + face value) at the market yield. Coupon rate never changes; price moves inversely with yield. Coupon = market rate → par; market rate above coupon → discount; below → premium.
  • Worked example: 8% annual-coupon bond, 5 years left, market yield 9%. Price = 8 × annuity factor (3.8897) + 100 × discount factor (0.6499) = 31.1176 + 64.99 = Rs 96.1076 using table factors (exact discounting gives about 96.11); below par because yield = Rs 96.1076 (below par, as yield > coupon)gt; coupon.
  • Theorem 1: price and yield are inversely related. 2: a fall in yield raises price by more than an equal rise in yield lowers it (convexity). 3: longer maturity → greater price sensitivity. 4: for equal maturity, the lower-coupon bond is more sensitive.
MCQ

"Which is NOT a bond theorem?" → longer maturity = lower sensitivity (wrong; it is higher).

4/5★ Must-learnPrimary Dealers, FIMMDA and Retail Direct

  • Primary Dealers (system started by RBI in 1995): entities licensed to buy G-secs directly from RBI and resell; they bid as principals in auctions, underwrite, quote two-way prices for T-bills and dated securities and develop the secondary market. Must meet minimum bidding, turnover and underwriting norms. Bank PDs (a department of a bank) vs stand-alone PDs (NBFCs); the book counts 7 stand-alone and 14 bank PDs. Applicant must have been registered as an NBFC for at least one year. PDs may borrow in the ICD market only (no lending).
  • FIMMDA – the self-regulatory organisation of banks, FIs and PDs for the fixed-income, money and derivatives markets: interface with regulators, benchmark rates, standard documentation and best practices, arbitration between members.
  • RBI Retail Direct Scheme (launched 12 Nov 2021): retail investors open a free Retail Direct Gilt (RDG) account with RBI via an online portal; buy T-bills, dated G-secs, Sovereign Gold Bonds and SDLs in the primary market (non-competitive route) and on NDS-OM (odd-lot/RFQ). Needs a rupee savings account, PAN, an OVD for KYC, email and mobile. Joint accounts allowed; up to two nominees; no account fees; payment by net banking or UPI (including UPI block); grievances handled by the PDO Mumbai; corporate bonds are not covered.
MCQ

"PD system introduced in?" → 1995. "Not eligible under Retail Direct?" → corporate bonds. "Retail Direct launched?" → 12 Nov 2021.

5/5★ Must-learnCorporate bonds and inter-corporate deposits

  • The corporate bond market is shallow: issuers are mostly PSUs, banks (AT1/Tier-2 capital), AIFIs, HFCs and NBFCs; players are banks, PDs, mutual funds, insurers and PFs. Bonds are priced at a spread over the matching G-sec reflecting default risk. Ratings run AAA to D; BBB and above = investment grade. All bonds are issued and held in demat form since June 2002 (NSDL, CDSL). Types: fixed, floating, put/call, zero-coupon, hybrid, STRIPS (separate trading of registered interest and principal).
  • ICD: unsecured borrowing by one corporate from another with surplus funds; priced above the CD rate (higher risk); tenor 1 day to 1 year, most commonly 90 days. For PDs: borrowing only, limit 150% of NOF, minimum tenor 7 days (book).
Remember

Investment grade = BBB and above. Corporate bonds in demat since June 2002.

Module D · Unit 33Foreign Exchange Markets5 topics · 3 ★ must-learn · 2 ☆ good to know

1/5★ Must-learnProfile and evolution of the forex market

  • Foreign exchange (FEMA s.2(n)) = foreign currency, deposits/balances payable in foreign currency, drafts, TCs, LCs and bills expressed in rupees but payable in foreign currency, and those drawn abroad but payable in rupees.
  • Mostly an OTC, 24-hour, inter-bank market that took shape in 1971 when fixed rates gave way to floating ones. Instruments: spot, forward, futures, swaps, options. The book quotes global turnover of about USD 6.6 trillion a day (BIS 2019 survey; later surveys are higher).
  • India: market began with the RBI's 1978 permission to trade intraday (square position only at close). 1975–92: rupee set by RBI against a weighted basket of currencies. 1–3 Jul 1991: two-step devaluation. LERMS (dual exchange rate; Rangarajan High Level Committee on BoP) in Mar 1992; unified market-determined rate from 1 Mar 1993; current-account convertibility Aug 1994 (IMF Article VIII); Sodhani Committee (Expert Group on Foreign Exchange, Nov 1994) widened the market.
MCQ

"LERMS introduced in?" → March 1992. "Current account convertibility accepted?" → August 1994 (Article VIII).

Remember

Dual rate 1992 → unified rate 1 Mar 1993 → Article VIII Aug 1994. Capital-account convertibility: Tarapore Committee (1997).

2/5☆ Good to knowCharacteristics, participants, LIBOR and ARRs

  • Features: very high liquidity, round-the-clock trading, no explicit service charge (the cost is the bid-ask spread), leverage by agreement (around 1:100 in retail trading).
  • Participants: commercial and investment banks, brokers, central banks (intervene using reserves), sovereign wealth funds, hedge funds, multinationals/corporates, institutional investors, individuals.
  • LIBOR (published by ICE Benchmark Administration) was discredited by the rate-rigging scandal and phased out; replaced by alternative reference rates (ARRs): USA SOFR (secured), UK SONIA, euro area €STR, Switzerland SARON, Japan TONAR. In India LIBOR linkages existed in ECBs, FCNR(B) deposits and derivatives (MIFOR); contracts had to be re-mapped to ARRs.

3/5★ Must-learnFEDAI, FIMMDA and FBIL

  • FEDAI (est. 1958): association of banks authorised to deal in forex; self-regulatory organisation. Functions: rules and guidelines for forex business, training, accreditation of forex brokers, advice to members, representation before RBI/Government, and announcing daily/periodic rates.
  • In March 2018 FEDAI, FIMMDA, IBA and others joined FBIL (Financial Benchmarks India Ltd), which administers rupee benchmark rates including the reference rate for USD/INR.

5/5☆ Good to knowFX-Retail platform, US Dollar Index, depository receipts

  • FX-Retail platform (announced RBI policy Oct 2017/Jun 2019; rolled out by CCIL on 5 Aug 2019): web-based anonymous order-matching platform for bank customers (individuals, proprietors, firms, SMEs, corporates); USD/INR in cash, tom, spot and forward (up to 13 months); no transaction charge for total daily volume below USD 50,000; customer registers on the CCIL platform and the bank verifies.

DR holders get dividends/voting rights per the DR terms and bear currency risk. Benefits: issuers raise capital abroad and widen their shareholder base; investors diversify with familiar settlement.

Module D · Unit 34Interconnectedness of Markets and Market Dynamics5 topics · 5 ★ must-learn

1/5★ Must-learnIntegration of financial markets

  • Integration = unifying markets so that risk-adjusted returns on assets of similar maturity converge across segments; it needs unimpeded access for participants. It has grown through deregulation, globalisation and IT. Shocks therefore spread — the "coupling" of economies and contagion (South-East Asian crisis 1997; Global Financial Crisis 2008-09).
  • Why markets are interconnected: mobile capital across borders; technology reducing arbitrage gaps; monetary policy moving from quantitative controls to price-based tools (policy rate); harmonised prudential regulation.
  • Importance: transmits price signals and monetary policy; promotes savings, investment and growth; supports an international financial centre; improves competition, efficiency, stability and innovation; imposes market discipline; promotes modern payment technology. Without integration there would be arbitrage and imperfect markets.
  • Heterogeneity (the reason integration matters): national vs international segments, depth/liquidity (money market more liquid than bonds), economic nature of instruments, risk profile (G-secs vs corporate bonds), and different participants.
MCQ

"Not a reason for interconnectedness?" → profitability parameters. "Spread of a crisis from one market to another?" → contagion.

2/5★ Must-learnLevels of integration and the Asian Clearing Union

  • Three levels: domestic (horizontal linkages across segments around a basic reference rate), global (open cross-border capital and financial-service flows, harmonised standards), regional (ties with a regional financial centre; gravity models; e.g. ACU). (There is no "vertical integration" level — a quiz distractor.)
  • Asian Clearing Union (ACU): multilateral net settlement of intra-regional payments among member central banks, set up on the initiative of UN-ESCAP in December 1974; saves foreign-exchange reserves and transfer costs. 9 members: Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Iran, Maldives, Myanmar, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka.
Remember

ACU: 9 members, Dec 1974, ESCAP. Levels: domestic, global, regional.

3/5★ Must-learnBenefits and costs of integration

  • Benefits: savings mobilisation, risk sharing and consumption smoothing, stronger transmission of policy, market-based governance, higher growth and lower volatility; local markets reduce reliance on foreign capital. Global openness can raise productivity and cut intermediation cost; direct investment is more stable than other flows.
  • Costs: contagion through the real channel (domino exposures) and the information channel (panic withdrawals); volatile, pro-cyclical short-term flows; sudden reversals; exchange-rate instability; monetary expansion, inflation and real appreciation from large inflows. This is why the Tarapore Committee capital-account recommendations were put on hold during the 1997 Asian crisis.

4/5★ Must-learnHow the markets move together

  • Money market: policy-rate change → WACR (weighted average call rate) moves almost at once → other money-market rates follow at spreads; WACR–policy-rate spread widens near financial year-end. 10-year G-sec yields track the repo rate.
  • Credit market: bank lending rates (WALR) still key because India is bank-dominated; pass-through is slowed by administered rates, deposit repricing rigidity, subventions, informal finance, statutory pre-emptions (CRR/SLR) and weak asset quality. Corporate bond yields move with G-sec yields.
  • Capital market: bond yields and equities move inversely (bond yield = opportunity cost of equity: a 7% yield + 5% equity risk premium → 12% hurdle). Indian and US indices (Nifty–S&P 500) move together.
  • Forex market: stock-market fall → FII outflows → rupee depreciation (USD-INR up); equity inflows → rupee strengthens. So the stock-market level and USD-INR move inversely.
MCQ

"Which pair moves inversely?" → bond yields and equity prices. "Policy rate changes first affect?" → call money rate (WACR).

5/5★ Must-learnIntegrated treasury and contagion examples

  • Integrated treasury merges the forex dealing room and domestic treasury so the bank manages funding and deployment across rupee and foreign-currency markets. Functions: reserve management and investment (CRR/SLR, duration analysis), liquidity and funds management, asset-liability management, market-risk management (credit appraisal remains with the Credit Department) and arbitrage (e.g. borrow USD → convert to rupees → hedge forward → invest domestically).
  • Contagion examples: 1997 South-East Asian crisis (Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea, Philippines; roots in excess dollar debt) spread to Latin America and Russia; the 2013 "Taper Tantrum" — US Fed signalling a cut in QE bond purchases → emerging-market currencies fell between May and Aug 2013.

2/4★ Must-learnLicensing and SEBI norms

Module D · Unit 36Derivatives Market4 topics · 4 ★ must-learn

1/4★ Must-learnMeaning, history and participants

  • Derivative (RBI definition): value changes with a specified underlying (interest rate, security price, commodity price, exchange rate, index, credit rating); needs little or no initial net investment; settled at a future date. SCRA s.2(ac) also defines it as a security derived from a debt instrument/share/loan/risk instrument/contract for differences, or a contract deriving value from prices or index of prices of underlying securities.
  • India: Bombay Cotton Trade Association started commodity futures in 1875 (the first Indian derivative was in cotton); cash settlement and options banned in 1952; Securities Laws (Amendment) Ordinance 1995 lifted the prohibition on options; L.C. Gupta Committee recommended financial derivatives; trading began June 2000 (Nifty index futures on NSE, 12 Jun 2000); Sensex options 4 Jun 2001; options on individual stocks Jul 2001; single-stock futures Nov 2001.
  • Participants: hedgers (reduce risk; majority), speculators (leverage bets), arbitrageurs (exploit price gaps). There is no "guarantor" participant.
  • Functions: price discovery, risk transfer, hedging, lower transaction cost, access to otherwise unavailable markets, leverage (futures margin roughly 20–40% in the book; option buyer pays only premium).
  • Exchange-traded contracts are standardised, margined and guaranteed by the clearing house, transparent; OTC contracts are customised, bilateral, less regulated (oversight increased after the 2008 crisis). Currency futures are exchange-traded; swaps, forwards and exotics (caps, collars) are OTC.

3/4★ Must-learnOptions

  • An option gives the buyer the right, not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) at the strike price on or before expiry; the buyer pays a premium to the seller (writer), who has the obligation. American option: exercisable any time up to expiry; European: only on expiry. Open interest = contracts outstanding.
  • Call example: strike Rs 1,500, premium Rs 100 → breakeven Rs 1,600; at Rs 1,800 profit = (1,800 − 1,500) − 100 = Rs 200; below Rs 1,500 the buyer loses only the Rs 100 premium.
  • Put example: strike Rs 2,300, premium Rs 125; at Rs 1,800 profit = 2,300 − 1,800 − 125 = Rs 375; above 2,300 loss is the premium.
  • Buyer's loss is limited to premium; seller's gain is limited to premium.

4/4★ Must-learnSwaps and credit default swaps

  • Swap = exchange of cash flows between two parties on a notional amount. Currency swap: principal and interest in one currency exchanged for another, principal swapped at start and re-exchanged at maturity at the same rate. Interest rate swap (IRS): only interest streams exchanged (fixed ↔ floating), no principal exchange; used to cut cost or hedge. Example: bank funds a floating-rate loan with a fixed-rate FCNR(B) deposit → uses a floating-to-fixed IRS to remove the risk of falling SOFR.
  • Currency swap worked example (book): ABC (Indian parent) borrows Rs 75 lakh at 12%; XYZ (US parent) borrows USD 100,000 at 4%; swapped at Rs 75/USD. ABC's subsidiary would otherwise pay 6% on USD (USD 6,000) → pays USD 4,000, saving USD 2,000; XYZ's subsidiary would pay 15% on Rs 75 lakh (Rs 11,25,000) → pays 12% (Rs 9,00,000), saving Rs 2,25,000. A swap bank usually intermediates.
  • Credit default swap (CDS): the protection buyer pays a periodic premium to a protection seller, who pays if the reference entity defaults — it transfers credit risk (not cash flows). Minimum three parties: reference entity (borrower), protection buyer (bond holder), protection seller (often a bank/insurer). Counterparty risk remains (AIG, Lehman, Bear Stearns in 2008).
  • RBI: draft guidelines Feb 2021; final RBI (Credit Derivatives) Directions, 2022 issued 10 Feb 2022, effective 9 May 2022. Under the framework retail users may buy protection only to hedge an underlying exposure; non-retail users (insurers, pension funds, mutual funds, AIFs, FPIs, etc.) can also sell protection; market-makers (scheduled commercial banks except SFBs, payments banks, LABs and RRBs; larger NBFCs/SPDs; EXIM, NABARD, NHB, SIDBI) quote both sides. Reference obligations are rated INR corporate bonds, CPs, CDs, short NCDs; no related-party deals; OTC trades are reported to CCIL's trade repository.
  • ISDA Master Agreement: standard documentation for OTC derivatives (from the 1985 Swaps Code); the pre-printed master is customised only through the Schedule, with confirmations for each trade and credit support documents.

Module D · Unit 37Factoring, Forfaiting and TReDS3 topics · 3 ★ must-learn

1/3★ Must-learnFactoring

  • Factoring = financing against invoices on buyers, where the factor also does credit control, sales-ledger administration and collection. Governed in India by the Factoring Regulation Act, 2011 (s.2(j) defines "factoring business" as acquiring receivables by assignment or financing against security interest over receivables; it excludes ordinary bank credit against receivables). It is short-term, on open-account sales, and works by assignment of debts.
  • Types: recourse (factor recovers from the seller if the buyer defaults) and non-recourse (factor bears bad debt and gives credit protection; the book says non-recourse domestic factoring is not permitted in India). Also domestic vs international (cross-border). Full-service factoring = finance + ledger + collection + credit protection.
  • Domestic factoring (book): advance about 80% of invoice value (the book also cites 85%; margin up to 20%), balance on collection; maximum credit period 150 days including a 60-day grace period; factor fixes sub-limits per buyer; MIS reports (debtor ageing, statements) to seller.
  • Fees: finance charge on the prepaid amount outstanding (monthly) and service fee of 0.1%–0.3% of invoice value (collection, ledger, MIS).
  • International factoring: two-factor system (export factor + import factor: prepayment, sales-ledger, credit protection, collection); single-factor (import factor pays export factor only if the buyer fails to pay within 90 days); direct export factoring (only an export factor); direct import factoring (only an import factor). There is no "three-factor" system.
  • Bills discounting vs factoring: bills discounting is transaction-based, always with recourse, needs a strong balance sheet/collateral, collection by the exporter, no assignment; factoring is ledger-based, may be without recourse, factor collects, includes MIS and credit insurance, involves assignment of debt, and can be done with a weaker balance sheet if receivables are good.
  • Advantages: instant finance against invoices, low margin, higher turnover of receivables, cash-discount purchasing, ledger and follow-up handled by the factor.
MCQ

"Not a form of international factoring?" → three-factor. "Maximum credit period under factoring?" → 150 days including 60 days grace.

2/3★ Must-learnForfaiting

  • Forfaiting = discounting of export receivables (bills of exchange, promissory notes, LC drafts), usually medium-term (1–5 years), without recourse to the exporter, at a fixed discount, up to 100% of invoice value. Name from French à forfait – "surrendering of rights". Typical minimum size about USD 250,000. In India only EXIM Bank and AD Category-I banks (non-recourse basis) may handle it.
  • Mechanism: forfaiter commits → exporter ships → importer's bank avalises/co-accepts the bill → exporter sells the avalised bill to the forfaiter and receives cash less discount → forfaiter collects from the importer's bank at maturity. Via EXIM Bank, the forfaiting charges are shown in the invoice/EDF/shipping bill for Customs.
  • Fees: discount fee (interest, linked to an international benchmark such as LIBOR/SOFR-type rates) and commitment fee (0.5%–1.5% p.a. on the unutilised amount until discounting or validity ends).
  • Advantages: 100% finance without recourse and without using credit lines, removes interest-rate, currency, credit and political risk, saves administration, lets exporter offer credit terms.

3/3★ Must-learnTReDS — Trade Receivables Discounting System

  • RBI concept (guidelines 2014) for financing trade receivables of MSMEs from corporate and other buyers (including Government departments and PSUs) through multiple financiers on an electronic platform; a form of factoring, without recourse to the MSME; covers invoices and bills of exchange; handles both factoring and reverse factoring (buyer-initiated).

  • Operator eligibility: minimum paid-up equity Rs 25 crore (the platform assumes no credit risk); non-promoter shareholding capped at 10%; promoters "fit and proper" with 5-year record; robust technology, real-time dissemination, BCP/DR and surveillance.

2/3☆ Good to knowAdvantages, accounting effect and bank leasing rules

  • Advantages: assured income to lessor; lessee conserves funds for growth; tax: lessee deducts rentals, lessor claims depreciation; cheaper than many other finance sources.
  • Effect on ratios (book example): a firm buying an asset with Rs 100 of debt moves from debt-equity 1:1 to 2:1; a firm leasing it stays at 1:1 because the lease was treated as off-balance-sheet — preserving borrowing capacity. 3/3★ Must-learnHire purchase

  • In hire purchase the hirer uses the goods, pays the price in instalments (each instalment treated as hire charge) and becomes owner only on paying the last instalment; until then the hire-vendor (owner) holds title and may repossess on default; the hirer may return the goods. Typically a down payment of 20–25% (or an FDR in its place) and EMIs over about 36–48 months.
  • Legal nature: a contract of bailment (Indian Contract Act, 1872) plus an element of sale (Sale of Goods Act, 1930); not a plain sale. The Hire Purchase Act, 1972 was enacted to regulate it (book says finance organisations are controlled by it; a 1989 amendment Bill never came into force). Terms: hire-vendor/hiree = owner; hirer/hire-purchaser = user.
  • Duties of the owner: deliver in time, goods of merchantable quality and to description, have good title, not disturb the hirer. Duties of the hirer: accept delivery, take due care, pay instalments on time, redeliver in same condition if the agreement ends, no disposal before full payment.
  • History: hire purchase in India for almost a century; early companies Commercial Credit Corporation (Madras) and Motor & General Finance (1920s–30s); consumer durables and commercial vehicles.

Module D · Unit 40Credit Rating and Credit Scoring4 topics · 4 ★ must-learn

1/4★ Must-learnCredit rating and credit rating agencies

  • A credit rating is an agency's opinion of an issuer's capacity and willingness to meet interest and principal on a particular instrument, in time. It guides investors; it is not a recommendation to buy, sell or hold. Ratings are forward-looking, in symbols, and kept under continuous surveillance (upgrade/downgrade, "watch").
  • History: John Moody began rating US railroad bonds in 1909; big three globally: Moody's, S&P, Fitch. In India CRISIL (est. 1987, operations January 1988) started credit rating; the book's tie-ups: CRISIL–S&P, ICRA–Moody's, CARE–Fitch (older). Seven SEBI-registered CRAs: CRISIL, India Ratings & Research (formerly Fitch India), ICRA, CARE, Brickwork, Infomerics, Acuité — all RBI-accredited for risk-weighting bank claims.
  • Who pays: the issuer (issuer fees about 95% of CRA revenue) plus an annual surveillance fee; investors do not pay.
  • Benefits: to investors – risk assessment, low-cost information, continuous monitoring, choice; to issuers – easier and cheaper borrowing, credibility for lesser-known firms, financial discipline, better disclosure.
  • Factors rated: ability to service debt (cash flows, liquidity, collateral, promoters), market position, management quality, industry risks, regulatory environment, cost structure, accounting quality, leverage, forex exposure.

Module D · Unit 41Mutual Funds8 topics · 7 ★ must-learn · 1 ☆ good to know

1/8★ Must-learnConcept, structure and NAV

  • A mutual fund pools money from the public by issuing units and invests it in securities as per the objectives in the offer document. Investors are unit holders; profit or loss is shared in proportion to units held. Must be registered with SEBI before collecting funds.
  • Four-tier structure: set up as a trust by a sponsor (like a promoter) → trustees hold the property for unit holders and supervise the AMC → AMC (SEBI-approved) manages investments → custodian (SEBI-registered) holds the securities.

  • NAV per unit = (market value of scheme securities − expenses) ÷ number of units. NAV is published daily on AMFI/AMC websites by 9 p.m. (fund-of-funds: by 10 a.m. next business day). Rounding: 4 decimals for index, debt, liquid and money-market schemes; 2 decimals for equity-oriented and balanced schemes (more allowed).

7/8★ Must-learnReturns, expenses, loads and SIP/STP/SWP

  • Absolute return = (current value − invested) ÷ invested × 100; ignores time, so use for under 1 year. Example: Rs 7,00,000 vs Rs 5,00,000 → (700000−500000)/500000 = 40%.
  • Annualised return (CAGR) = (Current value ÷ Beginning value)1/n − 1, n = years; used beyond 1 year; XIRR for irregular flows.
  • Expense ratio = annual operating expenses (administration, management, advertising) as % of daily net assets; 1% means 1% of assets goes to costs every year; disclosed in the offer document; limits set by SEBI and fungible across expense heads. Update: SEBI's new Mutual Funds Regulations, effective 1 April 2026, introduce a base expense ratio for the AMC fee, with brokerage, STT, stamp duty and exchange charges disclosed separately, and cut brokerage caps to 6 bps (cash) and 2 bps (derivatives).
  • Load: entry/exit charge as % of NAV. NAV Rs 10, 1% load → buy at Rs 10.10, sell at Rs 9.90. Entry load is banned by SEBI (since 2009); exit load may apply and is credited to the scheme. No-load fund = no charge at entry/exit.

6/7★ Must-learnBancassurance

Module D · Unit 43Pension Products6 topics · 6 ★ must-learn

1/6★ Must-learnPension basics

  • Pension addresses the risk of living too long (life insurance covers premature death). Needs arise from falling earning power, nuclear families, migration, rising costs and longevity.
  • Two stages: accumulation (regular contributions till retirement) and vesting/annuitisation (regular income for life after retirement).
  • Five core functions of any pension system: reliable collection of contributions; timely and correct benefit payment; secure and productive investment of assets; communication and record-keeping; financial reporting with governance and accountability.

2/6★ Must-learnEmployees' Provident Fund (EPF)

  • Mandatory scheme for the organised private sector, run by EPFO (statutory body under the Ministry of Labour) under the EPF and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952; apex body is the tripartite Central Board of Trustees (Government, employers, employees). Applies to establishments with more than 20 employees (book).
  • Three EPFO schemes: EPF, EPS (Employees' Pension Scheme) and EDLI (Employees' Deposit Linked Insurance). EPFO is also the nodal agency for bilateral social security agreements.
  • UAN: unique 12-digit number, umbrella for multiple member IDs; launched October 2014.
  • Contribution: employee 12% of salary (basic + DA); employer 12% split 3.67% → EPF and 8.33% → EPS; employer also pays about 1% (0.5% EDLI + 0.5% admin charges) as per the book. EPS is computed on a wage ceiling of Rs 15,000 (book), i.e. Rs 1,800 a month for the minimum option.
  • Interest: declared yearly by the CBT and ratified by the Finance Ministry; 8.25% for 2025–26 (fixed 2 Mar 2026, ratified June 2026; book's 8.10% is the 2021-22 rate). Tax: EEE status, subject to the 5-year continuous service condition for tax-free withdrawal.
  • Exempted funds: an employer may run its own trust if benefits are at least equal and EPFO approves. Investment pattern: 25% central government securities, 15% state government/guaranteed PSU bonds, 30% PFI/PSU bonds; trustees may put up to 10% in private-sector bonds (since 1998).
Remember

12% + 12% (3.67 EPF + 8.33 EPS) · UAN 12 digits · EPFO est. 1952 · exempted fund 25/15/30.

3/6★ Must-learnPublic Provident Fund (PPF)

  • Government savings scheme (National Savings Institute; post offices and banks). Lock-in 15 years, extendable in blocks of 5 years. Deposit Rs 500 minimum, Rs 1,50,000 maximum per year, lump sum or up to 12 instalments; interest compounded annually; rate notified by the Government every quarter.
  • EEE: deduction under s.80C (old regime), interest and maturity tax-exempt. Only one account per person; open to resident individuals, not NRIs or HUFs. Loan against PPF available from the 3rd year (book: between 3rd and 5th year).

  • Government scheme for unorganised-sector workers, run through PFRDA/NPS architecture. Entry age 18–40, savings bank/post-office account, Aadhaar, auto-debit contributions (monthly, quarterly or half-yearly) – amount depends on entry age and the chosen pension.

  • A Real Estate Investment Trust pools investor money (like a mutual fund) to own income-generating real estate and pays out most of the income; units are listed on stock exchanges. 2/3★ Must-learnInvITs

  • An Infrastructure Investment Trust pools money from many investors to own infrastructure assets that give predictable cash flows; set up under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882, regulated by SEBI (InvIT) Regulations, 2014 (amended 2016 onwards). Introduced in India in 2014.
  • Rationale: infrastructure companies get long-gestation capital without more debt, can repay debt and fund new projects, and get tax advantages of the trust form. Sectors: roads, ports, power, railways, airports, telecom, energy, oil & gas, coal, cement.
  • Parties: up to 3 sponsors (sponsors hold at least 15% for 3 years); Investment manager (returns and new investments); Project manager (operations, O&M, delivery of under-construction projects); SEBI-approved Trustee.
  • Types: by sector – energy; transport & logistics; communications; social and commercial infrastructure; water & sanitation. By funding – public-listed (units traded on exchanges) vs privately held (unlisted, few investors).
  • Rules: at least 80% of assets generating operational income; at least 90% of NDCF (net distributable cash flow) distributed, half-yearly. Book: minimum subscription cut in 2019 from Rs 10 lakh to Rs 1 lakh. Income depends on tariffs and usage (e.g. toll collections fell during the 2020 lockdown).
